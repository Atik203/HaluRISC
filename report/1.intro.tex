\section{Introduction}

Large language models are increasingly embedded in real products, from chatbots to retrieval-augmented question answering and content review tools, where a confidently wrong answer has a real cost. The models occasionally generate text that is fluent but factually unsupported, an effect known as \emph{hallucination}. Detecting hallucination is therefore a first-class safety problem for anyone deploying these systems.

One dominant strategy asks a strong LLM to judge its own output, and SelfCheckGPT implements this by sampling several responses and measuring their consistency without external resources \cite{selfcheck}. Small specialized evaluators such as Luna \cite{luna} make the judge approach cheaper, yet the pattern remains slow because every response needs a full forward pass. Neural classifiers over frozen encoders \cite{ieeeTai} are more accurate, but they require the model weights, GPU inference, and their decisions resist interpretation.

This paper takes a different route. HaluRISC treats hallucination detection as a supervised risk-prediction task over engineered evidence-consistency features, which measure how well the answer agrees with the question and the reference context in vocabulary, named entities, logical entailment, numbers, hedging, and semantics. A tuned XGBoost classifier consumes these features and outputs a calibrated probability, and SHAP explains every verdict with the feature contributions that drove it. The system never inspects the LLM, runs in tens of milliseconds on a CPU, and costs essentially nothing per prediction.

The main contributions are a reproducible feature-extraction pipeline with 26 features in seven groups, including bidirectional NLI entailment and contradiction signals, and a rigorous comparison of heuristic, logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost baselines under 5-fold cross-validation with a three-seed protocol. The study also compares Platt and isotonic calibration fit strictly on the validation split, evaluates zero-shot transfer to the natural RAGTruth corpus, and measures explanation reliability with perturbation analysis and feature-removal correlation. On the HaluEval QA benchmark the model reaches F1 = 0.9846 and AUROC = 0.9979 with an expected calibration error of 0.0045. Transfer to natural RAGTruth QA is poor (F1 = 0.3022), and this negative result is reported openly rather than hidden. The full system is deployed as a FastAPI backend with a Next.js and assistant-ui dashboard.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews the literature on hallucination detection. Section~3 describes the feature pipeline and the model, Section~4 details the experimental setup, and Section~5 reports results on calibration, cross-domain transfer, and explanation reliability. Section~6 presents the deployed web system, Section~7 discusses limitations and ethical considerations, and Section~8 concludes.
