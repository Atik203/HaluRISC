\begin{abstract}
  Large language models answer questions fluently, and some answers are not supported by the evidence. Detectors usually rely on a large judge model, an encoder classifier, or access to model internals. These options are costly, hard to interpret, and rarely report how their risk scores behave under distribution shift. Feature-based detectors are cheaper, but their calibration, length sensitivity, and cross-domain behaviour are seldom measured together, and on HaluEval answer length is almost a label proxy. This study tests whether a simple black-box model over explicit evidence features can estimate hallucination risk and how its calibration and ranking change under task and corpus shift. The proposed model, evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB), combines 26 base features, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator. It is trained on HaluEval QA and RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text rows with grouped splits and three seeds, and evaluated with Platt and isotonic calibration, SHAP attributions, a length-only baseline, and length controls. The design needs no LLM judge calls and no access to model internals, but it still runs small NLI, embedding, and NER models. This keeps inference cheap and the features inspectable while allowing a direct test of the length shortcut. EC-XGB reaches an F1 of 0.9855 and an AUROC of 0.9984 on HaluEval QA, matching conventional XGBoost on a saturated benchmark. On the RAGTruth tasks used in training, the pooled flag rate falls from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, while held-out QA is still flagged at 97.4\% and its AUROC is 0.549. Target calibration lowers ECE from 0.726 to 0.126, but a prevalence-only predictor at the calibration prevalence matches its Brier score, the deployed 0.5 threshold recalls no QA rows, and a 5\% false-positive budget recalls 5.0\%. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific risk score and adaptation check, not a general-purpose detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A number of researchers have examined how to apply machine learning techniques to assess the likelihood of a response being a hallucination. One such technique is SelfCheckGPT, which uses several generated answers and assesses their similarity to determine if there has been a hallucination \cite{selfcheck}. Another technique is Luna, where a smaller evaluation model is used instead of a larger judge model \cite{luna}. A third technique is semantic entropy, which identifies meaning-based groupings of similar responses \cite{farquhar2024semantic}. A hybrid framework combines frozen encoder features with lightweight classifiers and reports strong benchmark scores \cite{ieeeTai}. Judges carry high computational costs and encoder models are hard to interpret. Two recent reviews have grouped approaches to detecting hallucinations into three categories including feature-based detectors, encoder-based detectors, and combinations of the above \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey}. Both reviews identify evaluation and reliability as two major areas that require additional research, and a third review determines that reliability is an area of concern as well \cite{alansari2026survey}. A fourth survey frames the problem as factuality evaluation and reaches the same conclusion \cite{wang2024factuality}.

Six recent studies address the same topic of assessing hallucinations. HaluAgent demonstrated that small language models can select appropriate tools and detect multiple forms of hallucinations, but it required multiple tool calls for each answer \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. proposed a calibrated multi-score detector with a detailed cost analysis, but the detector relies heavily on large language model (LLM) scoring for its primary source of information \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. proposed a system that uses retrieval, natural language inference (NLI) verification, and an XGBoost classifier, and they report only classification metrics \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME attributions to each word, but did not test whether those values remained consistent across words \cite{multimedia}. Deng et al. found that detectors degrade once they leave the domain in which they were trained, and their method focuses on multi-turn dialogue signals \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench shows that modern LLMs still produce hard summarization hallucinations, and it does not assess calibration risks \cite{faithbench}.

The existing literature leaves four issues unaddressed. Calibration is infrequently reported for lightweight detectors, explanation quality is rarely measured, cross-domain behavior is rarely tested, and error patterns are rarely analyzed case by case. The conventional feature-based baseline is also saturated on HaluEval, depends on answer length, and loses calibration under shift. This paper addresses all four issues by developing a black-box model that takes a query $q$, a reference context $c$, and a candidate answer $a$, and outputs $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$. The model, evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB), consists of 26 evidence-consistency features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator, classified by a tuned XGBoost model. Platt and isotonic calibration yield the probability, SHAP explanations reveal feature contributions, artifact-control baselines and group-aware splits reduce shortcut solutions, and validation-only calibration quantifies the reliability problem. The claim-level aggregates support per-claim explanations, and the measured transfer gain comes from multi-source training rather than from those aggregates. Transfer limits are established on RAGTruth and FaithBench.

HaluEval QA provides 10{,}000 paired correct and hallucinated answers for training and in-domain testing \cite{haluEval}. RAGTruth provides natural responses for adaptation and recalibration \cite{ragtruth}. The scope is question answering with a reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. EC-XGB reaches an F1 of 0.9855 on HaluEval QA. On natural RAGTruth answers, the flag rate falls from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, and the AUROC rises from 0.475 to 0.582. Target calibration on 5{,}034 labeled rows lowers the RAGTruth QA ECE from 0.726 to 0.126, but it does not change the ranking. On HaluEval, recall stays above 99\% at a five percent false-positive rate, and one analysis takes 61.8~ms at about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Fluent hallucinations are claims where the amount of support from the evidence is not enough. The detection can be assessed in terms of precision, recall, F1, or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in an evaluation setting on labeled data. Typical datasets for testing detectors are HaluEval \cite{haluEval} and TruthfulQA \cite{truthqa}, and factual accuracy of long-form text generation can be verified by FActScore \cite{factscore}. In a deployment context, a detector faces tougher evaluation, because it has to contribute to a decision process and to be explainable. Calibration addresses the first problem -- the predicted risk has to represent the true risk, and calibration is measured with the use of expected calibration error (ECE) via reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. The second one -- having the detector's warning, the person needs an explanation of it. For tabular models such information comes from SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) attributions \cite{shap}. Both kinds of evaluation are conducted in the current work on the black box approach to the model.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 hand-crafted features. XGBoost handles nonlinear interactions, trains quickly on tabular data, and supports exact SHAP values. Boosted trees remain competitive with tuned neural networks on tabular data \cite{holzmuller2024better}. The model adds trees one at a time, and each tree fits the error left by the previous ensemble. The final score passes through a logistic link, so the output is a probability.

Let $x_i$ be the feature vector of row $i$ and $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label. The model sums $K$ trees, $z_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)$. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $\phi = \{f_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ collects the trees, $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves of weight $w_k$, $\gamma$ is a leaf-count penalty, and $\lambda$ is an L2 weight penalty. The loss $l$ is the standard logistic loss and the probability comes from the sigmoid of the summed leaf score, $\hat{y}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$.

At boosting step $t$, a new tree with $T$ leaves is added, and the objective is approximated by a second-order expansion around the current prediction,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $g_i$ and $h_i$ are the first and second derivatives of the loss at the current prediction. For a fixed tree structure, the optimal weight of leaf $j$ is
\begin{equation}
  w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda},
\end{equation}
where $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$ and $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$ are the sums over the rows $I_j$ that reach leaf $j$. The gain of a split is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda} \right] - \gamma,
\end{equation}
with $G_L$ and $H_L$ for the left child and $G_R$ and $H_R$ for the right child. A split is kept only when the gain is positive. This is the criterion used to grow each tree.

Tree depth, learning rate, tree count, and the two subsample rates are tuned on the training split; the selected values are in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:baseline} lists the eight-step procedure. The search draws 30 configurations and scores each with grouped five-fold cross-validation. The grouping keeps both answers to the same question in one fold. Each seed trains on 14,000 rows, fits Platt scaling on 3,000 validation rows, selects the threshold on validation, and tests once on 3,000 rows. The test rows are never used for tuning or calibration. Step 8 returns the mean over the three seeds, which is why every aggregate table reports a mean.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and evaluation)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, calibrator, and decision threshold
    \State extract the 26 evidence-consistency features for every row
    \State tune XGBoost with a randomized search and grouped 5-fold cross-validation
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State train the classifier on the training split
    \State fit Platt calibration and select the decision threshold on the validation split
    \State evaluate on the fixed test split
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This baseline has three limits. The benchmark is saturated, so extra features do not improve F1. The score is sensitive to answer length, and the probability scale breaks under shift \cite{ovadia2019}. The baseline also lacks claim-level evidence, trains on one corpus, and reports a source-calibrated probability that is not calibrated under target shift. These limits motivate the three changes in Section 3.

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Working Principle}

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{revision/architecture-flow-large.png}
  \caption{End-to-end pipeline of EC-XGB.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} reads from left to right. It takes a question, reference context, and a candidate answer from a black-box LLM, and produces a risk probability and per-feature explanations. The first stage collects the three inputs: the question, the reference context, and the candidate answer. A missing context is allowed and is treated as a separate condition. The second stage extracts 35 features. The seven base groups cover length and style, lexical overlap, entity novelty, NLI, numeric overlap, hedging, and semantic similarity. Eight claim-level NLI aggregates are added to the 26 base features, and a binary source indicator marks whether the answer came from a natural response corpus. The third stage passes the 35 features to the EC-XGB classifier. The classifier returns a raw score, which is a probability over the hallucinated class. The fourth stage applies the Platt calibrator fitted on the RAGTruth QA calibration split. The calibrated score is the number shown to the user. The fifth stage produces the outputs: the risk score, the per-claim verdicts, and the SHAP feature contributions. The lower band shows the deployment path. A Next.js front end calls a FastAPI service, and the service loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. The rightmost block lists the evaluation corpora. HaluEval measures in-domain performance, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text measure adaptation to task types seen in training, and RAGTruth QA and FaithBench are held out. The training band records the monotone constraints, the grouped five-fold cross-validation, and the three seeds used for every reported mean.

The task consists of estimating the probability $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$ of a hallucination, conditioned on a question $q$, reference context $c$ (if available) and a candidate answer $a$ coming from a black-box LLM. It is not aimed at verifying truthfulness of the candidate answer, but at finding those cases in which the candidate answer does not match the provided evidence, i.e. it does not follow from or contradicts it. The output is a probability, not a binary label. A decision threshold turns the probability into a flag.

\subsection{Features}

The model uses 26 base features in seven groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and eight aggregate features computed at the claim level (Table~\ref{tab:claim}). For NLI, six signal probabilities come from a DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder \cite{deberta} trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}. They indicate whether one statement follows logically from another. Semantic similarity uses MiniLM sentence embeddings \cite{sbert,minilm}, and entities come from spaCy named entity recognition \cite{spacy}.
Length and style features count characters, words, sentences, and average word length. They are included because the benchmark labels longer answers as hallucinated more often, so length is a strong but fragile signal. Lexical overlap measures how much of the answer appears in the context and in the question. It captures direct copying and is the strongest feature in the SHAP ranking, but it cannot detect a fluent paraphrase that contradicts the evidence. Entity features count named entities and the share that do not appear in the context. A novel entity is a useful warning sign, although a correct answer can also introduce a new name. The NLI features give the probability that the context entails, contradicts, or is neutral to the answer, in both directions. They are the only group that represents logical support rather than surface overlap. Numeric features compare the numbers in the answer with the numbers in the context. A changed number is a common hallucination and is easy to detect. Hedging features count uncertainty words such as ``may'' or ``likely''. They are weak on their own, but they help separate a cautious correct answer from a confident wrong one. Semantic features use cosine similarity between the context and the answer and between the question and the answer. They catch paraphrase and topic drift that lexical overlap misses. The eight claim-level aggregates in Table~\ref{tab:claim} repeat the NLI and overlap signals at the level of atomic claims, which is the level a reviewer actually checks. Entailment-based claim metrics are also used by RAGChecker \cite{ru2024ragchecker}.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB alters three key aspects of the conventional XGBoost learner. These changes allow each component of the learning process to be assessed individually. First, raw-length counts are replaced with log-scaled values, and monotonicity constraints ensure that risk cannot decrease as contradiction or novelty rises and cannot increase as entailment, overlap, or semantic similarity rises. Raw-count length is replaced with its log-scaled value, $\ell' = \log(1+\ell)$. Second, the eight claim-level aggregates in Table~\ref{tab:claim} are added to the evidence signals that the deployed verification layer reports, so the classifier sees the same signals. Consider an answer composed of $K$ claims, with $C(k)$ the contradiction and $E(k)$ the entailment of claim $k$, and let $v_k$ be the verdict of claim $k$. The aggregate vector is
\begin{gather}
  \psi(x) = [\max_k C(k), \overline{C}, r_{\text{con}}, r_{\text{sup}}, r_{\text{unsup}}, \min_k E(k), \overline{E}, K], \\
  r_{\text{con}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{contradicted}], \quad
  r_{\text{sup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{supported}], \\
  r_{\text{unsup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{unsupported}].
\end{gather}
Here $\overline{C}$ and $\overline{E}$ are the means of $C(k)$ and $E(k)$ over the claims. Let $\phi'(x)$ be the complete model input and $\tilde{\phi}(x)$ the remaining 24 base features excluding the two log-scaled length columns. There are 35 input features in total (24 base + 2 log-scaled length + 8 claim + 1 source). The claim count $K$ is kept as a feature because an answer with many claims is harder to verify.
The third modification is the addition of a source indicator $s \in \{0,1\}$ as a training feature for RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text tasks. Since all RAGTruth QA samples are filtered out during the training phase, the QA task itself is still zero-shot. The deployed display score is fitted on the labeled target samples and is therefore not zero-shot. This combination of modifications is termed EC-XGB. The deployed checkpoint uses seed 42.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecxgb} lists the training and deployment procedure. Steps 1 and 2 build the feature matrix. The length columns are log-scaled, and the monotone constraints are attached to the features they govern. Steps 3 to 9 repeat the fit for the three seeds. Each repetition builds the 35-feature input, trains on HaluEval plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, fits the display calibrator on the 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows, selects the source threshold on the HaluEval validation split, and selects the target threshold on the calibration rows at a 5\% false-positive budget. The QA rows are excluded from training in every repetition, which keeps the QA test zero-shot. The output is the model, the display calibrator, and the two thresholds.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled HaluEval QA rows, RAGTruth non-QA rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}$
    \Ensure deployed model, display calibrator, source threshold, target threshold
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim-level aggregates
    \State log-scale the length features and set the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input with the source indicator
    \State train XGBoost on HaluEval plus RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of training
    \State fit the display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration split (P1)
    \State select the source-only threshold on the HaluEval validation split (P0)
    \State select the target threshold on the same calibration split at five percent FPR (P2)
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the deployed model, calibrator, and thresholds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The contribution is the multi-source training protocol and the evaluation, not a new learner. Each piece is standard on its own, and together they hold the in-domain score, cut the false-alarm rate on the task types seen in training, and produce feature-level attributions whose reliability is measured. The deployed system adds claim-level verification with evidence retrieval and a display calibrator fitted on the target domain. Relative to the closest feature-based detector \cite{ijert}, the claim-level features support per-claim explanations. The measured transfer gain comes from multi-source training and the calibration protocol. Relative to the LLM-scored detector \cite{costeff} and the tool-based detector \cite{haluagent}, the difference is that inference needs no LLM judge calls; the feature pipeline still runs three small pretrained models. Their scores come from other corpora and other protocols, so the comparison is about the protocol and not about the numbers. None of the three reports the calibration error or the shifted flag rate that this study measures.

\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

Source calibrators are fit on the validation split only. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid $p(x) = \sigma(A f(x) + B)$ to the raw score $f(x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are fit by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing step function $g$ so that $p(x) = g(f(x))$ \cite{isotonic}. Both are compared against the raw model on the test split. The comparison uses expected calibration error over $M=10$ bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the accuracy and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, together with the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$ and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. Post-hoc calibration is not robust when the target distribution moves \cite{gasparin2024distance}. A second step targets a new domain. The calibrator can be re-fit on a small labeled sample of the target corpus, and the effect is measured on a disjoint held-out part of the target corpus. The deployed EC-XGB display score follows the same rule: its calibrator is fitted on the 5{,}034-row RAGTruth QA calibration split, and the source indicator is set to one for natural responses. This target calibration needs labeled target rows and is not zero-shot. The source-only protocol uses the HaluEval validation split for both the calibrator and the threshold.

The global SHAP \cite{shap} explanations from a TreeExplainer applied to the raw XGBoost model were used to create the Mean Absolute Importance and Beeswarm Summaries. Local explanations highlighting the contributions of features involved in a particular prediction are given. Post-hoc attribution methods can be unstable even when the model is fixed \cite{kelodjou2024shap}. Since the feature-attribution techniques might be highly unreliable, the reliability of the explanations has been evaluated directly \cite{adebayo2018}. The test responses have been perturbed in six different ways (by replacing entity names, numbers, and dates, eliminating support context, inserting additional context, and shuffling clauses). This study quantifies the reliability by calculating four different measures (the mean absolute score change, the fraction of large score changes, the top-1 flip rate, and the Spearman rank correlation between the original and perturbed scores).

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

These methods are used in a two-tiered web application. The system is a Next.js frontend and a FastAPI backend that loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. Three checkpoints appear in Section~\ref{sec:results}. B2 is the conventional 26-feature model trained on HaluEval only. It supplies the conventional row of the model-comparison table, the feature-group ablation, the conventional rows of the target-calibration table, and the SHAP analysis and audit. B6 m0 is a separate 26-feature tuning run under the EC-XGB protocol, reported as m0 in the component ablation and as the conventional rows of the cross-domain table. EC-XGB m3 is the 35-feature multi-source model that supplies the remaining EC-XGB rows and the deployed system. Claims are split from the answer and checked with sentence-level NLI against the provided context, an indexed collection, or live web search. A support-first rule treats any supporting sentence as evidence, and a contradiction counts only when no support exists. A relevance gate removes retrieved sentences with no claim vocabulary, and the verdicts are shown per claim.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments were run on a Windows-based computer (laptop), with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU, CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128, and 32\,GB of RAM. The programming language used to run the code is Python. The Python version used in this experiment is 3.12. The code utilizes the following packages: scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. Each run can be reproduced because of saved split indices and centralized seeds. A randomized search was performed over 30 hyperparameter configurations with grouped five-fold cross-validation. For the seed-42 conventional model, the configuration has a maximum tree depth of 5, a learning rate of 0.05, 300 trees, a subsampling ratio of 0.7, and a column sampling ratio of 0.7. Each seed and each EC-XGB variant was tuned separately. Seeds 42, 123, and 456 are repeated where a seed applies; the single-seed tables are labeled.

\subsection{Dataset}

The QA subset of HaluEval \cite{haluEval} serves as the training data. This dataset has 10{,}000 questions. Each question has a knowledge passage that it is based upon, a correct answer, and a hallucinated version of that answer. Therefore, there are 20{,}000 rows of labeled data. The 20{,}000 rows are split 70/15/15 at the group level into 14{,}000 training, 3{,}000 validation, and 3{,}000 test rows. Since both versions of the answer to each question come from the same knowledge passage, the model can easily learn the relationship between the context and the answers, so the split is at the group level. About 85\% of HaluEval was generated by ChatGPT through sampling and filtering \cite{haluEval,bang-etal-2025-hallulens}. The boundary between a factual error and a hallucination is therefore not always clean.
Transfer was evaluated using two datasets. First, RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth}: a collection of responses produced through natural processes that have been annotated with word-level spans by humans. Second, FaithBench \cite{faithbench}: a hallucination-based summarization benchmark based on outputs from modern large language models. RAGTruth ships official splits. Summarization has 4{,}758 training and 900 test rows, and data-to-text has 5{,}298 training and 900 test rows. The QA task contributed 5{,}934 rows in 989 groups, of which 900 rows in 150 groups form its test set and 5{,}034 rows form the calibration block. The groups do not overlap. The three 900-row test splits are held out from training, so the pooled RAGTruth test set has 2{,}700 rows. The positive rate is 50.0\% on HaluEval, 34.9\% on the pooled RAGTruth test split, 17.8\% on the RAGTruth QA test rows, 31.1\% on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows, and 68.1\% on FaithBench. The summary of the corpora is provided in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.

\subsection{Baselines and Experimental Procedure}

Ten baselines and EC-XGB are tested. The heuristic sets risk to one minus lexical overlap. Logistic regression and a 300-tree random forest are the classical comparators; the conventional XGBoost model \cite{xgboost} uses the 26 base features. Three controls isolate the feature design: NLI-only, answer-only TF-IDF, and full-input TF-IDF. A majority classifier and a fine-tuned RoBERTa-base \cite{liu2019roberta} complete the set; the two judges are in Section~\ref{sec:results}.



Internal-state detectors are outside the black-box setting \cite{inside2024}. The RoBERTa baseline receives up to 256 tokens and trains for three epochs. EC-XGB's display calibrator is fitted on the RAGTruth QA split; the in-domain Platt calibrator is fitted on validation. Metrics are reported to four decimals, NLL to two, and rates to one. Significance uses McNemar on seed-42 predictions, 1,000 group bootstrap resamples, and Wilcoxon across seeds. No question appears in both training and evaluation. Calibration is compared on raw, Platt, and isotonic scores; Table~\ref{tab:protocols} defines P0, P1, and P2, and every protocol uses the same test rows.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Two families of metrics are reported. Threshold-dependent metrics measure the decision at a fixed cut-off. Threshold-free metrics measure how well a model ranks answers. Both are needed because a model can rank well and still make poor decisions when the threshold is fixed. The confusion matrix supplies the four counts used below, TP, FP, FN, and TN. Precision is the share of flagged answers that are truly hallucinated, and a high value means a reviewer can trust an alert. Recall is the share of hallucinated answers that the detector catches, and a high value means few bad answers pass. F1 is the harmonic mean of the two, and it punishes a model that is strong on one metric and weak on the other. The false-positive rate is the share of correct answers that are flagged. It is the operating constraint under distribution shift, where most natural answers are correct. The flag rate is the share of answers above the decision threshold, and it measures the review workload. A flag rate near 100\% means the detector sends every answer to a human.
\begin{gather}
  P = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
  R = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
  F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}, \\
  \mathrm{FPR} = \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{TN}}, \qquad
  \mathrm{Flag} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}_i \ge \tau].
\end{gather}

The second group measures ranking, calibration and paired error patterns. AUROC is the probability that a randomly chosen hallucinated answer receives a higher score than a randomly chosen correct answer. It measures ranking and does not depend on a threshold, and a value of 0.5 is chance. PR-AUC is the area under the precision-recall curve, and it is more sensitive than AUROC when the positive class is rare. RAGTruth QA has a positive rate of 17.8\%, so PR-AUC is reported beside AUROC. MCC uses all four cells of the confusion matrix and ranges from $-1$ to $1$, with zero at chance. Accuracy is misleading on an imbalanced test set, so MCC is the safer single-number summary. Negative log-likelihood rewards confident correct probabilities and penalizes confident wrong ones, and lower is better. The Wilson interval gives a 95\% range for a proportion and is used for the P2 recall and false-positive rate, where the counts are small.
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{AUROC} = \int_{0}^{1} \mathrm{TPR}(u)\, d\,\mathrm{FPR}(u) = P(s^{+} > s^{-}), \\
  \mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} P(r)\, dr, \qquad
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}\cdot\mathrm{TN} - \mathrm{FP}\cdot\mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}, \\
  \mathrm{NLL} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)\right], \\
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\end{gather}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the HaluEval test results for ten baselines and EC-XGB, averaged over the three seeds where a seed applies. The F1 ranking is tight. Logistic regression is 0.019 behind the conventional model, the random forest is 0.0004 behind, and EC-XGB is 0.0009 ahead. The 0.0009 lead is much smaller than the width of the seed-42 bootstrap interval for the conventional F1, [0.9812, 0.9895], so the ordering is not resolved by the data. The precision and recall columns show why. The random forest gains recall but loses precision, and EC-XGB improves both slightly. These differences are small because the benchmark is saturated. The conventional row in Table~\ref{tab:models} is the B2 checkpoint. The m0 row in the component ablation is a separate tuning run under the EC-XGB protocol, so the two differ by 0.0002 in F1 and 0.0021 in ECE.

The ranking columns tell the same story. AUROC and PR-AUC place EC-XGB first among the feature-based models, at 0.9984 and 0.9987, with the conventional model and the random forest within 0.0006. RoBERTa is higher at 0.9990 and 0.9993. Logistic regression is lower, at 0.9932 AUROC. MCC is 0.9697 for the conventional model and 0.9715 for EC-XGB. The calibration columns are separate from discrimination. The conventional model has the lowest ECE at 0.0045, but its Brier score is higher than EC-XGB and RoBERTa. The two summaries therefore disagree on which model is better. ECE depends on the bin edges, while the Brier score uses every row. The neural baseline is the strongest model in-domain, at 0.9912 F1 and 0.9990 AUROC. The heuristic reaches 0.9352 F1, the length-only control 0.9457, the NLI-only control 0.6714, the answer-only TF-IDF model 0.9224, and the full-input TF-IDF model 0.5999. The majority baseline scores zero. The NLI-only result shows that the six probabilities carry some signal, but not enough. The gap between the two TF-IDF models shows that adding the question and context to a bag-of-words model hurts.

The XGBoost margin over the random forest is small, at 0.0004 in F1, and the paired McNemar test on the same seed-42 rows gives $p=0.502$. The difference is not significant. The margin over logistic regression is larger, at 0.0187 in F1, and the same test gives $p=1.86\times10^{-10}$. The margin over the heuristic is 0.0495 in F1, with $p=2.28\times10^{-30}$. The random forest is therefore the only classical baseline that matches XGBoost on this benchmark. A neural baseline trained solely on the source material outperforms EC-XGB in-domain (F1 0.9912 vs 0.9855), but it is near chance on each RAGTruth task seen in training (AUROC 0.455 for summarization and 0.477 for data-to-text), against 0.582 for EC-XGB. The pooled 0.315 is lower because the two tasks have different positive rates. Training an adapted encoder on exactly the same multi-source rows raises it to 0.694 on those tasks, above EC-XGB's 0.582, so the gain on seen tasks is not specific to the feature model. This adaptation does not enable transfer learning, since neither the adapted encoder nor EC-XGB generalizes to the unseen QA task (adapted encoder = 0.490 vs EC-XGB = 0.549), and FaithBench is worse for both (adapted encoder = 0.434 vs original EC-XGB = 0.594). Adaptation improves any model's performance on seen tasks, whereas the feature model retains its relative ordering on unseen tasks.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Statistical tests use the paired seed-42 predictions and 1{,}000 group-level bootstrap resamples. McNemar's test compares the discordant counts $b$ and $c$, where $b$ is the number of rows where the first model is right and the second is wrong, and $c$ is the reverse. The test statistic is
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2 = \frac{(|b-c|-1)^2}{b+c},
\end{equation}
with a continuity correction. Holm's method orders the $m$ p-values and adjusts the $i$-th value as
\begin{equation}
  p_{(i)}^{\text{Holm}} = \min_{j \ge i} \left\{ \min\left(1, (m-j+1)p_{(j)}\right) \right\},
\end{equation}
which controls the family-wise error rate across the four baseline comparisons. The paired bootstrap delta is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_b = \theta_{\text{EC}}(\mathcal{D}_b) - \theta_{\text{conv}}(\mathcal{D}_b),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_b$ is a resample of groups and $\theta$ is the metric. The 95\% interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the $\Delta_b$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \left[\Delta_{(\alpha/2)}, \Delta_{(1-\alpha/2)}\right].
\end{equation}
The Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic is
\begin{equation}
  W = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \operatorname{rank}(|d_s|) \mathbf{1}[d_s > 0],
\end{equation}
where $d_s$ is the difference between the two models on seed $s$ and $S=3$.

Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} gives the McNemar counts. EC-XGB and the conventional model are not significantly different ($p=0.45$). That is the primary comparison, and it is not part of the Holm family, which covers the four baseline comparisons against the conventional model. At $p=0.45$ it would not survive correction. The random forest matches the conventional model ($p=0.502$). Logistic regression and the heuristic are significantly worse ($p=1.86\times10^{-10}$ and $p=2.28\times10^{-30}$), and both survive Holm correction. The Wilcoxon test across three seeds cannot reach significance (minimum $p=0.25$). Table~\ref{tab:stats} gives the external bootstrap deltas, which are seed 42. The QA AUROC delta is $+$0.0367 with interval [0.0056, 0.0681]. The per-seed deltas are $+$0.0372, $-$0.0673, and $+$0.0379, with a mean of $+$0.0026. The seed-42 interval describes one draw. The three-seed mean is 0.0026, so the QA gain is within seed noise. The AUROC interval excludes zero on every task. The ECE interval excludes zero on RAGTruth QA, summarization, and data-to-text, and is positive on FaithBench. The summarization F1 interval includes zero, and the FaithBench F1 interval is below zero.

\subsection{Explanation, Efficiency, and Judges}

The explanation results use the conventional B2 model with the source Platt calibrator; the study predates the EC-XGB checkpoint, but the deployed system applies the same TreeSHAP procedure. Figure~\ref{fig:shapimp} ranks the top-12 features by mean absolute SHAP. Context overlap dominates at 3.04, answer length follows at 1.19 and 1.17, contradiction is 0.41, and question-answer cosine is 0.34. The remaining features contribute less than 0.29. Panel (a) shows the mean; panel (b) shows the beeswarm, where each point is one test row and the colour is the feature value. Three checks support the ranking: Kendall tau 0.66 against permutation importance, Jaccard 1.0 for the top-five set after 1{,}000 bootstrap resamples, and a monotone drop from $-$0.079 to $-$0.412 when the top-$k$ features are neutralized. The ranking explains why the error analysis focuses on length and overlap.

Structural edits barely move it: irrelevant insert 0.006 and clause shuffle 0.001. The top-1 SHAP feature never flips, and Spearman correlation stays between 0.84 and 0.95. The score reacts to evidence changes and stays still under reordering.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Discussion}

On the seed-42 run, the conventional calibrated XGBoost made 48 errors: 12 false positives and 36 false negatives, an error rate of 1.6\%. A heuristic auto-tagging of 20 errors sampled evenly across the two directions grouped them into short-answer ambiguity and label ambiguity. The categories are rule-based. The sample was not independently re-annotated. Seven false positives and four false negatives were label ambiguity. Three false positives and six false negatives were short-answer ambiguity. The sample is balanced, so these counts describe the reviewed cases, not the full population. The counts here are seed 42, and the rates in Table~\ref{tab:length} are three-seed means, so its totals differ slightly. Table~\ref{tab:length} shows why length matters. The hallucination rate rises from 2.1\% for one-word answers to 12.7\% at three to four words and 90.2\% at five to eight words, then to 99.2\% at 17+ words. False positives concentrate in the five to eight bin, and false negatives in the short bins. Answer length is therefore a near-perfect label indicator on HaluEval. The mean score follows the same curve, from 0.024 to 0.993.

Four controls test the length explanation. A logistic regression on the two length variables reaches F1 0.9457 and AUROC 0.9759. A reweighting control equalizes six length bins with 247 examples each. On that set, EC-XGB reaches F1 0.9867 and AUROC 0.9987, against 0.9475 and 0.9758 for a length-only baseline. The gain is +0.039 F1 and +0.023 AUROC, so the length cue is not the whole story. The control does not prove the shortcut is gone.

A length-matching control pairs one positive and one negative at each length, giving 163 pairs (326 examples) over 200 resamples. EC-XGB holds AUROC 0.9608 $\pm$ 0.0022 while the length-only baseline falls to 0.5257 $\pm$ 0.0034, a gap of 0.4351 [0.429, 0.442]. A length-conditional test removes the cue without subsampling: within each exact-length stratum, length is constant, so the length-only model is at 0.5 by construction. Across 12 strata, EC-XGB reaches 0.9773 $\pm$ 0.0009. The length cue explains the zero-shot failure but not the in-domain ranking.

The shape explains the zero-shot result: complete sentences are long, so the conventional model reads them as hallucinations. The informative results are outside the benchmark. EC-XGB ranks the RAGTruth task types seen in training better than the conventional model, and the display calibrator gives readable target scores. The gain comes from multi-source training, not a different learning system. HaluEval labels length: 2.1\% of one-word answers are hallucinations, and 99.2\% at 17+ words. The ablation leaves lexical and length as the only groups that matter, so the in-domain model is a length and overlap detector. That is why natural long answers are flagged at 99.9\%, calibration cannot repair the ranking, and held-out QA stays at 0.549. The contribution is the protocol that exposes this chain, not a general detector. A fine-tuned encoder still wins in-domain, so the choice is a cost-transparency-accuracy trade-off. Four limits bound these claims. Deployment uses gold context, so retrieval quality and end-to-end verdict accuracy are not evaluated. The judge comparison uses 200 balanced HaluEval rows. The audit is error-enriched. The HaluEval benchmark is saturated, so its in-domain differences are not resolvable.




\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A useful hallucination detector must be fast, inexpensive, and explainable. This research has created EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent XGBoost risk predictor. EC-XGB uses 26 evidence-consistency features, 8 claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator, with log-scaled lengths under monotone constraints, multi-source training, calibrated probabilities, and SHAP explanations. On HaluEval QA it reaches an F1 of 0.9855 and an AUROC of 0.9984, which matches conventional XGBoost on a saturated benchmark. On the RAGTruth tasks used in training, the pooled flag rate falls from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, while held-out QA remains at 97.4\% and AUROC 0.549. Target calibration lowers ECE from 0.726 to 0.126, but a prevalence-only predictor at the calibration prevalence matches its Brier score, and recall at a 5\% false-positive budget is 5.0\%. Under matched multi-source training, the adapted encoder reaches 0.694 on the seen RAGTruth tasks, above EC-XGB's 0.582, while the source-only encoder wins in-domain (0.9912 F1) but is near chance on each seen task (0.455 and 0.477). On unseen QA both are near chance (AUROC 0.490 vs 0.549), and EC-XGB's F1 stays near 0.30, so EC-XGB is less degraded than the adapted encoder but not a usable QA detector. One analysis takes 61.8 ms and costs about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions. The GPT 5.6 Luna judge costs about 106 times more per 1{,}000 predictions and runs about 23 times slower. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific risk assessment tool and adaptation check, not a general-purpose detector. Future work will cover multicalibration under stronger shifts, conformal prediction, threshold transfer, and controlled tests for shortcuts.
