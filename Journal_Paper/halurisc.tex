\begin{abstract}
  Large language models answer questions in fluent text, and some answers are wrong in ways the evidence does not support. Detectors for false information have many limitations. For example, they require a large judge model; a computer with graphics capability to run an encoder classifier; feature-based detectors provide little information regarding how well their estimates are calibrated or how well these detectors perform outside the domain of their training data. Therefore, this study was designed to see if a simple black-box model could accurately predict the likelihood of hallucinations from the candidate answer itself along with the question asked and the supporting evidence provided. The proposed method, Evidence Consistent eXtreme Gradient Boosting (EC-XGB), uses an ensemble of decision trees trained over 35 features derived from the input evidence. Eight of the 35 features represent the extent to which the claims are supported. Because the benchmark is nearly fully saturated, the useful gain comes from better reliability during out-of-domain shifts. A tree model using only explicit evidence features requires no model access and remains inexpensive to interpret. Results show that EC-XGB achieves an F1 of 0.9855 and an AUROC of 0.9984 on the HaluEval question answering task, competitive with a conventional XGBoost model. When evaluating results on task type categories (RAGTruth) included in the training set, the AUROC increases from 0.475 to 0.582, while the proportion of natural RAGTruth answers identified as at-risk decreases from 99.9\% to 61.3\%. Performance on a QA evaluation task not seen during training is consistent with random guessing (i.e., an F1 near 0.30). Finally, a model-specific display calibrator fit to labeled target rows reduces the average target calibration error from 0.726 to 0.126.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A number of researchers have examined how to apply machine learning techniques to assess the likelihood of a response being a hallucination. One such technique is SelfCheckGPT, which uses several generated answers and assesses their similarity to determine if there has been a hallucination \cite{selfcheck}. Another technique is Luna, where a smaller evaluation model is used instead of a larger judge model \cite{luna}. A third technique is semantic entropy, which identifies meaning-based groupings of similar responses \cite{farquhar2024semantic}. A hybrid framework combines frozen encoder features with lightweight classifiers and reports strong benchmark scores \cite{ieeeTai}. Judges carry high computational costs and encoder models are hard to interpret. Two recent reviews have grouped approaches to detecting hallucinations into three categories including feature-based detectors, encoder-based detectors, and combinations of the above \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey}. Both reviews identify evaluation and reliability as two major areas that require additional research, and a third review determines that reliability is an area of concern as well \cite{alansari2026survey}.

Six recent studies address the same topic of assessing hallucinations. HaluAgent demonstrated that small language models can select appropriate tools and detect multiple forms of hallucinations, but it required multiple tool calls for each answer \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. proposed a calibrated multi-score detector with a detailed cost analysis, but the detector relies heavily on large language model (LLM) scoring for its primary source of information \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. proposed a system that uses retrieval, natural language inference (NLI) verification, and an XGBoost classifier, and they report only classification metrics \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME attributions to each word, but did not test whether those values remained consistent across words \cite{multimedia}. Deng et al. found that detectors degrade once they leave the domain in which they were trained, and their method focuses on multi-turn dialogue signals \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench shows that modern LLMs still produce hard summarization hallucinations, and it does not assess calibration risks \cite{faithbench}.

The existing literature leaves four issues unaddressed. Calibration is infrequently reported for lightweight detectors, explanation quality is rarely measured, cross-domain behavior is rarely tested, and error patterns are rarely analyzed case by case. This paper addresses all four issues by developing a black-box model that takes a query $, a reference context $, and a candidate answer $, and outputs (H \mid Q, C, A)$. The model, evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB), consists of 26 evidence-consistency features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a tuned XGBoost classifier. Platt and isotonic calibration yield the probability, SHAP explanations reveal feature contributions, artifact-control baselines and group-aware splits prevent shortcut solutions, and validation-only calibration solves the reliability problem. Transfer limits are established on RAGTruth and FaithBench.

HaluEval QA provides 10{,}000 paired correct and hallucinated answers for training and in-domain testing \cite{haluEval}. RAGTruth provides natural responses for transfer and recalibration \cite{ragtruth}. The scope is question answering with a reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. EC-XGB reaches an F1 of 0.9855 on HaluEval QA. On natural RAGTruth answers, the flag rate falls from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, and the AUROC rises from 0.475 to 0.582. Target calibration lowers the RAGTruth QA ECE from 0.726 to 0.126. On HaluEval, recall stays above 99\% at a five percent false-positive rate, and one analysis takes 61.8~ms at about \.001 per 1{,}000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Fluent hallucinations are those that are not supported by the available evidence. In most cases, detection quality is assessed using labeled benchmarks and a metric, typically precision, recall, F1 or the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Current test beds include HaluEval \cite{haluEval} and TruthfulQA \cite{truthqa} for hallucination detection. FActScore \cite{factscore} assesses factual accuracy for long-form generation. For deployment, there are two key evaluation considerations. First, calibration. Only if a risk score has a numeric value that is consistent with the probability of generating a hallucination can that risk score be used effectively. Expected calibration error (ECE) and reliability diagrams are used to show how far off a model is in terms of calibration \cite{guo}. Second, the need for interpretability. Users need to understand what specific signal caused their high-risk flag to appear, and this can be done through feature attribution techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for tabular models \cite{shap}. This work assesses both of these properties within a black-box setting.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method in this study is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 hand-crafted evidence-consistency features. XGBoost is selected as the baseline because it handles nonlinear interactions, it trains quickly on a tabular design matrix, and its tree structure supports exact SHAP values. XGBoost is a gradient-boosting ensemble, so the model adds decision trees one at a time and each new tree fits the error left by the trees already in the ensemble \cite{xgboost}. Each tree splits the feature space into leaves, and each leaf stores a weight that adds to the running score. The final score passes through a logistic link, so the output is a probability of the hallucinated class.

Let $x_i$ be the feature vector of row $i$ and $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label. The model sums $K$ trees, $z_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)$. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $\phi = \{f_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ collects the trees, $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves of weight $w_k$, $\gamma$ is a leaf-count penalty, and $\lambda$ is an L2 weight penalty. The loss $l$ is the standard logistic loss and the probability comes from the sigmoid of the summed leaf score, $\hat{y}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$.

Tree depth, learning rate, tree count, subsample rate, and column subsample rate are tuned on the training split, and the selected values and the search protocol are reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. This baseline is the reference for every later modification, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:baseline} summarizes its training and inference procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and inference)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, calibrator, and decision threshold
    \State extract the 26 evidence-consistency features for every row
    \State tune XGBoost with a randomized search and grouped 5-fold cross-validation
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State train the classifier on the training split
    \State fit Platt calibration and select the decision threshold on the validation split
    \State evaluate on the fixed test split
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Limitations and Research Gap}

This baseline performs well as a standalone for its specific benchmark, but it is limited. On HaluEval the benchmark has saturated, so the addition of features has little impact on performance. The baseline relies heavily on both answer length and lexical similarity. As demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:results}, answer length is essentially an ideal feature to utilize when working with this dataset. However, under domain shift, the raw probabilities lose their reliability. Specifically, the baseline flags virtually all natural answers, and its confidence no longer correlates with the observed error rate.

The benchmark itself represents one limiting factor, since close to 85\% of HaluEval answers were produced by sampling and filtering~\cite{bang-etal-2025-hallulens}. The line separating factual errors from hallucinations is therefore blurred in many instances. Another issue is that the baseline does not include a claim-level evidence signal, was trained on a single corpus, and provides a probability without a method to calibrate it to the target domain or a reliability test for its explanations.

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Working Principle and Task}

The entire proposed pipeline is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. The system accepts a question, an optional reference context (evidence), and a candidate answer produced by a black-box LLM, and it produces the calibrated risk probability and per-feature explanations for that answer.

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.76\linewidth]{architecture-flow.png}
  \caption{End-to-end architecture of the proposed model. The inputs feed 35 features into the EC-XGB classifier, Platt calibration produces the deployed score, and the outputs are the risk score, the per-claim verdicts, and the SHAP explanations. The system is deployed as a Next.js and FastAPI application and evaluated in-domain on HaluEval and under shift on RAGTruth and FaithBench.}

The goal is to estimate the conditional probability $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$ of hallucination, conditioned on the question $q$, the reference context $c$ (if applicable), and the candidate answer $a$ produced by a black-box LLM. The approach does not estimate truth. It flags cases where an answer does not follow from, or contradicts, the provided evidence.

\subsection{Features}

The model uses 26 base features in seven categories (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and eight claim-level aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:claim}). For NLI, six signal probabilities are determined using a DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder \cite{deberta} trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}. These probabilities capture whether a given statement follows logically from another. Semantic similarity uses MiniLM sentence embeddings \cite{sbert,minilm}, entities are detected by spaCy named entity recognition \cite{spacy}, and numeric and hedging features are found through regular expression matching or a small hedging lexicon.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB alters three key aspects of the conventional XGBoost learner. These changes allow each component of the learning process to be assessed individually. First, raw-length counts are replaced with log-scaled values, and monotonicity constraints ensure that risk cannot decrease as contradiction or novelty rises and cannot increase as entailment, overlap, or semantic similarity rises. Raw-count length is replaced with its log-scaled value, $\ell' = \log(1+\ell)$.

Second, the eight claim-level aggregates in Table~\ref{tab:claim} are added to the evidence signals that the deployed verification layer reports, so the classifier sees the same signals. Consider an answer composed of $K$ claims, with $C(k)$ the contradiction and $E(k)$ the entailment of claim $k$, and let $v_k$ be the verdict of claim $k$. The aggregate vector is
\begin{gather}
  \psi(x) = [\max_k C(k), \overline{C}, r_{\text{con}}, r_{\text{sup}}, r_{\text{unsup}}, \min_k E(k), \overline{E}, K], \\
  r_{\text{con}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{contradicted}], \quad
  r_{\text{sup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{supported}], \\
  r_{\text{unsup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{unsupported}].
\end{gather}
Here $\overline{C}$ and $\overline{E}$ are the means of $C(k)$ and $E(k)$ over the claims. Let $\phi'(x)$ be the complete model input and $\tilde{\phi}(x)$ the remaining 25 base features excluding $\ell'$; there are 35 input features in total.

Third, a source indicator $s \in \{0,1\}$ is added as a training column for RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text. Because all RAGTruth QA rows are excluded during training, the QA setting remains zero-shot. The deployed display score is fitted on labeled target rows and is therefore not zero-shot. The deployed checkpoint incorporating all three modifications is referred to as EC-XGB.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled HaluEval QA rows, RAGTruth non-QA rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}$
    \Ensure deployed model, display calibrator, source threshold, target threshold
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim-level aggregates
    \State log-scale the length features and set the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input with the source indicator
    \State train XGBoost on HaluEval plus RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of training
    \State fit the display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration split (P1)
    \State select the source-only threshold on the HaluEval validation split (P0)
    \State select the target threshold on the same calibration split at five percent FPR (P2)
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the deployed model, calibrator, and thresholds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The contribution is the combination. Each piece is standard on its own, and together they hold the in-domain score, cut the false-alarm rate under shift, and keep the output explainable at the feature level. The contribution is therefore a method and an evaluation protocol rather than a new learning algorithm. The deployed system adds claim-level verification with evidence retrieval and a display calibrator fitted on the target domain. Relative to the closest feature-based detector \cite{ijert}, the difference is the claim-level evidence features and the calibration protocol. Relative to the LLM-scored detector \cite{costeff} and the tool-based detector \cite{haluagent}, the difference is that inference needs no model calls. Their scores come from other corpora and other protocols, so the comparison is about the protocol and not about the numbers. None of the three reports the calibration error or the shifted flag rate that this study measures.

\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

Calibrators are fit on the validation split only. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid $p(x) = \sigma(A f(x) + B)$ to the raw score $f(x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are fit by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing step function $g$ so that $p(x) = g(f(x))$ \cite{isotonic}. Both are compared against the raw model on the test split. The comparison uses expected calibration error over $M=10$ bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the accuracy and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, together with the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$ and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. A second step targets a new domain. The calibrator can be re-fit on a small labeled sample of the target corpus, and the effect is measured on a disjoint held-out part of the target corpus. The deployed EC-XGB display score follows the same rule: its calibrator is fitted on the 5{,}034-row RAGTruth QA calibration split, and the source indicator is set to one for natural responses. This target calibration needs labeled target rows and is not zero-shot. The source-only protocol uses the HaluEval validation split for both the calibrator and the threshold.

The global SHAP \cite{shap} explanations from a TreeExplainer applied to the raw XGBoost model were used to create the Mean Absolute Importance and Beeswarm Summaries. The local explanations of features that contribute to a particular prediction are provided. Many feature attribution methods can be unreliable so explanation reliability is measured directly \cite{adebayo2018}. The test answers were changed in six different ways (replacing entity names, numbers, and dates; removing supporting context; adding irrelevant context; and shuffling the clauses). This study quantify the reliability by calculating four different measures (the mean absolute score change; the fraction of large score changes; the top-1 flip rate; and the Spearman rank correlation between the original and perturbed scores.)

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

These methods are used in a two-tiered web application. The Next.js frontend provides a streaming LLM conversation on the chat page using the assistant-ui library, while the FastAPI backend hosts the model, the calibrator, the SHAP explainer, and the feature models. All of these are loaded into memory at startup. A Next.js proxy forwards each request to a FastAPI endpoint to avoid cross-origin issues and keep the API key on the server. All quantitative results in Section~\ref{sec:results} pertain to the EC-XGB classifier and its calibrator.

A second deployment component is the claim verification layer. It displays a different verdict based on the evidence for each claim. It is demonstrated through the interface rather than quantified with the other measures.

In addition to providing the score, the system also verifies claims by checking them individually. Claims are broken down from the answer text and then checked with sentence-level NLI against one of three evidence sources: the provided context, an indexed document collection, or live web search. Verdicts are made first as support-based, so any supporting sentence provides evidence for a claim, while a contradiction counts only when no supporting sentence exists. A relevance gate is applied next to drop retrieved sentences that do not contain vocabulary related to the claim. The supported, contradicted, and unsupported verdicts are then displayed per claim. For each contradicting claim, the driving evidence sentence is also quoted.

The verdict card sets the risk to high for any contradicted claim, to medium for an unsupported claim, and to low for a fully supported claim. The risk order is consistent with the style sensitivity found in Section~\ref{sec:results}, where an answer containing complete sentences may cause the calibrated score to reach saturation levels while every individual claim is still supported.

The risk percentage shown on the card is determined by the EC-XGB display calibrator but is modified by the verdict assigned to each individual claim.

Without modification by a calibration method, the raw score itself does not represent a usable risk level. The deployed display calibrator indicates 35.4\% risk and classifies the answer as moderate risk.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Conditions}

All experiments were run on a Windows-based computer (laptop), with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU, CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128, and 32\,GB of RAM. The programming language used to run the code is Python. The Python version used in this experiment is 3.12. The code utilizes the following packages: scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. Each run can be reproduced because of saved split indices and centralized seeds.

A randomized search was performed over 30 hyperparameter configurations with grouped five-fold cross-validation. The configuration used for XGBoost has a maximum tree depth of 4, a learning rate of 0.01, 500 trees, a subsampling ratio of 0.9, and a column sampling ratio of 0.7. Seeds 42, 123, and 456 are repeated throughout.

\subsection{Dataset}

The QA subset of HaluEval \cite{haluEval} serves as the training data. This dataset has 10{,}000 questions. Each question has a knowledge passage that it is based upon, a correct answer, and a hallucinated version of that answer. Therefore, there are 20{,}000 rows of labeled data. The 20{,}000 rows are split 70/15/15 at the group level into 14{,}000 training, 3{,}000 validation, and 3{,}000 test rows. Since both versions of the answer to each question come from the same knowledge passage, the model can easily learn the relationship between the context and the answers, so the split is at the group level.

Transfer was evaluated using two datasets. First, RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth}: a collection of responses produced through natural processes that have been annotated with word-level spans by humans. Second, FaithBench \cite{faithbench}: a hallucination-based summarization benchmark based on outputs from modern large language models. The RAGTruth QA task contributed 5{,}934 rows of QA tasks in 989 groups. The remaining 900 rows in 150 groups formed the test set, and none of these groups overlap with those used in the recalibration set. The summary of the corpora is provided in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.

Text is normalized for whitespace. Lexical features work on lowercased token collections. NER and NLI use the original case. Missing context is treated as a special condition. Lexical overlap is 0, and NLI has all probabilities equal to one third. All three of these datasets were developed using their original licensing agreements: HaluEval and RAGTruth (MIT) and FaithBench (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). The raw data is not redistributed; however, the repository ships the download scripts, the source revisions, and the SHA-256 hashes, which allows regeneration of each of the splits based upon the original repositories.

\subsection{Experimental Procedure}

Nine methods are tested. The overlap heuristic sets risk to one minus the lexical overlap between the answer and the context. Two additional classifiers were used: a logistic regression on all standardized input features (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and a random forest with 300 decision trees. The conventional method is XGBoost \cite{xgboost}, which uses the 26 base features and is the reference for the proposed model in Section~\ref{sec:proposed}. To assess the impact of the feature design alone, three control experiments were run. An NLI-only logistic regression was fit solely on the six NLI probability outputs; an answer-only TF-IDF linear model was fit on the vectorized answers; and a TF-IDF linear model was fit on the entire input. Internal-state detectors need model access and fall outside the black-box setting \cite{inside2024}. A majority classifier that always predicts the negative class shows that the test data is balanced across classes.

A RoBERTa-base classifier fine-tuned on the labeled source data provides the source-only neural evaluation \cite{liu2019roberta}. The classifier receives the answer, question, and context as a single input sequence of up to 256 tokens. The model is trained with binary cross-entropy loss for three epochs. Platt scaling is fitted on the validation split.

Evaluation uses precision, recall, F1, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC), and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). Calibration is measured with expected calibration error over 10 bins, the Brier score, and negative log-likelihood. Metrics are reported to four decimal places, and latency in milliseconds. Significance is assessed with McNemar's test on the paired seed-42 predictions, bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals from 1{,}000 resamples over question or source groups, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test across seeds. Rows that represent the same question never occur in both the training and evaluation splits.

\subsection{Additional Protocols}

Calibration methods were evaluated through a comparison of the three probability sets (raw, Platt, and isotonic) on the test split. As well as being influenced by distribution shift \cite{ovadia2019}, the same comparison was performed during the target-domain evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:proposed} presents the details of the three protocols (P0, P1, and P2; presented in Table~\ref{tab:protocols}), along with the row split for each protocol.

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

Both P1 and P2 metrics utilized the 900 rows of the test set. No calibration row used to fit either model overlaps this subset.

SHAP \cite{shap} TreeExplainers provide global explanations for the raw XGBoost model. There are three ways to determine whether these explanations can be trusted. First, the mean absolute SHAP ranking is compared with permutation importance via Kendall's tau correlation. Secondly, the top five feature set is re-estimated based upon 1{,}000 bootstrap resamples of the data, then the Jaccard index is computed between these two feature sets. Finally, two perturbation studies are performed on the test responses.

In content edits, entities, numbers, or dates are changed, supporting context is removed or an unrelated sentence is added to the content; each edit should affect the calibrated score if it alters the evidence. In structural edits, clause order is modified; the calibrated score should remain constant under such modifications. Four different metrics are computed for each type of operator.

The first metric is the mean absolute score change of each operator. The second metric is the proportion of operators for which a ``large'' change occurred. The third metric is the top-1 SHAP flip rate of each operator. The fourth metric is the Spearman rank correlation between original and perturbed scores.

A neutralization study determines how much the score assigned to each sample by a model will change once its $k$ largest SHAP feature values have been reset to their respective medians. Mean changes are reported over samples for $k = 1, 3, 5, 10$.

Latency was determined on 200 test samples on a reference laptop equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU. Features in the models other than those corresponding to NLI and embedding computations ran on the CPU. Latency was also measured on the same 200 samples using GPT 5.6 Luna \cite{zheng2023judge}, a biased model that serves as a standard judge comparison, with a fixed prompt, and costs were calculated from the recorded token counts.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model comparison}

The performance of all the baselines has been averaged across the three runs to report their overall performance on the test set. For example, the F1 score of 0.9846 and AUROC of 0.9979 were both obtained when using the XGBoost method. These values are slightly greater than those achieved through the use of the random forest (by 0.0004) and greater than those obtained using logistic regression (by 0.0186). The heuristic (which finds its own threshold without any need for further training) yielded an F1 value of 0.9352, and the majority baseline (wherein the classifier always predicts the most common label) produced an F1 value of 0.0000, providing evidence that the data is well-balanced. The NLI-only baseline scored very poorly at 0.6714. This shows that the other baselines captured some information about the lexical, length, and semantic properties of the input texts that was not available via the NLI cross-encoder alone. The TF-IDF models were positioned between these two extremes; the answer-only linear model scored 0.9224, and the full-input model scored 0.5999.

Although the XGBoost margin over random forest is relatively small (only 0.0004 in F1), it was statistically significant in a McNemar test comparing the paired seed-42 test predictions ($p=0.044$). However, there were no such statistical differences when comparing XGBoost to logistic regression ($p = 0.43$) or the heuristic ($p = 0.35$).

A neural baseline trained solely on the source material outperforms EC-XGB in-domain (F1 0.9912 vs 0.9846), but fails completely to generalize across domains (RAGTruth AUROC = 0.337 vs EC-XGB AUROC = 0.582). Training an adapted encoder on exactly the same multi-source rows used to train EC-XGB reduces this domain-shift loss and raises the adapted encoder to 0.694, against EC-XGB's 0.582. This adaptation does not enable transfer learning, since neither the adapted encoder nor the original EC-XGB generalizes to the unseen QA task (adapted encoder = 0.490 vs original = 0.549), and even less so to FaithBench (adapted encoder = 0.434 vs original = 0.594). Adaptation improves any model's performance on seen tasks, whereas the feature model retains its relative ordering on unseen tasks.

Single-answer behavior shows how individual answers can disagree in ways that aggregate measures hide. For example, if two answers were generated from one question and one context, both would have an exact match under the overlap heuristic. They could also produce an identical result for an NLI-only model. However, logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost all produced different results.

\subsection{Cross-domain transfer}

The conventional model's baseline was based solely on its zero-shot performance since it was not retrained on the target corpora. Thus, the conventional model saw no RAGTruth summarization or data-to-text rows during training, while EC-XGB did, so the EC-XGB results for those tasks measure adaptation (Table~\ref{tab:shift}). Since 99.9\% of the RAGTruth rows and 99.5\% of the FaithBench rows exceeded the 0.5 threshold for the conventional model, there is little value in using the decision rule outside the benchmark. Recall rates did remain quite high for the conventional model. Compared with the conventional model, the EC-XGB method reduced the flag rate (the share of answers above 0.5) to 61.3\% on the full RAGTruth test split and to 7.3\% on FaithBench, and raised the AUROC from 0.475 to 0.582 on RAGTruth and from 0.509 to 0.594 on FaithBench. Furthermore, the ECE on RAGTruth decreased from 0.635 to 0.278. The held-out RAGTruth QA task is probably the most difficult test because none of its rows were included in the training set. There the F1 remained relatively constant (0.3022 vs. 0.3081). However, the ECE on the RAGTruth QA task decreased from 0.815 to 0.743. Therefore, multi-source training does not improve accuracy on previously unseen tasks for free. For example, on FaithBench, the conservative regime decreased the F1 from 0.813 to 0.120. As such, at this operating point, the AUROC and flag rate are the appropriate comparisons. However, the decrease in F1 is viewed as a limitation.

\subsection{Explanation reliability}

Figure~\ref{fig:shapimp} presents the mean absolute SHAP values of the top-12 features. The overlap in contexts has the greatest effect, followed by the lengths of answers, then the contradictions between contexts and answers (in terms of probability), and lastly the cosine similarity between questions and answers. Three tests check whether the provided explanations are consistent. The SHAP rankings agree with the permutation importances at a Kendall tau of 0.66. The same five top features appear consistently after bootstrapping 1{,}000 times, yielding a Jaccard of 1.0. Reducing the predicted mean score monotonically from $-$0.079 at $k=1$ to $-$0.412 at $k=10$ when neutralizing the top-$k$ features also indicates that these top features drive the predictions.

Table~\ref{tab:perturb} provides the results of a perturbation test. Changes in content cause score changes according to how much each edit affects the semantics of the original input. On average, replacing an entity caused a 0.360 change in score; numbers caused a 0.345 change; dates caused a 0.279 change; and removing supporting context caused a 0.272 change. Irrelevant inserts as well as clause shuffles had almost no effect on the score, changing it by 0.006 and 0.001, respectively. Not once did the top-1 SHAP feature flip under any operator used in this test. Spearman correlations stayed in the range of 0.84 to 0.95 throughout all tests conducted. Sample sizes were small for both date and clause shuffle edits, 13 and 14 samples respectively, as not every answer contained an editable element, and the size of each group was reported openly in Table~\ref{tab:perturb}.

A structured expert audit assessed every explanation. The B5 export produced 26 error-enriched cases. These included 9 false positives, 10 false negatives, and 7 borderline cases in the 0.35 to 0.65 interval. Two review passes used an AI assistant. In both passes the AI assistant evaluated each explanation against the answer-context evidence, not the dataset label. The two passes agreed on 23 of the 26 explanations (88\%). Each pass marked 15 explanations as implausible. False positives inherit the length confound. False negatives occur when a wrong answer reuses entities from its context, which keeps overlap high. There are two audited examples (item numbers 2251 and 2436) that have a hallucinated label for their correct answer although the context directly supports the answer. Therefore, these appear to be errors due to label noise in the benchmark rather than errors produced by the model. \subsection{Error analysis}

There were 48 errors in the 3{,}000-sample test set for the conventional calibrated XGBoost: 12 false positives and 36 false negatives, for an error rate of 1.6\%. A heuristic analysis of 20 randomly selected errors (10 false positives and 10 false negatives) was grouped into two categories. These categories are (i) short-answer ambiguity, when answers are too short or ambiguous in terms of lexical or natural language inference (NLI) evidence, and (ii) label ambiguity, when answers are semantically possible under both labels. Seven false positives and four false negatives were classified as label ambiguity. Similarly, three false positives and six false negatives were classified as short-answer ambiguity. The sample is balanced across the two error directions, so these counts describe the reviewed cases, not the full population.

The key aspect of the data is how the answer length of incorrect answers is distributed. Table~\ref{tab:length} shows how many were false positives and false negatives. The amount of hallucinations per answer length grows very quickly. For instance, 2.1\% of one-word answers were hallucinations compared to 99.2\% of answers longer than 17 words. Because of this relationship, answer length acts as a nearly perfect indicator of whether an answer will be labeled as a hallucination in HaluEval. The average score increased from 0.024 to 0.993 across the groupings. False positives occur mostly at the lower end: about 66.7\% are in the five to eight word grouping. False negatives were most frequent by rate in the one to four word groups (about 18\%), but the five to eight word group had the largest count (18 of 36). This mechanism explains the zero-shot transfer result. Natural language generation outputs are usually complete sentences, so the conventional model reads their length as evidence of hallucination. This is why recall was 100\% while AUROC remained around 50\%.

Several tests and controls support the claim that answer length drives performance. First, a logistic regression on the two answer length variables achieved F1 = 0.9457 and AUROC = 0.9759. Second, a reweighting control selected 247 examples from each of six answer length groups, for 1{,}482 examples. On this reweighted dataset, EC-XGB achieved F1 = 0.9867 and AUROC = 0.9987, while a length-only baseline achieved F1 = 0.9475 and AUROC = 0.9758. The difference is +0.039 F1 [0.029, 0.051] and +0.023 AUROC [0.016, 0.031], so EC-XGB still outperforms the length-only baseline after bin counts are equalized. This control does not prove that the shortcut is gone. Third, a length-matching control paired one positive example with one negative example at each answer length. This gave 163 pairs (326 examples) on the full test set, repeated 200 times at different sampling seeds. EC-XGB maintained AUROC = 0.9608 $\pm$ 0.0022, while the length-only baseline dropped to 0.5257 $\pm$ 0.0034, and the difference was 0.4351 [0.429, 0.442]. Finally, a length-conditional test removed the cue without subsampling. Within each exact-length stratum that contains both classes, length is constant, so the length-only model is at AUROC 0.5 by construction. Across the 12 such strata, EC-XGB reached a pair-weighted AUROC of 0.9773 $\pm$ 0.0009 over seeds.

\subsection{Discussion}

The benchmark is close to saturation, so the informative result is the behavior outside it. EC-XGB keeps a usable ranking on RAGTruth where the conventional model flags almost every answer, and the display calibrator makes the target scores readable. The gain comes from evidence-consistent features and multi-source training, not from a new learner. A fine-tuned encoder still wins in-domain, so the feature model trades some accuracy for cost and transparency.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Hallucination detection needs to be cheap, fast, and explainable before it is useful in practice. This study presented EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent XGBoost risk predictor. It combines 26 evidence-consistency features with eight claim-level NLI aggregates, log-scaled length features under monotone constraints, multi-source training, calibrated probabilities, and SHAP explanations. On HaluEval QA it reaches an F1 of 0.9855 and an AUROC of 0.9984, on par with the conventional XGBoost on a saturated benchmark. The gain appears on the RAGTruth task types used in training, not on unseen tasks. The conventional model flags 99.9\% of natural RAGTruth answers as risky and EC-XGB flags 61.3\%, with the AUROC rising from 0.475 to 0.582 and the ECE falling from 0.635 to 0.278. A seed-42 display calibrator cuts the RAGTruth QA ECE from 0.726 to 0.126 and needs a labeled target sample (5{,}034 rows here), yet 252 labeled rows already bring the test ECE to 0.133. One analysis takes 61.8\,ms and costs about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions, roughly 100 times less than the GPT 5.6 Luna judge.

The negative results matter as much as the positive ones. A source-only fine-tuned encoder wins the benchmark (F1 0.9912) but ranks worse outside it (RAGTruth AUROC 0.337 against 0.582). The raw model needs no recalibration on HaluEval, the entity and hedging features add no measurable value, and the held-out RAGTruth QA task stays near an F1 of 0.30. The paper therefore frames the supervised model as a domain-specific risk estimator and adaptation check, not a general-purpose detector, and the deployed system adds claim-level verification with evidence retrieval. Future work targets multicalibration under stronger shift, conformal prediction, per-domain threshold transfer, and shortcut-controlled evaluation on unseen tasks.
