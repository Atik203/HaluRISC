\begin{abstract}
  Large language models answer fluently, but some answers are not supported by the evidence. Detectors based on a large judge model, an encoder, or model internals are expensive and rarely report calibration under domain or task shift. Feature-based detectors can be cheaper. Their calibration and sensitivity to answer length are usually not analyzed together. This work investigates whether a simple feature model can predict hallucination risk and how its ranking and calibration are affected by shift. This work introduces EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent XGBoost model with base features, claim-level natural language inference (NLI) aggregate scores, and a source indicator. The model is trained on HaluEval QA and RAGTruth non-QA rows with grouped splits and three seeds, and analyzed with fixed-hyperparameter ablations, source and target calibration, conformal prediction, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) stability analysis, a length-only control model, and judge stress tests. A feature model is cheap and interpretable, and its inputs can be observed directly, making it a feasible choice for routine screening. The fixed-hyperparameter model on HaluEval QA achieved a high performance on the metrics of F1 0.985 and AUROC 0.999. However, this performance was based on both lexical overlap with the correct answers and also length of the answers. On MedHallu, for example, in 86.7\% of the 1,000 pairs tested, the model ranked the answer to be incorrect as higher than the correct answer. Additionally, the model flagged every single row that was inputted into it; however, its calibration was poor (ECE = 0.493). On RAGTruth QA, the model stays close to chance. Target calibration lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, but flags no target rows at the default threshold. Split conformal prediction reaches 0.944 coverage at 90\%, with only 0.685 positive coverage. The dialogue dataset is almost completely flagged, summarization ranks lower than a length-only model, and a GPT judge beats the feature model on natural QA. Hence, EC-XGB is a domain-specific risk score, not a general-purpose detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Black-box detectors evaluate candidate answers in their context \cite{selfcheck,luna}. Detectors take the question, supporting evidence, and the candidate answer as input and output a score or a decision. Some detectors use a large judge model, some use an encoder on the input pair, and some analyze internal states \cite{farquhar2024semantic,ieeeTai}. These detectors require heavy computation, and encoder-based methods are hard to interpret. Since the input is plain text, a detector can be placed before any model. Two recent reviews classify detectors \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey}. Evaluation and reliability of detectors remain open questions, and the third review identifies the reliability gap independently \cite{alansari2026survey}. Thus, four open problems remain in existing research. First, the calibration of lightweight detectors is rarely evaluated. Second, explanation quality is evaluated even less often. Third, few models have been evaluated on cross-domain behavior. Finally, error-pattern analysis on a per-example basis remains rare.

HaluAgent showed that small models could pick tools, though it required several calls per answer \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. designed a calibrated multi-score detector with a cost analysis, but it is based on LLM scoring \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. proposed a retrieval, natural language inference (NLI), and XGBoost solution, but reported only classification metrics \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME explanations to words but did not measure their reliability \cite{multimedia}. In Deng et al.'s study of multi-turn dialogue, detector performance deteriorated outside the training domain \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench shows that current LLMs still produce summarization hallucinations, but it does not measure detector calibration \cite{faithbench}. The existing conventional feature-based benchmark also has several issues: it depends on answer length, does not give claim-level explanations, trains exclusively on HaluEval with a source-fitted calibrator, and lacks transferable probabilities \cite{ovadia2019}. HaluEval is saturated as well. Although the system is able to generate both ranked claims and their corresponding probabilities, there remains a disconnect between a ranking score that maintains relative order over time as well as maintaining confidence levels when the overall body of information has changed. This paper fills these gaps with a feature-based model that scores a black-box LLM output. Given a query $q$, a reference context $c$, and a candidate answer $a$, it outputs $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$. The EC-XGB model uses 26 features divided into seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator inside a tuned XGBoost classifier. This ranking score is then converted into a confidence level using Platt's calibration method. Grouped splits with length and overlap controls are used to reduce shortcut learning. Source calibration is fitted only on validation data. Additionally, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were used to provide supporting evidence or explanations for why the system chose a particular claim. Experiments also included evaluating the target application for calibration by checking how reliable the system's predictions were. The log transformation compresses the length scale but keeps the cue. Although the system was able to aggregate support based on individual explanations for each of the claims, the primary contributor to performance improvement on RAGTruth was due to the additional multi-source row data and the inclusion of a source indicator rather than the aggregation itself. In addition to creating transfer limits for RAGTruth and FaithBench, an additional new evaluation was added which includes MedHallu \cite{pandit2025medhallu} and a HaluBench subset \cite{ravi2024lynx} with no overlap during training, the HaluEval task shift, conformal prediction, and judge stress tests. Each of these evaluations is reported using F1, ranking, calibration, and paired error metrics with the distinction made between ranking and calibration such that although the system transferred ranking performance to medical pairs, the system's associated probability did not transfer, and failed under the task shift.

HaluEval QA contains 10,000 question pairs, each with a correct and a hallucinated answer, for training and in-domain testing. RAGTruth provides natural responses for adaptation and recalibration. The target application is question answering with a reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. The fixed-hyperparameter EC-XGB stack (m3) reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986 on HaluEval QA. RAGTruth shows the same pattern: the flag rate falls on tasks seen in training, while held-out QA stays near chance. On MedHallu, the same model ranks the hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1,000 pairs but flags every row. On HaluEval dialogue it flags almost every row, and on HaluEval summarization it ranks below a length-only model. Target calibration on 5,034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125 without changing the ranking. One answer takes 127.5~ms on GPU (2.00~s on CPU), and the approximate cost is about \$0.001 per 1,000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

This section covers the traditional feature-based detector along with its evaluation metrics. The hallucinations are the answers that cannot be supported by the reference context or contradict it. Every data row is in the form of a tuple $(q_i,c_i,a_i)$ comprising the question, reference context, and candidate answer with the label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. The traditional way of evaluating the detector is to use any one of the four metrics: precision, recall, F1, or AUROC of the labeled tuples. There are two additional requirements that must be satisfied by the deployed detector. Calibration demands that the risk score should match the observed frequency of the positive outcome, which is summarized by ECE \cite{guo}. Explanation means that the detector must point out the features that contributed to the warning, which is provided by SHAP for tabular models \cite{shap}.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 base features in Table~\ref{tab:features}, trained on HaluEval QA. XGBoost models nonlinear interactions and gives exact TreeSHAP values, which the explanation analysis needs. A published feature-based detector uses the same learner with retrieval and NLI features \cite{ijert}, so this model is the natural baseline. Trees are added one at a time, and each tree fits the current gradient and curvature of the loss. A row enters at the root of each tree, follows the split conditions to one leaf per tree, and the leaf weights are summed into a raw margin. A logistic link maps the raw margin to a probability. Let $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{26}$ be the feature vector of row $i$, $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label, and $n$ the number of training rows. The model sums $K$ trees into a raw margin, $z_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)$. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, z_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $\phi = \{f_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ collects the trees, $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves and leaf-weight vector $w_k \in \mathbb{R}^{T_k}$, $\gamma$ is the penalty per leaf, and $\lambda$ is an L2 weight penalty. The loss $l(y_i,z_i)$ is the logistic loss on the raw margin. The probability is the sigmoid of the raw margin, $\hat{p}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$. At boosting step $t$, a new tree $f_t$ with $T$ leaves and leaf weights $w_1,\dots,w_T$ is added, and the objective is approximated, up to a constant, by a second-order expansion around the current raw margin,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $g_i = \partial l(y_i,z)/\partial z$ and $h_i = \partial^2 l(y_i,z)/\partial z^2$ are evaluated at $z_i^{(t-1)}$, and $f_t(x_i) = w_{q(x_i)}$ is the leaf weight that row $i$ reaches, with $q(x_i)$ the leaf index. For the logistic loss, $g_i = \hat{p}_i - y_i$ and $h_i = \hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)$. For a fixed tree structure, the optimal weight of leaf $j$ is
\begin{equation}
  w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda},
\end{equation}
where $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$ and $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$ are the sums over the rows $I_j$ that reach leaf $j$. The gain of a split is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda} \right] - \gamma,
\end{equation}
with $G_L$ and $H_L$ for the left child and $G_R$ and $H_R$ for the right child. A split is kept only when the gain is positive. The tree also stops at the maximum depth or when a leaf has fewer than the minimum child weight.

Depth, learning rate, tree count, and the row and column subsampling rates are tuned on the 14{,}000 training rows. Each seed runs its own 30-configuration search with stratified group five-fold cross-validation, and the grouping keeps both answers to the same question in one fold. The seed-42 values are in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}, and the per-seed values are in the repository. Each seed trains on the 14{,}000 rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows (30 rounds). The 300-tree setting is the tuned upper bound, and seed 42 stops at 209 trees. The headline comparison uses the uncalibrated probability at a fixed 0.5 threshold on the 3{,}000 test rows. Platt and isotonic maps are fitted on the same validation rows for the calibration study. The source-only P0 threshold is selected on those rows at a 5\% false-positive budget. The test rows are never used for tuning, early stopping, calibration, or threshold selection. The last of these will calculate the mean for the three seed values. The search leaves $\gamma$, $\lambda$, and the minimum child weight at their XGBoost defaults. Algorithm~\ref{alg:baseline} summarizes the tuning, training, and calibration steps.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and evaluation)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, test metrics, and Platt and isotonic maps
    \State extract the 26 base features for every row
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State tune XGBoost on the 14{,}000 training rows with 30 random configurations and stratified group 5-fold cross-validation
    \State train the classifier on the same rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows
    \State evaluate the raw score on the 3{,}000 test rows at the fixed 0.5 threshold
    \State fit Platt and isotonic maps on the validation rows for the calibration study
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Working Principle}

The aim is to predict $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$, where $q$ is the input question, $c$ is the optional context, and $a$ is the answer generated by the LLM. Truthfulness is not the concern here, but rather the assessment of evidence consistency, that is, whether $a$ is entailed by $c$ or contradicts $c$. The output of the system is a probability value, which is translated to a label through the decision threshold. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline used to calculate the probability. The pipeline takes the inputs and extracts 35 features: 26 base features split into seven groups, eight aggregate NLI scores at the claim level, and a source indicator. EC-XGB produces a raw score from the features, which is calibrated by a Platt calibrator trained on RAGTruth QA calibration rows to obtain the displayed probability. The model outputs a risk score, verdicts at the claim level, and SHAP values. The bottom part of the figure shows the deployment pipeline, where the Next.js front end calls the FastAPI service, which loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. The right block lists the evaluation datasets. HaluEval evaluates in-domain performance, while RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text measure performance on task types seen in training. RAGTruth QA and FaithBench are held out.

\subsection{Features}

The model uses 26 base features in seven groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and eight claim-level aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:claim}). Probabilities of NLI are derived from the cross encoder DeBERTa-v3 model \cite{deberta} which was trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}. Semantic similarities are calculated by using embeddings from MiniLM \cite{sbert,minilm} to determine semantic similarities. Entities are identified using spaCy's named entity recognition (NER) tool \cite{spacy}. Since HaluEval labels answers that contain many words in addition to being longer as hallucinations more often than others, the length of an answer can be considered as a strong indicator of whether an answer has been labeled as a hallucination or not. Although lexical overlap is the most significant SHAP feature indicating copy and paste behavior, this does not indicate when someone copies the information into their response in a way that contradicts the original evidence while still paraphrasing. A novel entity is a useful warning sign, although a new name can be part of a correct answer. The NLI features are the only group that provides logic supporting the evidence rather than simply providing some form of overlap. A changed number is a common and easy-to-detect case of hallucination. Although hedging words are weak on their own, they make it possible to tell apart a cautious correct answer from a confident wrong one. Semantic similarity finds paraphrase and topic changes missed by lexical overlap. The eight claim-level aggregates repeat NLI features at the level of atomic claims, which is the level reviewers usually operate at. RAGChecker uses entailment-based claim metrics \cite{ru2024ragchecker}.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB modifies the conventional setup in terms of features and training samples. The character and word counts are log-transformed, and monotone constraints are placed on the evidence features. The constraints are negative for context overlap, context Jaccard, context entailment, context cosine, claim minimum and mean entailment, and the supported-claim ratio, and positive for context contradiction, novel-entity ratio, novel numbers, claim maximum and mean contradiction, and the contradicted-claim ratio. All other features remain unconstrained. Eight claim features from Table~\ref{tab:claim} are included: seven NLI aggregates and the claim count. They use the same NLI scorer and the support-first rule as the verification layer. A claim is supported if there is a retained sentence with an entailment probability of at least 0.5 that is at least as large as its contradiction probability. It is contradicted if there is no supporting sentence and there is a sentence with a contradiction probability of at least 0.5 that is larger than its entailment probability. Otherwise, the claim is considered unsupported. Consider an answer with $K$ claims. Let $C(k)$ be the maximum contradiction probability of claim $k$ over its selected context sentences, $E(k)$ the maximum entailment probability, and $v_k$ its verdict. The aggregate vector is
\begin{equation}
  \psi(x) = [\max_k C(k), \overline{C}, r_{\text{con}}, r_{\text{sup}}, r_{\text{unsup}}, \min_k E(k), \overline{E}, K],
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  r_{\text{con}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{contradicted}], \quad
  r_{\text{sup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{supported}], \quad
  r_{\text{unsup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{unsupported}].
\end{equation}
where $x=(q,c,a)$ is the input triplet, and $\overline{C}$ and $\overline{E}$ are the means of $C(k)$ and $E(k)$ over the claims. For $K>0$, the three ratios sum to one. The model input has 35 dimensions: the 26 base features (two of them log-scaled), the eight claim features, and one source indicator.

The training sample is extended to include the RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text rows. The ablation starts with m0, the 26-feature conventional reference, and moves on to m1, which uses log-transformed length counts under monotone constraints, to m2, which adds the eight claim features (34 features total), and to m3, which uses the RAGTruth non-QA rows with the source indicator (35 features total). A binary source indicator equal to one is used for those rows and equal to zero for HaluEval, which allows the classifier to separate the two sources. The classifier cannot view the RAGTruth QA rows, therefore it has performed zero-shot QA ranking. On the other hand, the display calibrator views labeled QA rows and generates a non-zero shot display score. The 35 feature approach is referred to as EC-XGB, where seed 42 was used as the deployed checkpoint and other seeds were used as a way to check stability. Ablation (as shown in Section~\ref{sec:results}) demonstrates that the Claim Block contributes nothing towards an aggregate F1 score; however, the claim count $K$ is retained because answers that have more claims are generally harder to verify. Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecxgb} explains how this works: Steps 1 and 2 create the Feature Matrix, while Steps 3-9 fit the model with all three seeds. This results in each iteration creating the 35 feature input, training on HaluEval plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, fitting the display calibrator using the 5{,}034 QA Calibration Rows, and setting both source and target threshold values.


\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require HaluEval QA training and validation rows, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text training rows, RAGTruth QA calibration rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}=\{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure seed-42 model and display calibrator, source and target thresholds, per-seed metrics
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim features for every row
    \State log-scale the character and word counts, and attach the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input, with the source indicator at zero for HaluEval and one for RAGTruth
    \State train XGBoost on the HaluEval rows plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of classifier training
    \State fit the Platt display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows
    \State select the source threshold on the HaluEval validation split
    \State select the target threshold on the QA calibration rows at a 5\% false-positive budget
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the seed-42 model and calibrator, both thresholds, and the per-seed metrics
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

The source calibration uses the HaluEval validation split. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid mapping $\hat{p} = \sigma(a s + b)$ to the uncalibrated probability $s$, where $a$ and $b$ are learned by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression learns a non-decreasing step function $g$ such that $\hat{p} = g(s)$ \cite{isotonic}. Both methods are tested against the uncalibrated probability on the HaluEval test split. The comparison provides the expected calibration error measured over 10 equal-width bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the empirical positive rate and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$, and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. Post-hoc calibration degrades when the target distribution moves \cite{gasparin2024distance}. In the second protocol, the calibrator is trained on labeled target rows and tested on a disjoint target test split. The deployed EC-XGB display score is calibrated using a Platt calibrator fitted on the 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows. These rows have the source indicator set to one, and the API uses the same value for all requests. This target calibration requires labeled target rows and is not zero-shot. The source-only protocol (P0 in Table~\ref{tab:protocols}) fits the calibrator and selects the threshold on the HaluEval validation split. The deployed calibrator is Platt. Platt versus isotonic was compared on the test split in an earlier deployment; see Section~\ref{sec:results}.

TreeExplainer computes the exact SHAP values \cite{shap} of the raw XGBoost model. Global Feature Importance is defined as the average absolute SHAP Value over a Stratified Test Sample. The Explanation Endpoint will output five features with the highest absolute SHAP Values for a specific prediction. While post-hoc attribution scores may be unstable even though there is a stable underlying model \cite{kelodjou2024shap}, this pilot evaluates them directly \cite{adebayo2018}. A 20 case pilot assesses the impact of six operators: replacement of entities, numbers and dates, removal of support, addition of context and shuffling clauses. This pilot measures four metrics; the mean absolute score change, the fraction of large changes, the top-1 flip rate, and Spearman Rank Correlation Coefficient between the original and perturbed rankings.

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

The framework works within a Next.js front-end and a FastAPI back-end in an open environment using web applications. A request by the browser to access the BFF (Back-End For Front End) provided by Next.js accesses the Next.js model, and it provides tokenized chat data while sending tool requests to the model. The analyze call to the FastAPI is made via the \texttt{/api/ml/*} rewrite. Upon starting up, FastAPI also initializes the EC-XGB model, Platt calibration model, SHAP explainer model, and feature models for NLI, spaCy, and MiniLM. The features loaded correspond to the 35 feature m3 model with a seed value of 42. Section~\ref{sec:results} tests this model against B2, the conventional 26-feature checkpoint trained on HaluEval QA, and against B6 m0, the fixed-hyperparameter 26-feature reference from the same tuning protocol. Claims are separated from the answer and verified with sentence-level NLI against the context, a local passage index, or a web search API. A claim is supported if at least one retained sentence provides an entailment probability of at least 0.5 and not lower than its contradiction probability. A contradiction decides only if no sentence supports the claim and a sentence has a contradiction probability of at least 0.5 that is higher than its entailment probability. A relevance gate filters out sentences that do not contain claim vocabulary, and verdicts are returned for each claim. The card shows these verdicts as follows: a contradicted claim is considered high-risk, an unsupported claim is medium-risk, and a fully supported answer is low-risk. The percentage is the display score. The chat card adjusts this score based on the claim mixture, and the analyze card reports the unadjusted Platt score. As shown in Figures~\ref{fig:pipeline} and~\ref{fig:system}, the process of generating a score for an individual answer consists of calling the appropriate model or function in either the client or server side based on the type of input used for that particular question.

In reference to the previously discussed example of the hallucinated number, the Context states ``five days per decade'' however the Answer states ``ten.'' The raw score generated for this question is 0.999 and after applying the Platt Calibration Model the calibrated score for this question becomes 35.4\%, which is then rounded by the card to 35\% and placed in the medium range (0.35 to 0.60). The Claim View indicates that the number claim is unsupported therefore the Chat Card will report a Medium Risk. Although both Labels indicate the same level of risk in this Example, these Labels were created by separate Rules. The requirement for auditing of the Claim Verdict is defined in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The Requirements File contains references to all required packages; the Split Indices are saved along with the Artifacts; and Audit Scripts verify that Reported Values equal Rebuilt Values at all times. When a rebuild occurs, an Audit will be executed after rebuilding has completed and will result in failure should any discrepancy occur. This paper's primary contribution lies in its Protocol for Training and Evaluation rather than in Developing a New Learner. Three typical modifications are performed by EC-XGB to create a typical XGB model: Monotonic Constraints on Log-Scaled Length Counts; Claim-Level NLI Aggregation to Generate Explanation for Each Claim; and Multi-Source Learning Using Source Indicator. Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates what each change contributes. The claim aggregations are kept for explaining each claim and do not contribute to any additional F1 gain. The seen-task gain comes from the m3 stack that adds the RAGTruth rows and the source indicator, and the two are not disentangled. No call to the LLM judge is required for the risk score. The claim judge provides an additional verification step, but the reported EC-XGB results do not depend on it.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments were done on a Windows laptop equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6~GB GPU, CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128, and 32~GB of RAM. The code is executed using Python 3.12 with scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. Each experimental run can be reproduced owing to stored split indices and centrally set seeds. A random search was done across 30 hyperparameter configurations using grouped five-fold cross-validation. For the seed-42 conventional model, the hyperparameter configuration has a maximum tree depth of 5, a learning rate of 0.05, 300 trees, a subsampling ratio of 0.7, and a column sampling ratio of 0.7. Each seed was independently tuned for the main experiment, and the ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} holds one configuration fixed in all variants so that feature effects are isolated from tuning. Seeds 42, 123, and 456 are repeated where a seed applies, and the single-seed tables are labeled.

\subsection{Dataset}

HaluEval QA \cite{haluEval} has 10{,}000 question-answer pairs, with a knowledge passage, a correct answer, and a hallucinated answer. The data was partitioned using a 70/15/15 ratio based on items, so both answers corresponding to an identical question will remain grouped together. HaluEval is entirely generated by ChatGPT \cite{haluEval,bang-etal-2025-hallulens}, somewhat blurring the difference between factual inaccuracies and hallucinations. RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth} has natural answers with human span annotations, and FaithBench \cite{faithbench} offers hallucinations for contemporary LLM summaries. RAGTruth maintains its official document-level partition: summarization and data-to-text provide training rows and a held-out test set each, and QA provides 5{,}034 calibration rows and a held-out test set. RAGTruth QA is a subset of the full corpus, so its rows are not additive. The pooled RAGTruth test set has three held-out test sets. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the corpora, their sizes, and positive rates. MedHallu pqa\_labeled \cite{pandit2025medhallu} provides medical QA pairs, along with human-annotated gold answers and paired LLM hallucinations. HaluBench \cite{ravi2024lynx} provides a stratified 30\% sample of its DROP, FinanceBench, and covidQA rows, balanced between PASS and FAIL; its HaluEval, RAGTruth, and PubMedQA rows are excluded. Seeded samples of HaluEval tasks are used as task-shift sets. External corpora are never used for fitting or threshold selection.

Leading and trailing whitespace is trimmed, and lexical matching is done in a case-insensitive manner. NER and NLI are performed on the original text, with entity strings converted to lowercase for matching. Missing context is a special case where lexical overlap is zero and NLI probabilities are uniformly distributed. Both count measures (length) for m1 and subsequent versions are log-transformed (log1p), whereas in version m0 and the baseline version, raw counts are applied. Each claim is segmented into no more than six clause-level segments. Conjunctions are separated into separate clause levels. If the context exceeds four sentences, each claim will be mapped to its four most similar sentences according to token overlap and both sides of each pair will be truncated to 400 characters. Both index sets are frozen. An answer from a RAGTruth is classified as positive when it includes at least one annotated hallucination span. Worst-severity annotations are condensed using a worst-severity principle from FaithBench and questions that have a ``Questionable'' annotation are classified as positive. The label scheme used by HaluEval is unique. Both NLI and MiniLM inference were conducted on the reference GPU in fp16 format. Lexical and entity features were calculated on the CPU. Baseline versions that use feature-based logistic standardize their inputs on the training split. Sparse features are used by TF-IDF models and XGBoost does not scale. Values for the source indicator will be set to either 1 for FaithBench or MedHallu or 0 for HaluBench and the task-shift sets. FaithBench, RAGTruth summarization, and data-to-text have no question; therefore, question overlap is 0, and question similarity is defined as the cosine similarity between the answer and the empty string encoding. Licenses and the data availability statement are in the Declarations.

\subsection{Baselines and Experimental Procedure}

Twelve models are used in the evaluation. The heuristic assigns risk as one minus lexical overlap, and the length-only logistic control uses two log-scaled length counts. The classical models include logistic regression and a 300-tree random forest. The conventional XGBoost model \cite{xgboost} uses all 26 base features. Three more controls investigate the feature design: NLI-only, answer-only TF-IDF, and full-input TF-IDF. The set is completed by the majority classifier, fine-tuned RoBERTa-base \cite{liu2019roberta}, and the light reimplementation of the ensemble of Sundaragiri et al.\ \cite{ijert}; the two judges are presented in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Internal-state detectors are beyond the scope of the black-box approach \cite{inside2024}. The RoBERTa baseline processes up to 256 tokens and trains for three epochs. The majority baseline predicts the negative class, and, therefore, probability metrics are not applicable. The external ensemble uses five features (ROUGE-L, MiniLM cosine similarity, two NLI probabilities, answer length) in the logistic regression model trained on 14{,}000 training samples; the selected threshold of 0.55 gives F1 = 0.9514, so the fixed 0.5 threshold does not affect the comparison. EC-XGB's display calibrator is fit on the RAGTruth QA split, and the in-domain Platt calibrator is fit on the validation set. Metrics are rounded to four decimals and rates to one. Significance uses McNemar's test on the seed-42 predictions, 2,000 two-level bootstrap resamples, and Wilcoxon tests across seeds. No question appears in both training and evaluation. The calibration quality is estimated on raw, Platt, and isotonic scores; see Table~\ref{tab:protocols} for definitions of P0, P1, and P2; the test rows are the same for all protocols.


The deployed system uses P1, and P2 is its thresholded version. Only P0 is a zero-shot model; P1 and P2 read labels from the target calibration split, which is disjoint from the test split. Global and local explanations are provided by the SHAP \cite{shap} TreeExplainers. Reliability is tested with two checks: rank agreement of the SHAP importances of the m3 and B2 models and a perturbation pilot using six operators. The feature latency is estimated on 200 HaluEval samples on the reference laptop. The 900 RAGTruth QA rows are scored by two judges, GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen 3.5 4B, according to the LLM-as-a-judge protocol \cite{zheng2023judge}; the costs are derived from the token counts.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Two types of metrics are provided here. The initial collection of data presents threshold-related measures for assessing a determination that was made based upon a fixed cut-off point. The second collection provides threshold-free measures for determining how good the model's ability is for response ordering. Both are needed since the model can have high ranking performance and poor decision performance if the cut-off point is fixed. Precision is the proportion of flagged responses that were hallucinated. Recall is the proportion of hallucinated responses detected by the system, and F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. False-positive rate (FPR) is the proportion of correct responses that get flagged and serves as an operating constraint under distributional shift. True positive (TP) and true negative (TN) indicate the correctly classified instances, and false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) are a correct answer flagged as hallucinated and a hallucinated answer that is not flagged, respectively. The flag rate is the proportion of responses exceeding the decision threshold; if it approaches 100\%, then every response reaches a human reviewer.
\begin{gather}
  \text{Precision }(P) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
  \text{Recall }(R) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
  F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}, \\
  \text{False-positive rate }(\mathrm{FPR}) = \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{TN}}, \qquad
  \text{Flag rate} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}_i \ge \tau].
\end{gather}

The second set includes ranking, calibration, and paired error metrics. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) is equal to the probability that a random hallucinated answer has a higher score than a random correct answer, with 0.5 meaning chance level. Area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) is more informative when the positive class is rare, and RAGTruth QA has a positive rate of 17.8\%. Thus, PR-AUC is also reported. The Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) uses all four cells of the confusion matrix and has values ranging from $-1$ to $1$, with zero being the value of random performance. The Wilson interval gives a 95\% confidence range for a proportion and is used for the P2 recall and FPR when the number of observations is small.
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{AUROC} = \int_{0}^{1} \mathrm{TPR}(u)\, d\,\mathrm{FPR}(u) = P(s^{+} > s^{-}), \\
  \mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} P(r)\, dr, \qquad
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}\cdot\mathrm{TN} - \mathrm{FP}\cdot\mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}, \\
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\end{gather}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:models} shows the HaluEval evaluation results for eleven baselines and EC-XGB averaged over three seeds where relevant. EC-XGB outperforms the conventional model in F1 by 0.0009, an improvement smaller than the width of the conventional seed-42 bootstrap confidence interval [0.9812, 0.9895], such that the benchmark cannot provide a definite order. There appears to be an equivalent pattern within the ranking columns: EC-XGB ranked highest among feature-based models in both AUROC and PR-AUC. The traditional model, along with random forest models, rank next closest to EC-XGB, and RoBERTa is ranked just above them. However, when looking at calibration metrics, the traditional model has the lowest expected calibration error (ECE), and both EC-XGB and RoBERTa have the lowest Brier score. Weak control sets include NLI-only and full-input TF-IDF. Answer-only TF-IDF is far stronger than either of these. Adding both question and context decreases performance. The reduced external ensemble lags behind EC-XGB in terms of F1 by 0.0337 [0.0262, 0.0419] and AUROC by 0.0135 [0.0098, 0.0177]; however, the external ensemble is trained on fewer rows, using a smaller number of features, so the difference can be considered purely descriptive. Figure~\ref{fig:rocpr} illustrates the curves that provide the basis for Table~\ref{tab:models}; the curves represent results from seed 42, whereas the tabular values were produced by taking three-seed averages.

The XGBoost margin relative to the random forest is 0.0004 F1, McNemar $p = 0.502$, meaning that there is no statistically significant difference. The margin over logistic regression is 0.0187 F1 ($p = 1.86\times10^{-10}$), and over the heuristic is 0.0495 ($p = 2.28\times10^{-30}$). A source-only encoder wins in-domain on F1 (0.9912 vs 0.9855) but is near chance on each RAGTruth task (AUROC 0.458 on summarization and 0.491 on data-to-text, compared to 0.736 and 0.770 for EC-XGB on those tasks). Pooling gives 0.337 for the encoder and 0.582 for EC-XGB, as the encoder's pooled score is a blend of between-task score shifts and within-task ranking. An adapted encoder on the same multi-source rows reaches 0.694 on the pooled RAGTruth test set, beating EC-XGB's 0.582; hence, the seen-task gain is not specific to the feature model. Neither model generalizes to unseen QA (0.490 vs 0.549), and FaithBench is worse for both (0.434 vs 0.594). Adaptation helps any model on seen tasks; the feature model preserves its relative ordering on unseen tasks.

\subsection{Calibration, Shift, and External Validity}

Values of ECE for the raw, Platt-scaled, and isotonic variants of the conventional model are 0.0045, 0.0091, and 0.0070, respectively, on HaluEval, demonstrating that calibration does not help to decrease ECE in this particular example. Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} explains this fact. On HaluEval, the points at the extreme ends lie on the diagonal, and there are only a few distinct values for the mid-score range; therefore, a small number of stable bins are used. FaithBench is aggregated because its scores concentrate near 1.0. The Platt and isotonic curves overlap the raw curve at both ends. The m3 raw score is already well calibrated in-domain; hence, the ECE value is quite low at 0.0078, which leaves little room for further calibration on HaluEval. Platt scaling applies a two-parameter logistic transformation where $s$ (the raw score in the figures) is transformed into a calibrated probability,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p} = \sigma(a s + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
  \label{eq:platt}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the calibration slope and $b$ is the intercept. A slope below one means the raw score is overconfident, and a slope near one means the scale is already usable. The Brier skill score (BSS) compares the calibrated score with a constant predictor at the evaluation prevalence,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{prior}}}.
  \label{eq:bss}
\end{equation}
A positive BSS means the score beats the prior, and a non-positive BSS means it does not.

Table~\ref{tab:conformal} summarizes the coverage. Split conformal prediction offers a coverage guarantee for the score. The nonconformity score for label $y$ is $s(x,y)=1-\hat{p}_y(x)$, where $\hat{p}_1=\hat{p}$ and $\hat{p}_0=1-\hat{p}$. The calibration quantile and the prediction set are
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q} = s_{(k)}, \quad k = \left\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}\},
  \label{eq:conformal}
\end{equation}
where $s_{(1)} \le \cdots \le s_{(n)}$ are the ordered calibration scores; this finite-sample order statistic yields coverage of at least $1-\alpha$ for the prediction set. A class-conditional version replaces the single quantile with one quantile per label,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q}_y = s_{y,(k_y)}, \quad k_y = \left\lceil (n_y+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}_y\},
  \label{eq:conformal_class}
\end{equation}
where $s_{y,(1)} \le \cdots \le s_{y,(n_y)}$ are the ordered scores in class $y$ and $n_y$ is the number of calibration rows with label $y$.
The main run fits the Platt map and the quantile on the same 5{,}034 rows; thus, its coverage serves as a construction check and gives 0.940 and 0.967 coverage on QA and 0.900 and 0.950 on the calibration rows. A disjoint sensitivity fits Platt on 2{,}514 rows and the quantile on 2{,}520 rows. In this case, on the split, the 900 QA test rows achieved 0.944 coverage at 90\%; this demonstrates that the marginal guarantee also held true. Nevertheless, the marginal guarantee hides the class split: positive coverage is 0.685, while negative coverage is 1.000. A class conditional quantile fit to the full calibration rows raised positive coverage to 0.887 which is still slightly under nominal coverage. The coverage of FaithBench fell to 0.320 and positive coverage to 0.003, due to the fact that a Platt map fit to QA sent nearly every FaithBench score into the negative region. Therefore conformal prediction only verifies the marginal rate if both the calibrator and quantile are fit to disjoint rows; additionally it does not transfer between tasks. Multicalibration by answer length and number of claims demonstrate where error lies within the average ECE. On the 900 QA test rows, the target Platt score had an overall ECE of 0.125. The worst length bin is 60 words or fewer (n=154), with ECE 0.275; the worst claim-count bin is a single claim (n=36), with ECE 0.346. The Brier score does not follow the ECE ranking, so averaging is not a safe summary for short or one-claim answers. FaithBench is not improved by the QA calibrator: its target-Platt ECE is 0.629 against 0.453 for the raw score.

The pair accuracy over $M$ pairs is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PA} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\left[\hat{p}(a_m^{+}) > \hat{p}(a_m^{-})\right],

\end{equation}
where $a_m^{+}$ is the hallucinated answer and $a_m^{-}$ the grounded answer. The conventional m0 model reaches 82.2\% and 0.781. The length-only baseline is inverted, at 20.0\% and 0.260, because grounded MedHallu answers are longer than the paired hallucinations (39.7 vs 27.8 words), the opposite of HaluEval (2.2 vs 11.0). The ranking transferred over to the scale did not: m3 flagged every single row in terms of all positive responses for that row and had an F1 of 0.667 (all-positive) and an ECE of 0.493. The source indicator was a one for MedHallu and a zero for HaluBench. A source-zero sensitivity on MedHallu resulted in no greater than a 0.003 increase or decrease in the pair accuracy as well as AUROC. HaluBench is a 30\% stratified random sampling of the data. HaluBench's total AUROC value is 0.600 and is broken down into the following values: covidQA 0.833, DROP 0.513, and FinanceBench 0.527. The model transfers to the biomedical subset, however, it performs at random when tested on the other two subsets. The length-only baseline is at 0.514 overall, so 0.600 is not a length artifact. All of the data used for evaluating the performance of the models were designed to have equal amounts of each class as possible (i.e., fifty-fifty), so F1 and the flag rate will be dependent upon the prevalence of the classes and will not represent how well a model would perform if deployed in its normal operating environment. Since HaluBench is unpaired there is no way to evaluate pair accuracy. Additionally, since only one training set is available to train the model, the length-only baseline is fit only to the HaluEval training set.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Statistical tests use the paired fixed-hyperparameter predictions and 2{,}000 two-level bootstrap draws, where groups are resampled within each seed and then seeds are resampled. McNemar's test compares the discordant counts $b$ and $c$ on the paired seed-42 rows, where $b$ is the number of rows where the first model is right and the second is wrong, and $c$ is the reverse; the statistic uses a continuity correction. Holm's method controls the family-wise error rate across the four baseline comparisons. The 95\% interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the paired bootstrap deltas. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares the two models across the three seeds. McNemar's results are presented in Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar}. There were no statistical differences (i.e., $p = 0.45$) when comparing the performance of EC-XGB under the deployed checkpoints against the conventional method; the fixed-hp ablation shows $p = 0.789$ on the same test rows. The fixed-hp m0-vs-m3 comparison is the primary test, and it is not part of the Holm family. The family consists of four baseline comparisons against the conventional method. The test uses the single configuration defined in Table~\ref{tab:ec}. The random forest shows no significant difference from the conventional method ($p = 0.502$). Logistic regression and the heuristic significantly underperform ($p = 1.86\times10^{-10}$ and $p = 2.28\times10^{-30}$), with both surviving the Holm correction. When evaluating m0 against m3 with fixed hp to determine if tuning provided an improvement over a non-tuning build, no statistically significant differences were identified (minimum $p$-value = 0.25), as the Wilcoxon tests included a maximum of three seed values. Table~\ref{tab:stats} illustrates the results from a two-level bootstrap analysis. On the HaluEval dataset, the F1 delta for m3 minus m0 is $+$0.0006, with CI [$-$0.0010, 0.0021], which encompasses zero, whereas the AUROC delta is $+$0.0007, with CI [0.0002, 0.0012], which excludes zero by a small margin. The F1 deltas for m2 minus m1 and m3 minus m1 are $-$0.0004 (CI [$-$0.0020, 0.0012]) and $+$0.0001 (CI [$-$0.0015, 0.0018]). Therefore, the inclusion of the additional claim features did not result in an overall increase in F1. The deltas for AUROC were $+$0.0009 and $+$0.0010 respectively; however, each interval was positive.

In the held-out RAGTruth QA experiment, the m3$-$m0 F1 difference is $+$0.0076 [0.0045, 0.0109], while the AUROC difference is $+$0.0017 [$-$0.0270, 0.0291], which includes zero. As such, the majority of the F1 improvement resulted from the ability to identify nearly all rows rather than improving their placement or order. In addition, based upon the pooled seen task experiment, the F1 differential was $+$0.0451 [0.0348, 0.0554] and the AUROC differential was $+$0.0962 [0.0644, 0.1295]. All of the paired fixed-minus-tuned differences contained zero, which indicates that this rebuild had no effect on those results. In HaluEval, the groups are item pairs (1{,}500); in RAGTruth QA, there are 150 source groups; and in the pooled split, there are 450 groups. The QA AUROC difference includes zero, suggesting that the held-out transfer result is unresolved, and the pooled row measures adaptation to seen tasks.

\subsection{Explanation, Efficiency, and Judges}

The explanation was based on a pre-trained fixed-hyperparameter m3 ``seed-42'' checkpoint. Figure~\ref{fig:shapimp} shows the first fifteen ranked features by average absolute SHAP values over 1{,}000 stratified HaluEval test rows. Context overlap drove most of the variance in the predictions, then word count, followed by Question-Answer Cosine Similarity, Character Count, and Question-Answer Jaccard Similarity. The Claim-Contradiction Mean appeared at rank eight. The top ten features cover 77.9\% of the attributions, and exact TreeSHAP matches the model margin up to $9.2\times10^{-6}$. These attribution results were obtained for the Raw Margin, and not the Display Score. The m3 rankings are highly correlated with the frozen B2 rankings (Spearman's $\rho = 0.878$, Kendall's $\tau = 0.730$); therefore, the claim features refine the explanation, rather than replace the original drivers. Cross-seed Spearman correlation is 0.972 and 0.950; nine of the top ten features are shared, and the top five features are identical. The explanation is resilient to the choice of the seed but not to content editing. Table~\ref{tab:perturb} presents a 20-case perturbation pilot: content edits move the score, structural edits barely shift it, and the top-1 feature is flipped in 54.5\% of entity perturbations and none of the other operators. The entity flipping rate is higher than in B2, so the explanation is less stable when the answer entity changes; the per-operator sample size is small. A Structured AI-Assisted Audit of 26 Error-Enriched Cases Recalculated Under m3 evaluated each explanation for consistency with Answer-Context Evidence, rather than the Dataset Label. Thirteen explanations were Implausible, Three Uncertain, and Ten Plausible, so these explanations are used as Diagnostic Tools, not Faithful Attributions. False positives carry the length confound, and false negatives frequently rely on entities repeated in the context. Since the sample was enriched for errors, the percentages reflect the Audit Results, not the test set. The labels are author-checked pre-annotations: LLM tools assisted the annotation, and the authors reviewed and verified the final labels. Agreement with the pre-annotations was 0.98 for explanation, 0.94 for taxonomy, and 0.88 for claims. Cohen's $\kappa$ was 0.965, 0.923, and 0.788; the authors changed 2\%, 6\%, and 12\% of rows, and the blind Fleiss $\kappa$ was 0.940, 0.868, and 0.761.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Discussion}

The m3 seed-42 model made 43 mistakes (see Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}), giving it a 1.4\% mistake rate. WP5 has included 7 of 8 false positives, as well as 12 of 35 false negatives in order to create coverage with the code book. A separate 50-instance worksheet, evaluated by one author, identified 31 artifact instances and 19 content instances that could fit within 12 different categories. These records are about the instances being reviewed, not the entire test set of 3{,}000 rows. Table~\ref{tab:length} shows that, for the conventional B2 model, length is essentially a perfect indicator on the HaluEval benchmark: false positives tend to occur in the five-to-eight length bin and false negatives tend to occur in the shorter bins, although there are only two negative rows in the 17+ bin. Both controls affect the length distribution. First, the reweighting control ensures equal weighting across six length bins, each containing 247 instances. Second, the length-matching control matches one positive instance and one negative per each length value. Reweighting results in an improvement of 0.039 for F1 and 0.023 for AUROC in EC-XGB in contrast with length-only regression, which is already competitive on its own (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Length matching gives 79 pairs (158 instances) over 1{,}000 bootstraps. EC-XGB holds AUROC of 0.956 whereas the length-only baseline falls to 0.521. The gap in AUROC between these two models is 0.435 (the bootstrap mean is 0.436 with 95\% confidence interval of [0.351, 0.522]), and the gap in F1 is 0.431 (the bootstrap mean is 0.440 with 95\% confidence interval of [0.334, 0.552]).

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The current paper tests EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent, 35-feature model, as a hallucination risk score and how its calibration and ranking change under domain and task shifts. The model's ranking is still intact after the corpus has changed and allows the score to rank answers for review. Moreover, its per-claim verdicts provide a reviewer with the explanation of each flagged instance. On the other hand, its probability is not so resilient; the display score requires labeled target rows, and target prevalence dictates the score's value. As a result, EC-XGB operates as a domain-specific screening score, not a general-purpose detector. The upcoming assessment considers adaptation to an unseen question-answering domain under prevalence shift. The seen-task source ablation will separate RAGTruth rows from the source indicator.
