\begin{abstract}
  Large language models answer fluently, but some answers are not supported by the evidence. Detectors that rely on a large judge model, an encoder, or model internals are costly and rarely report calibration under domain or task shift. Feature-based detectors are cheaper, but their calibration and sensitivity to answer length are seldom measured together. This study asks whether a simple feature model can estimate hallucination risk and how its ranking and calibration change under shift. We present EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent XGBoost model that combines base features, claim-level natural-language-inference aggregates, and a source indicator. The model is trained on HaluEval QA and RAGTruth non-QA rows with grouped splits and three seeds, and evaluated with fixed-hyperparameter ablations, source and target calibration, conformal prediction, SHAP stability, a length-only control, and judge stress tests. A feature model is cheap and explainable, and its inputs can be inspected directly, which makes it a practical option for routine screening. On HaluEval QA the fixed-hyperparameter model reaches F1 0.985 and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) 0.999, but lexical overlap and length carry that score. On MedHallu it ranks the hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of pairs, but it flags every row and is badly calibrated (expected calibration error 0.493). On RAGTruth QA it stays near chance. Target calibration lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125 but flags no target rows at the default threshold, and split conformal reaches 0.944 coverage at 90\% with only 0.685 positive coverage. Dialogue is flagged almost entirely, summarization ranks below a length-only model, and a GPT judge beats the feature model on natural QA. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific risk score, not a general-purpose detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Black-box detectors score a candidate answer from its text and context. SelfCheckGPT samples several answers and checks whether they agree \cite{selfcheck}. Luna replaces the large judge with a smaller evaluation model \cite{luna}. Semantic entropy clusters sampled answers by meaning \cite{farquhar2024semantic}. A hybrid framework combines frozen encoder features with lightweight classifiers \cite{ieeeTai}. Judges carry high computational costs, and encoder models are hard to interpret. Two recent reviews group detectors into feature-based, encoder-based, and hybrid approaches \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey}. Both name evaluation and reliability as open problems, and a third review reaches the same conclusion about reliability \cite{alansari2026survey}.

HaluAgent showed that small models can select tools, but it needs multiple calls per answer \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. proposed a calibrated multi-score detector with a cost analysis, but it relies on LLM scoring \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. combined retrieval, NLI, and XGBoost, but report only classification metrics \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME attributions to each word, but did not measure their reliability \cite{multimedia}. Deng et al. found that detectors degrade outside their training domain, but focus on multi-turn dialogue \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench shows that modern LLMs still produce summarization hallucinations, but does not measure detector calibration \cite{faithbench}.

The existing literature leaves four issues unaddressed. Calibration is infrequently reported for lightweight detectors, explanation quality is rarely measured, cross-domain behavior is rarely tested, and error patterns are rarely analyzed case by case. The conventional feature-based baseline also has limits. It depends on answer length, it has no claim-level evidence, it trains on HaluEval alone with a source-fitted calibrator, and its probability does not transfer to a new corpus \cite{ovadia2019}. HaluEval is also saturated. The gap is a risk score that keeps its ranking and probability when the corpus changes. This paper addresses these gaps with a feature-based model that scores the output of a black-box LLM. It takes a query $q$, a reference context $c$, and a candidate answer $a$, and outputs $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$. EC-XGB combines 26 base features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator in a tuned XGBoost classifier. Platt calibration maps the score to a probability, SHAP explains feature contributions, and grouped splits with length and overlap controls reduce shortcut solutions. Source calibration is fitted on validation rows only, and the target-calibration experiments measure the reliability problem. The log transform compresses the length scale but does not remove the cue. The claim-level aggregates support per-claim explanations, and the measured seen-task gain comes from adding multi-source rows and the source indicator, not from the claim aggregates alone. Transfer limits are established on RAGTruth and FaithBench. The evaluation adds MedHallu \cite{pandit2025medhallu}, a HaluBench subset \cite{ravi2024lynx} with no training overlap, the HaluEval task shifts, conformal prediction, and judge stress tests. It reports ranking, calibration, and paired errors rather than F1 alone. The added evidence separates ranking from calibration: the model transfers its ranking to medical pairs but not its probability, and it fails under a task shift.

HaluEval QA provides 10{,}000 question pairs, each with a correct and a hallucinated answer, for training and in-domain testing. RAGTruth provides natural responses for adaptation and recalibration. The target use case is question answering with a reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. The fixed-hyperparameter EC-XGB stack (m3) reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986 on HaluEval QA. RAGTruth shows the same pattern: the flag rate falls on the tasks seen in training, while held-out QA stays near chance. On MedHallu the same model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 pairs but flags every row. On HaluEval dialogue it flags almost every row, and on HaluEval summarization it ranks below a length-only model. Target calibration on 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, but it does not change the ranking. One answer takes 127.5~ms on GPU (2.00~s on CPU) at about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

This section describes the conventional feature-based detector and the criteria used to evaluate it. A hallucination is an answer that the reference context does not support, or that the context contradicts. Each row is a triple $(q_i,c_i,a_i)$ of question, reference context, and candidate answer, with label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. A detector is usually scored with precision, recall, F1, or the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) on labeled rows. A deployed detector faces two more demands. Calibration requires the predicted risk to match the observed frequency, and expected calibration error (ECE) summarizes the gap across confidence bins \cite{guo}. Explanation requires the warning to name the features that contributed to it, and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) provide that for a tabular model \cite{shap}. This work measures both properties on the black-box model.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 base features in Table~\ref{tab:features}, trained on HaluEval QA. XGBoost models nonlinear interactions and gives exact TreeSHAP values, which the explanation analysis needs. A published feature-based detector uses the same learner with retrieval and NLI features \cite{ijert}, so this model is the natural baseline. Trees are added one at a time, and each tree fits the current gradient and curvature of the loss. A row enters at the root of each tree, follows the split conditions to one leaf per tree, and the leaf weights are summed into a raw margin. A logistic link maps the raw margin to a probability.

Let $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{26}$ be the feature vector of row $i$, $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label, and $n$ the number of training rows. The model sums $K$ trees into a raw margin, $z_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)$. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, z_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $\phi = \{f_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ collects the trees, $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves and leaf-weight vector $w_k \in \mathbb{R}^{T_k}$, $\gamma$ is the penalty per leaf, and $\lambda$ is an L2 weight penalty. The loss $l(y_i,z_i)$ is the logistic loss on the raw margin. The probability is the sigmoid of the raw margin, $\hat{p}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$.

At boosting step $t$, a new tree $f_t$ with $T$ leaves and leaf weights $w_1,\dots,w_T$ is added, and the objective is approximated, up to a constant, by a second-order expansion around the current raw margin,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $g_i = \partial l(y_i,z)/\partial z$ and $h_i = \partial^2 l(y_i,z)/\partial z^2$ are evaluated at $z_i^{(t-1)}$, and $f_t(x_i) = w_{q(x_i)}$ is the leaf weight that row $i$ reaches, with $q(x_i)$ the leaf index. For the logistic loss, $g_i = \hat{p}_i - y_i$ and $h_i = \hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)$.

For a fixed tree structure, the optimal weight of leaf $j$ is
\begin{equation}
  w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda},
\end{equation}
where $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$ and $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$ are the sums over the rows $I_j$ that reach leaf $j$. The gain of a split is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda} \right] - \gamma,
\end{equation}
with $G_L$ and $H_L$ for the left child and $G_R$ and $H_R$ for the right child. A split is kept only when the gain is positive. The tree also stops at the maximum depth or when a leaf has fewer than the minimum child weight.

Depth, learning rate, tree count, and the row and column subsampling rates are tuned on the 14{,}000 training rows. Each seed runs its own 30-configuration search with stratified group five-fold cross-validation, and the grouping keeps both answers to the same question in one fold. The seed-42 values are in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}, and the per-seed values are in the repository. Each seed trains on the 14{,}000 rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows (30 rounds). The 300-tree setting is the tuned upper bound, and seed 42 stops at 209 trees. The headline comparison uses the uncalibrated probability at a fixed 0.5 threshold on the 3{,}000 test rows. Platt and isotonic maps are fitted on the same validation rows for the calibration study. The source-only P0 threshold is selected on those rows at a 5\% false-positive budget. The test rows are never used for tuning, early stopping, calibration, or threshold selection. The final step returns the mean over the three seeds. The search leaves $\gamma$, $\lambda$, and the minimum child weight at their XGBoost defaults. Algorithm~\ref{alg:baseline} summarizes the tuning, training, and calibration steps.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and evaluation)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, test metrics, and Platt and isotonic maps
    \State extract the 26 base features for every row
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State tune XGBoost on the 14{,}000 training rows with 30 random configurations and stratified group 5-fold cross-validation
    \State train the classifier on the same rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows
    \State evaluate the raw score on the 3{,}000 test rows at the fixed 0.5 threshold
    \State fit Platt and isotonic maps on the validation rows for the calibration study
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Working Principle}

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{revision/architecture-flow-large.png}
  \caption{End-to-end pipeline of EC-XGB.}

The task is to estimate $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$ for a question $q$, reference context $c$ (if available), and a candidate answer $a$ from a black-box LLM. The target is not truthfulness in general but evidence consistency: whether $a$ follows from $c$ or contradicts it, and the output is a probability that a decision threshold turns into a flag. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline that computes this probability. The system takes the three inputs and extracts 35 features: 26 base features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator. EC-XGB maps the feature vector to a raw score, and a Platt calibrator fitted on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows maps the score to the displayed probability. The system reports the risk score, per-claim verdicts, and SHAP contributions. The lower band shows the deployment path: a Next.js front end calls a FastAPI service that loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. The rightmost block lists the evaluation corpora. HaluEval measures in-domain performance, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text measure adaptation to task types seen in training, and RAGTruth QA and FaithBench are held out.

\subsection{Features}

The model uses 26 base features in seven groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and eight claim-level aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:claim}). Natural language inference (NLI) probabilities come from a DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder \cite{deberta} trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}, semantic similarity uses MiniLM embeddings \cite{sbert,minilm}, and entities come from spaCy named entity recognition (NER) \cite{spacy}. Length is included because HaluEval labels longer answers as hallucinated more often, so it is a strong but fragile signal. Lexical overlap captures copying and is the strongest SHAP feature, but it cannot detect a fluent paraphrase that contradicts the evidence. A novel entity is a useful warning sign, although a correct answer can introduce a new name. The NLI features are the only group that represents logical support rather than surface overlap. A changed number is a common and easy-to-detect hallucination. Hedging words are weak on their own but separate a cautious correct answer from a confident wrong one. Semantic similarity catches paraphrase and topic drift that lexical overlap misses. The eight claim aggregates repeat the NLI signals at the level of atomic claims, which is the level a reviewer checks. RAGChecker also uses entailment-based claim metrics \cite{ru2024ragchecker}.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB changes the conventional setup in its feature treatment and its training rows. The character and word counts are log-scaled, and monotone constraints are placed on the evidence features. The constraints are negative on context overlap, context Jaccard, context entailment, context cosine, claim minimum and mean entailment, and the supported-claim ratio, and positive on context contradiction, novel-entity ratio, novel numbers, claim maximum and mean contradiction, and the contradicted-claim ratio. All other features are unconstrained. The eight claim features in Table~\ref{tab:claim} are added: seven NLI aggregates and a claim count. They use the same NLI scorer and support-first rule as the verification layer. A claim is supported when any retained sentence has an entailment probability of at least 0.5 and at least as high as its contradiction probability. It is contradicted when no sentence supports it and a sentence has a contradiction probability of at least 0.5 and above its entailment probability. Otherwise it is unsupported.

Consider an answer with $K$ claims. Let $C(k)$ be the maximum contradiction probability of claim $k$ over its selected context sentences, $E(k)$ the maximum entailment probability, and $v_k$ its verdict. The aggregate vector is
\begin{equation}
  \psi(x) = [\max_k C(k), \overline{C}, r_{\text{con}}, r_{\text{sup}}, r_{\text{unsup}}, \min_k E(k), \overline{E}, K],
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  r_{\text{con}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{contradicted}], \quad
  r_{\text{sup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{supported}], \quad
  r_{\text{unsup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{unsupported}].
\end{equation}
Here $x=(q,c,a)$ is the input triplet, and $\overline{C}$ and $\overline{E}$ are the means of $C(k)$ and $E(k)$ over the claims. For $K>0$, the three ratios sum to one. The model input has 35 dimensions: the 26 base features (two of them log-scaled), the eight claim features, and one source indicator.

The training set adds the RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text rows. The ablation ladder starts from m0, the 26-feature conventional reference, and adds log-scaled length counts with monotone constraints at m1, the eight claim features at m2 (34 features), and the RAGTruth non-QA rows with the source indicator at m3 (35 features). A binary source indicator is one for those rows and zero for HaluEval, so the classifier can separate the two sources. The classifier never sees RAGTruth QA rows, so its QA ranking is zero-shot. The display calibrator does see labeled QA rows below, so the display score is not zero-shot. The 35-feature combination is called EC-XGB, and seed 42 is the deployed checkpoint while the other seeds are a stability check. The ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that the claim block adds no aggregate F1 gain, and the claim count $K$ is kept because an answer with many claims is harder to verify. Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecxgb} lists the procedure: steps 1 and 2 build the feature matrix, and steps 3 to 9 repeat the fit for the three seeds. Each repetition builds the 35-feature input, trains on HaluEval plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, fits the display calibrator on the 5{,}034 QA calibration rows, and selects the source and target thresholds.



\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require HaluEval QA training and validation rows, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text training rows, RAGTruth QA calibration rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}=\{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure seed-42 model and display calibrator, source and target thresholds, per-seed metrics
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim features for every row
    \State log-scale the character and word counts, and attach the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input, with the source indicator at zero for HaluEval and one for RAGTruth
    \State train XGBoost on the HaluEval rows plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of classifier training
    \State fit the Platt display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows
    \State select the source threshold on the HaluEval validation split
    \State select the target threshold on the QA calibration rows at a 5\% false-positive budget
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the seed-42 model and calibrator, both thresholds, and the per-seed metrics
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

Source calibration uses the HaluEval validation split. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid $p(x) = \sigma(A f(x) + B)$ to the uncalibrated probability $f(x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are fit by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing step function $g$ so that $p(x) = g(f(x))$ \cite{isotonic}. Both are compared with the uncalibrated probability on the HaluEval test split. The comparison reports expected calibration error over 10 equal-width bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the empirical positive rate and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$, and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. Post-hoc calibration degrades when the target distribution moves \cite{gasparin2024distance}.

The second protocol fits the calibrator on labeled target rows and evaluates it on a disjoint target test split. The deployed EC-XGB display score uses a Platt calibrator fitted on the 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows. Those rows are scored with the source indicator set to one, and the API uses the same value for every request. This target calibration needs labeled target rows and is not zero-shot. The source-only protocol (P0 in Table~\ref{tab:protocols}) fits both the calibrator and the threshold on the HaluEval validation split. The deployed calibrator is Platt. An earlier deployment fit compared Platt and isotonic on the test split, and Section~\ref{sec:results} records that choice.

A TreeExplainer computes exact SHAP values \cite{shap} for the raw XGBoost model. Global importance is the mean absolute SHAP value over a stratified test sample, and the explanation endpoint returns the five features with the largest absolute value for one prediction. Post-hoc attributions can be unstable even when the model is fixed \cite{kelodjou2024shap}, so the pilot evaluates them directly \cite{adebayo2018}. A 20-case pilot applies six operators: entity, number, and date replacement, support removal, context insertion, and clause shuffling. It reports the mean absolute score change, the fraction of large changes, the top-1 flip rate, and the Spearman rank correlation between the original and perturbed rankings.

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

The method is deployed in a web application with a Next.js front end and a FastAPI backend. The API loads the EC-XGB model, the Platt calibrator, the SHAP explainer, and the NLI, spaCy, and MiniLM feature models at startup. The deployed checkpoint is the 35-feature m3 model with seed 42. Section~\ref{sec:results} compares it with B2, the conventional 26-feature checkpoint tuned on HaluEval QA, and with B6 m0, the fixed-hyperparameter 26-feature reference from the same tuning protocol that lacks the EC-XGB changes. Claims are split from the answer and checked with sentence-level NLI against the provided context, a local passage index, or a web search API. A claim is supported when any retained sentence has an entailment probability of at least 0.5 and at least as high as its contradiction probability. A contradiction decides only when no sentence supports the claim. A relevance gate drops sentences with no claim vocabulary, and the verdicts are shown per claim.

The contribution is the training and evaluation protocol, not a new learner. EC-XGB combines three standard changes: log-scaled length counts with monotone constraints, claim-level NLI aggregates for explanation, and multi-source training with a source indicator. Section~\ref{sec:results} measures what each change contributes. The claim aggregates are kept for per-claim explanation and do not add an aggregate F1 gain. The seen-task gain comes from the m3 stack that adds the RAGTruth rows and the source indicator, and the two are not separated. The risk score needs no LLM judge calls. The optional claim judge is a separate verification step, and the reported EC-XGB results do not use it.



\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments were run on a Windows laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU, CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128, and 32\,GB of RAM. The code runs on Python 3.12 with scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. Each run can be reproduced because of saved split indices and centralized seeds. A randomized search was performed over 30 hyperparameter configurations with grouped five-fold cross-validation. For the seed-42 conventional model, the configuration has a maximum tree depth of 5, a learning rate of 0.05, 300 trees, a subsampling ratio of 0.7, and a column sampling ratio of 0.7. Each seed is tuned separately for the main comparison, and the component ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} holds one configuration fixed across variants so that the feature effects are not confounded with tuning. Seeds 42, 123, and 456 are repeated where a seed applies; the single-seed tables are labeled.

\subsection{Dataset}

HaluEval QA \cite{haluEval} provides 10{,}000 question pairs, each with a knowledge passage, a correct answer, and a hallucinated answer. The split is 70/15/15 by item, so both answers to a question stay together. HaluEval is entirely ChatGPT-generated \cite{haluEval,bang-etal-2025-hallulens}, so the boundary between a factual error and a hallucination is not always clean. RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth} supplies natural responses with human span annotations, and FaithBench \cite{faithbench} supplies modern-LLM summarization hallucinations. RAGTruth keeps its official document-level split: summarization and data-to-text supply the training rows and a held-out test set each, and QA supplies 5{,}034 calibration rows and a held-out test set. RAGTruth QA is a subset of the full corpus, so its rows are not additive. The three held-out test splits form the pooled RAGTruth test set. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the corpora, sizes, and positive rates. MedHallu pqa\_labeled \cite{pandit2025medhallu} adds medical QA pairs with human-annotated gold answers and paired LLM-written hallucinations. HaluBench \cite{ravi2024lynx} contributes a stratified 30\% sample of its DROP, FinanceBench, and covidQA rows, balanced across PASS and FAIL; its HaluEval, RAGTruth, and PubMedQA rows are excluded. A seeded sample from each HaluEval task forms the task-shift sets. These external corpora are never used for fitting or threshold selection.

Leading and trailing whitespace is trimmed, and lexical matching is case-folded. NER and NLI read the original text, and entity strings are lowercased for matching. In m1 and later variants, the two length counts are log1p-transformed; m0 and the conventional model keep raw counts. Claims are split into at most six clause-level segments, with conjunctions broken into separate clauses. When the context has more than four sentences, each claim is matched to its four best sentences by token overlap, and each side of a pair is truncated to 400 characters. Both index sets are frozen. \subsection{Baselines and Experimental Procedure}

Twelve models are tested. The heuristic sets risk to one minus lexical overlap, and a length-only logistic control uses the two log-scaled length counts. Logistic regression and a 300-tree random forest are the classical comparators. The conventional XGBoost model \cite{xgboost} uses the 26 base features. Three further controls isolate the feature design: NLI-only, answer-only TF-IDF, and full-input TF-IDF. A majority classifier, a fine-tuned RoBERTa-base \cite{liu2019roberta}, and a reduced reimplementation of the lightweight ensemble of Sundaragiri et al.\ \cite{ijert} complete the set; the two judges are in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Internal-state detectors are outside the black-box setting \cite{inside2024}. The RoBERTa baseline receives up to 256 tokens and trains for three epochs. The majority baseline predicts the negative class, so probability metrics are undefined. The external ensemble uses five features (ROUGE-L, SBERT cosine, two NLI probabilities, answer length) in a logistic regression fit on the 14{,}000 training rows, and a validation-selected threshold of 0.55 gives F1 0.9514, so the fixed 0.5 threshold does not change the comparison. EC-XGB's display calibrator is fitted on the RAGTruth QA split; the in-domain Platt calibrator is fitted on validation. Metrics are reported to four decimals and rates to one. Significance uses McNemar on seed-42 predictions, 2,000 two-level bootstrap resamples, and Wilcoxon across seeds. No question appears in both training and evaluation. Calibration is compared on raw, Platt, and isotonic scores; Table~\ref{tab:protocols} defines P0, P1, and P2, and every protocol uses the same test rows.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Two families of metrics are reported. Threshold-dependent metrics measure the decision at a fixed cut-off, and threshold-free metrics measure how well a model ranks answers. Both are needed because a model can rank well and still make poor decisions when the threshold is fixed. Precision is the share of flagged answers that are truly hallucinated. Recall is the share of hallucinated answers that the detector catches, and F1 is their harmonic mean. The false-positive rate (FPR) is the share of correct answers that are flagged, which is the operating constraint under distribution shift. TP and TN are true positives and true negatives, while FP and FN are false positives and false negatives: a correct answer flagged as hallucinated, and a hallucinated answer that is not flagged. The flag rate is the share of answers above the decision threshold, and a rate near 100\% means every answer reaches a human reviewer.
\begin{gather}
  \text{Precision }(P) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
  \text{Recall }(R) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
  F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}, \\
  \text{False-positive rate }(\mathrm{FPR}) = \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{TN}}, \qquad
  \text{Flag rate} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}_i \ge \tau].
\end{gather}

The second group measures ranking, calibration, and paired error patterns. AUROC is the probability that a randomly chosen hallucinated answer receives a higher score than a randomly chosen correct answer, with 0.5 at chance. The area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) is more sensitive than AUROC when the positive class is rare. RAGTruth QA has a positive rate of 17.8\%, so PR-AUC is reported beside AUROC. The Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) uses all four cells of the confusion matrix and ranges from $-1$ to $1$, with zero at chance. The Wilson interval gives a 95\% range for a proportion and is used for the P2 recall and false-positive rate, where the counts are small.
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{AUROC} = \int_{0}^{1} \mathrm{TPR}(u)\, d\,\mathrm{FPR}(u) = P(s^{+} > s^{-}), \\
  \mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} P(r)\, dr, \qquad
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}\cdot\mathrm{TN} - \mathrm{FP}\cdot\mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}, \\
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\end{gather}

\subsection{Model Comparison}

Neither model generalizes to unseen QA (0.490 vs 0.549), and FaithBench is worse for both (0.434 vs 0.594). Adaptation helps any model on seen tasks; the feature model keeps its relative ordering on unseen tasks.

\subsection{EC-XGB and Ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ec} reports the component ablation under one fixed-hyperparameter configuration (max depth 5, learning rate 0.05, 300 trees, subsample 0.7, colsample 0.7, selected once on the m3 seed-42 run), so the columns measure feature effects rather than tuning effects. \subsection{Calibration, Shift, and External Validity}

The raw, Platt-scaled, and isotonic versions of the conventional model give ECE 0.0045, 0.0091, and 0.0070 on HaluEval, so the calibrated ECEs are not lower in this sample. Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} shows why. On HaluEval, the points at the two ends sit on the diagonal; the middle of the score range holds few distinct values, so the curve has only a few stable bins. FaithBench is aggregated because its scores concentrate near 1.0. The Platt and isotonic curves overlap the raw curve at both ends. The m3 raw score is also well calibrated in-domain, with ECE 0.0078, so there is little room for calibration to improve on HaluEval. Platt scaling fits a two-parameter logistic map from the uncalibrated probability $s$, called the raw score in the figures, to a probability,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p} = \sigma(a s + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
  \label{eq:platt}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the calibration slope and $b$ is the intercept. A slope below one means the raw score is overconfident, and a slope near one means the scale is already usable. The Brier skill score (BSS) compares the calibrated score with a constant predictor at the evaluation prevalence,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{prior}}}.
  \label{eq:bss}
\end{equation}
A positive BSS means the score beats the prior, and a non-positive BSS means it does not.

Table~\ref{tab:conformal} reports the coverage. Split conformal prediction adds a coverage guarantee to the score. The nonconformity score for label $y$ is $s(x,y)=1-\hat{p}_y(x)$, where $\hat{p}_1=\hat{p}$ and $\hat{p}_0=1-\hat{p}$. The calibration quantile and the prediction set are
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q} = s_{(k)}, \quad k = \left\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}\},
  \label{eq:conformal}
\end{equation}
where $s_{(1)} \le \cdots \le s_{(n)}$ are the ordered calibration scores; this finite-sample order statistic yields coverage of at least $1-\alpha$ for the prediction set. A class-conditional version replaces the single quantile with one quantile per label,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q}_y = s_{y,(k_y)}, \quad k_y = \left\lceil (n_y+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}_y\},
  \label{eq:conformal_class}
\end{equation}
where $s_{y,(1)} \le \cdots \le s_{y,(n_y)}$ are the ordered scores in class $y$ and $n_y$ is the number of calibration rows with label $y$.
The main run fits the Platt map and the quantile on the same 5{,}034 rows, so its coverage is a construction check; it gives 0.940 and 0.967 coverage on QA and 0.900 and 0.950 on the calibration rows. A disjoint sensitivity fits Platt on 2{,}514 rows and the quantile on 2{,}520 rows. On that split, the 900 QA test rows reach 0.944 coverage at 90\%, so the marginal guarantee holds. The marginal guarantee hides the class split: positive coverage is 0.685, while negative coverage is 1.000. A class-conditional quantile on the full calibration rows raises positive coverage to 0.887, still slightly below nominal. FaithBench coverage falls to 0.320 and positive coverage to 0.003, because a Platt map fitted on QA sends almost every FaithBench score to the negative side. Conformal prediction therefore certifies the marginal rate only when the calibrator and the quantile are fitted on disjoint rows, and it does not transfer across the task shift. Multicalibration by answer length and claim count shows where the average ECE hides error. On the 900 QA test rows, the target-Platt score has overall ECE 0.125. The worst length bin is 60 words or fewer (n=154), with ECE 0.275; the worst claim-count bin is a single claim (n=36), with ECE 0.346. The Brier score does not follow the ECE ranking, so the average is not a safe summary for short or one-claim answers. FaithBench is not improved by the QA calibrator: its target-Platt ECE is 0.629 against 0.453 for the raw score.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Statistical tests use the paired fixed-hyperparameter predictions and 2{,}000 two-level bootstrap draws, where groups are resampled within each seed and then seeds are resampled. McNemar's test compares the discordant counts $b$ and $c$ on the paired seed-42 rows, where $b$ is the number of rows where the first model is right and the second is wrong, and $c$ is the reverse; the statistic uses a continuity correction. Holm's method controls the family-wise error rate across the four baseline comparisons. The 95\% interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the paired bootstrap deltas. The two-level version resamples groups within each seed before resampling seeds. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares the two models across the three seeds. Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} gives the McNemar counts. EC-XGB and the conventional model are not significantly different under the deployed checkpoints ($p=0.45$), and the fixed-hyperparameter ablation gives $p=0.789$ on the same test rows. The fixed-hp m0-vs-m3 comparison is the primary test, and it is not part of the Holm family, which covers the four baseline comparisons against the conventional model. The test uses the single configuration of Table~\ref{tab:ec}. The random forest matches the conventional model ($p=0.502$). Logistic regression and the heuristic are significantly worse ($p=1.86\times10^{-10}$ and $p=2.28\times10^{-30}$), and both survive Holm correction. The Wilcoxon test across three seeds cannot reach significance (minimum $p=0.25$). Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports the two-level bootstrap. On HaluEval, the m3$-$m0 F1 delta is $+$0.0006 [$-0.0010$, 0.0021], which includes zero, and the AUROC delta is $+$0.0007 [0.0002, 0.0012], which excludes zero by a small margin. The m2$-$m1 F1 delta is $-$0.0004 [$-0.0020$, 0.0012], and the m3$-$m1 delta is $+$0.0001 [$-0.0015$, 0.0018], so the claim features do not add an aggregate gain. Their AUROC deltas are $+$0.0009 and $+$0.0010, both with intervals above zero, so the added features reorder a few rows without changing F1.

On held-out RAGTruth QA the m3$-$m0 F1 delta is $+$0.0076 [0.0045, 0.0109], but the AUROC delta is $+$0.0017 [$-0.0270$, 0.0291], which includes zero. The F1 gain comes from flagging almost every row, not from better ranking. On the pooled seen tasks the F1 delta is $+$0.0451 [0.0348, 0.0554] and the AUROC delta is $+$0.0962 [0.0644, 0.1295], which is the adaptation gain. The paired fixed-minus-tuned contrasts all include zero, so the rebuild does not change these conclusions. HaluEval groups are item pairs (1{,}500), RAGTruth QA has 150 source groups, and the pooled split has 450; the QA AUROC interval includes zero, so the held-out transfer result is not resolved, and the pooled row measures adaptation to seen task types, not unseen-task transfer.

\subsection{Explanation, Efficiency, and Judges}

The explanation results use the fixed-hyperparameter m3 seed-42 checkpoint. Figure~\ref{fig:shapimp} ranks the top-15 features by mean absolute SHAP over 1{,}000 stratified HaluEval test rows. Context overlap dominates, followed by word count, question-answer cosine, character count, and question-answer Jaccard; the claim-contradiction mean enters at rank eight. The top ten features carry 77.9\% of the total attribution mass, and exact TreeSHAP reproduces the model margin to $9.2\times10^{-6}$. The attributions are on the raw margin, not the display score. The m3 ranking agrees with the frozen B2 ranking (Spearman $\rho=0.878$, Kendall $\tau=0.730$), so the claim features refine the explanation rather than replace the original drivers. Cross-seed Spearman is 0.972 and 0.950, with nine of ten top features shared and an identical top-5 set. The explanation is stable to the seed but less stable to content edits. Table~\ref{tab:perturb} shows the 20-case perturbation pilot: content edits move the score, structural edits barely move it, and the top-1 feature flips in 54.5\% of entity perturbations and none of the other operators. The entity flip rate is higher than in B2, so the explanation is less stable when the answer entity changes; per-operator $n$ is small. A structured AI-assisted audit assessed 26 error-enriched cases selected under B2 and re-scored under m3, with each explanation checked against the answer-context evidence rather than the dataset label. Thirteen explanations were judged implausible, three unsure, and ten plausible, so the explanations are diagnostic aids, not faithful attributions. False positives inherit the length confound, and false negatives often reuse entities from the context. Because the sample is error-enriched, the percentages describe the audit, not the test set. The labels are author-checked pre-annotations: LLM tools assisted the annotation, and the authors reviewed and verified the final labels. Agreement with the pre-annotations was 0.98 for explanation, 0.94 for taxonomy, and 0.88 for claims. Cohen's $\kappa$ was 0.965, 0.923, and 0.788; the authors changed 2\%, 6\%, and 12\% of rows, and the blind Fleiss $\kappa$ was 0.940, 0.868, and 0.761.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Discussion}

On the seed-42 fixed-hyperparameter run, m3 makes 43 errors on the HaluEval test set (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}), an error rate of 1.4\%. The WP5 audit covers seven of the eight false positives and 12 of the 35 false negatives, chosen to cover the codebook. A separate 50-case sheet, reviewed by one author, adds 31 artifact cases and 19 content cases across 12 categories. These describe the reviewed cases, not the 3{,}000-row test population. Table~\ref{tab:length} shows that length is a near-perfect label indicator on HaluEval, with false positives concentrated in the five-to-eight bin and false negatives in the short bins, although the 17+ bin has only two negative rows. The controls change the length distribution in two ways. A reweighting control equalizes six length bins with 247 examples each, and a length-matching control pairs one positive and one negative at each length. Reweighting gives EC-XGB a gain of 0.039 F1 and 0.023 AUROC over the length-only regression, which is already competitive on its own (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Length matching gives 79 pairs (158 examples) over 1{,}000 bootstraps. EC-XGB holds AUROC 0.956 while the length-only baseline falls to 0.521. The AUROC point gap is 0.435 (bootstrap mean 0.436 [0.351, 0.522]), and the F1 point gap is 0.431 (bootstrap mean 0.440 [0.334, 0.552]).

Figure~\ref{fig:length} shows the same pattern. Complete sentences are long, so the conventional model reads them as hallucinations, and the in-domain model is mostly a length and overlap detector. Published HaluEval QA results use different splits, supervision, and evidence access. A supervised 13B agent reports F1 0.724 on 100 rows, so these published numbers are context rather than a head-to-head bar. The benchmark also asks for hallucinated answers about five words longer, and the observed gap is 8.5 words, so any comparison that ignores this construction artifact overstates semantic detection. The claims in this paper are bounded by the tests that were not run. Retrieval quality and end-to-end verdict accuracy are not evaluated, the judge comparison controls length but not domain, and the audit is error-enriched. The deployed display calibrator was selected in an earlier fit that compared Platt and isotonic on the test split, and Table~\ref{tab:target} refits Platt without that selection.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This study tested EC-XGB, a 35-feature evidence-consistent model, as a hallucination risk score and asked how its calibration and ranking change under domain and task shift. Its ranking survives a change of corpus, so the score can order answers for review, and the per-claim verdicts give a reviewer a reason for each flag. Its probability does not survive that change: the display score needs labeled target rows, and the target prevalence then sets its level. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific screening score, not a general-purpose detector. The next test is adaptation to an unseen QA domain, with thresholds selected under prevalence shift. A seen-task source ablation would then separate the RAGTruth rows from the source indicator.
