\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) answer in fluent text, and some answers are unsupported by the evidence a user has. A confidently wrong answer can mislead people in question answering. Current detectors have practical limits. A large judge model is slow and costly, encoder classifiers need model weights and a graphics card, and feature-based detectors rarely report calibration or behavior outside the training domain. This study asks whether a small black-box model can estimate the risk that an answer is hallucinated from the question, the evidence, and the candidate answer alone. A second question is whether that estimate can be trusted as a probability. The proposed method, evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB), is a gradient-boosted tree model over 35 features. The features cover overlap, entities, numbers, hedging words, semantic similarity, and entailment, and eight of them summarize the support of each atomic claim. Training caps the effect of answer length, keeps the score from falling when the answer contradicts the evidence, and adds natural responses with a source marker. The benchmark is close to saturated, so the gain has to come from reliability under shift, and the natural-answer corpora label that test with grouped splits and three seeds. On HaluEval question answering, EC-XGB holds the in-domain score at F1 = 0.9855. The gain appears under domain shift, although discrimination stays weak. The AUROC rises from 0.475 to 0.582 while the share of combined RAGTruth answers flagged as risky falls from 99.9 to 61.3 percent. The held-out RAGTruth QA task stays near F1 0.30. A display calibrator fitted on RAGTruth cuts the deployed expected calibration error from 0.726 to 0.126. One analysis takes 61.8 ms and costs about USD 0.001 per 1,000 predictions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Hallucination detection is an active research area. SelfCheckGPT samples multiple responses and measures their consistency without an external database \cite{selfcheck}. Luna uses a small evaluation model that costs less than a large judge \cite{luna}. Semantic entropy groups sampled answers by meaning and flags confabulation from disagreement, again with no external knowledge \cite{farquhar2024semantic}. A hybrid framework combines frozen encoder features with lightweight classifiers and reports strong benchmark scores \cite{ieeeTai}. Two practical problems remain. Judge models are slow and expensive, and encoder models are hard to interpret. Feature-based detectors are cheaper, but their probabilities are rarely calibrated and their behavior outside the training domain is rarely tested. Three recent surveys group these families and list evaluation and reliability limits among the open questions \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey,alansari2026survey}.

Six recent studies are closest to this work. HaluAgent shows that small language models can select tools and detect multiple hallucination types, yet each answer needs multiple tool calls \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. report a calibrated multi-scoring detector with a clear cost analysis, and the detector still depends on LLM scoring for its main signal \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. combine retrieval grounding, NLI verification, and an XGBoost classifier, and their evaluation reports classification metrics only \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. score hallucination with token-level SHAP-LIME attributions, without testing whether those attributions stay stable \cite{multimedia}. Deng et al. study cross-domain detection and show that detectors lose accuracy outside their training domain, and their method targets multi-turn dialogue signals \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench reports that modern LLMs still produce hard summarization hallucinations, and it is a summarization benchmark with no risk-calibration study \cite{faithbench}.

Four issues remain open in the literature. Calibration is rarely reported for lightweight detectors, explanation quality is rarely measured, cross-domain behavior is rarely tested, and error patterns are rarely analyzed case by case. This study addresses the four issues together. It builds a black-box model that reads a question, a reference context, and a candidate answer and estimates $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$. The model, evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB), combines 26 evidence-consistency features in seven groups with eight claim-level NLI aggregates and a tuned XGBoost classifier. Platt and isotonic calibration produce the probability, and SHAP explanations show the feature contributions. Group-aware splits and artifact-control baselines cover the benchmark shortcuts, and validation-only calibration answers the reliability question. Zero-shot runs on RAGTruth and FaithBench measure the transfer limits.

HaluEval QA provides the training and in-domain test data, and RAGTruth supplies natural responses for the transfer, multi-source training, and recalibration checks. FaithBench adds a summarization stress test. HaluEval QA is suitable for training because it provides paired correct and hallucinated answers for 10{,}000 questions. RAGTruth provides natural responses with human span annotations for an external check. The accepted outcomes are a calibrated risk model (EC-XGB) with F1 = 0.9855 on HaluEval QA. Under shift, the flag rate on natural RAGTruth answers falls from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, while the AUROC rises from 0.475 to 0.582. Target recalibration reduces the RAGTruth QA ECE from 0.8185 to 0.1335. An operating point maximizes recall under a five percent false-positive budget, and a deployed system completes one analysis in 61.8~ms for near \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

A hallucination is text that is fluent but not supported by the available evidence. Detection quality is usually judged with labeled benchmark splits and the standard metrics of precision, recall, F1, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Standard test beds include HaluEval \cite{haluEval} and TruthfulQA \cite{truthqa}, and FActScore \cite{factscore} measures factual precision in long-form generation. Two evaluation choices matter for deployment. The first is calibration. A risk score is only useful when its numeric value matches the observed frequency of hallucinated answers, and expected calibration error and reliability diagrams expose that gap \cite{guo}. The second is interpretability, since a user who sees a high-risk flag needs to know which evidence signal produced it, and feature-attribution methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) provide that view for tabular models \cite{shap}. The present work evaluates both properties in a black-box setting, where the detector reads the question, an optional reference context, and the candidate answer, and never inspects the generating model.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method in this study is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 hand-crafted evidence-consistency features. XGBoost is a gradient-boosting ensemble, so the model adds decision trees one at a time and each new tree fits the error left by the trees already in the ensemble \cite{xgboost}. Each tree splits the feature space into leaves, and each leaf stores a weight that adds to the running score. The final score passes through a logistic link, so the output is a probability of the hallucinated class.

Let $x_i$ be the feature vector of row $i$ and $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves of weight $w_k$, a leaf-count penalty $\gamma$, and an L2 weight penalty $\lambda$. The loss is the standard logistic loss and the probability comes from the sigmoid of the summed leaf scores, $\hat{y}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$.

Tree depth, learning rate, tree count, subsample rate, and column subsample rate come from a randomized search over 30 configurations, scored with grouped 5-fold cross-validation on the training split. The selected values are listed with the experimental setup in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. This baseline is the reference for every later modification, and its training and inference procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:baseline}. The procedure extracts the features once, tunes the hyperparameters with a grouped search, and repeats training, calibration, and evaluation for each seed so that the reported metrics are seed-averaged.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and inference)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, calibrator, and decision threshold
    \State extract the 26 evidence-consistency features for every row
    \State tune XGBoost with a randomized search and grouped 5-fold cross-validation
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State train the classifier on the training split
    \State fit Platt calibration and select the decision threshold on the validation split
    \State evaluate on the fixed test split
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Limitations and Research Gap}

The baseline works well on its own benchmark but has clear limits. On HaluEval the benchmark is close to saturated, so a better feature set buys almost no accuracy, and an in-domain win is a weak target. The model also leans on length and lexical overlap, and the error analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that answer length alone is close to a perfect cue in this corpus. Under domain shift the raw probabilities turn unreliable, since the model flags almost every natural answer and its confidence no longer matches the observed error rate.

These limits connect to open problems in the literature. Token-level attributions explain raw text rather than semantically meaningful evidence signals, and they are visualized without any reliability measurement \cite{multimedia}. Calibration is rarely reported for lightweight detectors, and target-domain behavior is rarely tested, while the closest multi-signal framework reports classification metrics only \cite{ijert}. The benchmark itself imposes limits as well, since around 85\% of HaluEval is generated with a sampling-then-filtering procedure \cite{bang-etal-2025-hallulens}, and such data blurs the line between factually wrong and hallucinated content. Cross-domain evaluation stays open, and SpikeScore shows that detectors trained in one domain lose accuracy in another \cite{spikescore}. This study fills these gaps by fusing claim-level evidence signals and multi-source training into an evidence-consistent XGBoost and by testing calibrated, explainable risk prediction under domain shift with a reproducible artifact.

\subsection{Working Principle and Task}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the full proposed pipeline at a glance. The system takes a question, an optional reference context, and a candidate answer produced by a black-box LLM, and it returns a calibrated risk probability together with per-feature explanations. Each stage is described in the following subsections.

Given a question $q$, an optional reference context $c$ (evidence), and a candidate answer $a$ produced by a black-box LLM, the task is to predict the probability $P(\text{hallucinated} \mid q, c, a)$. The proposed model predicts risk, not truth. It flags answers that are unsupported by, or contradictory to, the available evidence.

\subsection{Features}

Table~\ref{tab:features} lists the 26 base features in seven groups, and Table~\ref{tab:claim} lists the eight claim-level aggregates that the proposed model adds. Natural language inference (NLI) decides whether one sentence follows from another, and the NLI signals come from a DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder \cite{deberta} trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}, evaluated in both directions, context-to-answer and answer-to-context, which yields six probability features. Semantic similarity uses MiniLM sentence embeddings \cite{sbert,minilm}, entity features use spaCy NER \cite{spacy}, and the numeric and hedging groups are collected with regular expressions and a small hedging lexicon.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB keeps the standard XGBoost learner and changes three things, added cumulatively so that each change can be measured on its own. The first change replaces the raw length counts with log-scaled values and adds monotone constraints, so risk cannot fall when contradiction or novelty rises and cannot climb when entailment, overlap, or semantic similarity rises. A log-scaled length feature replaces the raw count, $\ell' = \log(1 + \ell)$, which compresses the long tail of the length distribution. A monotone constraint fixes the sign of the model response for a chosen feature, so an evidence-positive feature $x_j$ keeps $\partial \hat{y} / \partial x_j \leq 0$, and the sign flips for an evidence-negative feature such as contradiction. In plain terms, more contradiction can never lower the risk score, and more entailment can never raise it. The second change appends the eight claim-level aggregates from Table~\ref{tab:claim}, which lets the classifier see the same evidence signals that the deployed verification layer reports. For an answer split into $K$ claims with per-claim contradiction $c_k$ and entailment $e_k$, the aggregate vector and the full model input are
\begin{gather}
  \psi(x) = \big[ \max_k c_k, \ \overline{c}, \ r_{\text{con}}, \ r_{\text{sup}}, \ r_{\text{unsup}}, \ \min_k e_k, \ \overline{e}, \ K \big], \\
  \phi'(x) = \big[ \ell', \ \tilde{\phi}(x), \ \psi(x), \ s \big],
\end{gather}
where $r_{\text{con}}$, $r_{\text{sup}}$, and $r_{\text{unsup}}$ are the contradicted, supported, and unsupported claim ratios, and $\tilde{\phi}(x)$ holds the remaining base features after the length change, which gives 35 inputs in total. The third change adds RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text training rows with a binary source indicator $s \in \{0,1\}$, while every RAGTruth QA row stays out of training, which keeps the QA transfer result zero-shot for the multitask model. The deployed checkpoint combines all three changes and is called EC-XGB. A strict decision mode selects the validation threshold whose false-positive rate stays at or below five percent, and the calibration protocol below fits its display score. Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecxgb} lists the full procedure: it trains one model per seed, fits the display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration split, selects the strict threshold on the HaluEval validation split, and returns the deployed artifacts.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled HaluEval QA rows, RAGTruth non-QA rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}$
    \Ensure deployed EC-XGB model, display calibrator, and strict threshold
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim-level aggregates
    \State log-scale the length features and set the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input with the source indicator
    \State train XGBoost on HaluEval plus RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of training
    \State fit the display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration split
    \State select the strict threshold on the HaluEval validation split
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the deployed model, calibrator, and threshold
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The contribution is the combination. Each piece is standard on its own, and together they hold the in-domain score, cut the false-alarm rate under shift, and keep the output explainable at the feature level. The deployed system adds claim-level verification with evidence retrieval and a display calibrator fitted on the target domain. Relative to the closest feature-based detector \cite{ijert}, the difference is the claim-level evidence features and the calibration protocol. Relative to the LLM-scored detector \cite{costeff} and the tool-based detector \cite{haluagent}, the difference is that inference needs no model calls.

\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

Calibrators are fit on the validation split only. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid $p(x) = \sigma(A f(x) + B)$ to the raw score $f(x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are fit by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing step function $g$ so that $p(x) = g(f(x))$ \cite{isotonic}. Both are compared against the raw model on the test split. The comparison uses expected calibration error over $M=10$ bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the accuracy and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, together with the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$ and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. A second step targets a new domain. The calibrator can be re-fit on a small labeled sample of the target corpus, and the effect is measured on a disjoint held-out part of the target corpus. The deployed EC-XGB display score follows the same rule: its calibrator is fitted on the designated RAGTruth QA calibration split, and the source indicator is set to one for natural responses, the same convention used in the shift evaluation.

Global interpretability comes from SHAP \cite{shap}. A TreeExplainer over the raw XGBoost model produces mean absolute SHAP importance and beeswarm summaries, and local explanations show the feature contributions behind a single verdict. Explanation reliability is measured directly in this work, since feature-attribution methods can be unstable \cite{adebayo2018}. Each test answer is perturbed in six ways, by replacing entities, numbers, or dates, removing supporting context, inserting irrelevant context, and shuffling clauses, and the calibrated score should stay stable under neutral perturbations and react to semantic ones. Stability is quantified with Spearman and Kendall rank correlation between the original and perturbed scores, the fraction of top-1 flips, and the top-$k$ Jaccard overlap of the SHAP feature rankings.

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

The methods above are deployed as a two-tier web application. The Next.js frontend streams an LLM conversation on the chat page using the assistant-ui library, and the FastAPI backend holds the model, the calibrator, the SHAP explainer, and the feature models, all loaded once at startup. Every machine-learning request from the frontend goes through a backend-for-frontend proxy in the Next.js layer to the FastAPI endpoints, which avoids cross-origin issues and keeps the API key on the server side. All quantitative results in Section~\ref{sec:results} measure the EC-XGB classifier and its calibrator alone. The claim-verification layer described below is a separate deployment component. It changes the displayed verdict and is demonstrated through the interface rather than measured in the reported metrics.

The chat page streams through the Vercel AI SDK route. A tool call from the conversation model triggers the analysis endpoint, and the result renders as a risk card with the calibrated score, the per-claim verdicts, the SHAP bars, and an evidence panel.

Beyond the score, the system verifies the answer claim by claim. Each claim is decomposed from the answer text and checked with sentence-level NLI against one of three evidence sources, the provided context, an indexed document collection, or live web search. Verdicts are decided support-first, so any entailing sentence supports a claim and a contradiction decides only when no sentence supports it, and a relevance gate drops retrieved sentences that do not cover the claim vocabulary. The verdicts supported, contradicted, and unsupported are rendered per claim, with the driving evidence sentence quoted for contradicted claims. Web search uses the Brave Search API with its LLM-context endpoint, and the document index combines BM25, FAISS dense retrieval, and reciprocal rank fusion with a cross-encoder reranker. An uncertain verification score routes the claim to an LLM judge that returns a verdict with a short reasoning note.

The verdict headline leads the card. Any contradicted claim sets the risk to high, an unsupported claim sets it to medium, and fully supported claims set it to low, while the standard XGBoost score appears beneath it as the legacy model score. This ordering follows the style-sensitivity result in Section~\ref{sec:results}, where a full-sentence grounded answer can saturate the calibrated score even though every claim is supported. The displayed percentage comes from the EC-XGB display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA split and is adjusted by the claim verdicts.

The analyze page shows the risk gauge, the calibrated score, the SHAP bars, and a comparison against the standard model.

Figure~\ref{fig:screens} shows the analyze page for the hallucinated-number case, where the context states 5 days per decade and the answer states 10. The raw EC-XGB probability is 0.999, so the raw score alone is not an actionable risk level, while the deployed display calibrator reports 35.4\% and marks the answer as medium risk. The comparison panel shows the same pattern across the baselines, with the learned models all near 99.9\% and the overlap heuristic at 8.3\%, which is why the interface leads with the calibrated score and keeps the raw score as a secondary signal.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Conditions}

All experiments run on a Windows 11 laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU (CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128) and 32\,GB of RAM, and the code runs on Python 3.12. The model uses scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. The XGBoost parameters are depth 4, learning rate 0.01, 500 trees, subsample 0.9, and column subsample 0.7, selected by the randomized search described above. Every run is fixed by the saved split indices and the centralized seeds, so the same command reproduces the same tables.

\subsection{Dataset}

The QA subset of HaluEval \cite{haluEval} is the training source. It contains 10{,}000 questions, each with a knowledge passage, a correct answer, and a hallucinated answer, which gives 20{,}000 labeled rows. The rows are split 70/15/15 at the question-group level, producing 14{,}000 training, 3{,}000 validation, and 3{,}000 test rows. This grouped, leakage-free design matters because both answer variants of a question share the same context, and a random split could otherwise let the model memorize the pairing. The split indices are saved for exact reproducibility.

Transfer is measured on RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth}, a corpus of naturally generated responses with human word-level span annotations, and on FaithBench \cite{faithbench}, a summarization hallucination benchmark built from modern LLM outputs. The RAGTruth QA task contributes 5{,}934 rows in 989 groups, of which 5{,}034 rows (839 groups) form the recalibration set and 900 rows (150 groups) the held-out test, with no overlapping groups between the two. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the corpora.

Text is whitespace-normalized. Lexical features operate on lowercased token sets, while NER and NLI keep the original casing. The repository ships the download scripts, source revisions, and SHA-256 hashes instead, so every split can be regenerated from the original repositories.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

Eight models are compared. The overlap heuristic sets risk to one minus the lexical overlap between the answer and the context, and its threshold is tuned on validation. Logistic regression on standardized features and a random forest with 300 trees are the two standard classifiers. The standard classifier is XGBoost \cite{xgboost}, trained on the 26 base features, and it is the reference for the modified model in Section~\ref{sec:proposed}. Three controls isolate the value of the feature design, an NLI-only logistic regression that sees only the six NLI probabilities, an answer-only TF-IDF linear model, and a TF-IDF linear model over the full input. Internal-state detectors read hidden activations and need model access, so they sit outside the black-box setting \cite{inside2024}. A majority baseline that predicts the negative class for every row confirms the balance of the labels. XGBoost hyperparameters come from a randomized search of 30 iterations over depth, learning rate, tree count, subsample, and column subsampling, scored with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training split. The selected configuration gives a cross-validated AUROC of 0.9970, and the values are listed with the experimental setup in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. Every experiment is repeated with seeds 42, 123, and 456, and results are reported as the mean over seeds.

Evaluation uses precision, recall, F1, AUROC, the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC), and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and calibration with expected calibration error over 10 bins, Brier score, and negative log-likelihood. Metric tables report four decimals, and latency is reported in milliseconds. Hyperparameters are chosen by a randomized search of 30 iterations with grouped 5-fold cross-validation on the training split. Every experiment is repeated with seeds 42, 123, and 456 and reported as the mean over seeds. Significance is assessed with McNemar's test on the paired seed-42 test predictions, bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals from 1{,}000 resamples over question or source groups, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test across seeds. The group structure is respected everywhere, so the two rows of one question never appear in both the training and the evaluation folds.

\subsection{Additional Protocols}

Calibrators are fit on the validation split only, and the comparison of raw, Platt-scaled, and isotonic probabilities happens on the test split. Calibration is known to degrade under distribution shift \cite{ovadia2019}, so the target-domain protocol follows the same principle. The recalibration set of 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA rows is used only to fit the target calibrator, and all reported numbers come from the disjoint 900-row test set.

Global explanations come from a SHAP TreeExplainer \cite{shap} over the raw XGBoost model. Three measurements check whether the explanations are trustworthy. First, the mean absolute SHAP ranking is compared against permutation importance with a Kendall tau correlation. Second, the top-5 feature set is re-estimated from 1{,}000 bootstrap resamples and compared with the Jaccard index. Third, two perturbation studies are run on the test answers. Content edits replace entities, numbers, or dates, remove the supporting context, or insert an irrelevant sentence, and each edit should move the calibrated score if it changes the evidence. Structure edits shuffle the clause order, and these should leave the score nearly unchanged. For each operator the mean absolute score change, the fraction of large score changes, the top-1 SHAP flip rate, and the Spearman correlation between the original and perturbed scores are recorded. A neutralization study sets the top-$k$ SHAP features of each sample to its median and tracks the mean score change for $k = 1, 3, 5, 10$.

Latency is measured on 200 test samples on the reference laptop with an RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU, where NLI and embeddings run on the GPU and the remaining features on the CPU. The LLM-as-judge comparison uses GPT 5.6 Luna, a standard but biased comparator \cite{zheng2023judge}, on the same 200 samples with a fixed prompt, and the cost is computed from the recorded token counts.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the test-set performance of every baseline, averaged over the three seeds. XGBoost reaches an F1 of 0.9846 with an AUROC of 0.9979, ahead of the random forest by 0.0004 in F1 and the logistic regression by 0.0186. The overlap heuristic, which needs no training beyond its threshold, still reaches an F1 of 0.9352, and a majority baseline that predicts the negative class for everything scores zero, which confirms that the labels are balanced. The NLI-only baseline lags at 0.6714, which shows that the lexical, length, and semantic signals carry information the NLI cross-encoder alone does not. The TF-IDF variants sit between these extremes, with an answer-only linear model at 0.9224 and a full-input model at 0.5999. The margin between XGBoost and the random forest is small but significant in a McNemar test on the paired seed-42 test predictions ($p = 0.044$), while the comparison against logistic regression ($p = 0.43$) and the heuristic ($p = 0.35$) is not significant. The Wilcoxon test across the three seeds finds no difference, which is expected given that the test compares only three points. Bootstrap confidence intervals over 1{,}000 question-group resamples for XGBoost are F1 $[0.9809,\,0.9896]$ and AUROC $[0.9964,\,0.9989]$. The standard XGBoost row in Table~\ref{tab:models} and the m0 row in Table~\ref{tab:ec} come from the baseline and modified pipelines. The two runs differ only within seed-level variation (F1 0.9846 against 0.9844).

The split design is verified against a known confound. A row-level split, where the two rows of one question can fall into different partitions, inflates F1 to 0.9886, and the grouped split corrects this to 0.9846, a drop of 0.004 in F1 and 0.0001 in AUROC. The reported numbers all use the grouped split.

\subsection{Cross-domain transfer}

The transfer evaluation is the central negative result of the paper. The standard model is applied to every external corpus without training or adaptation, so its results there are a clean zero-shot measurement. EC-XGB has seen the RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text training rows, so only its held-out QA and FaithBench results are zero-shot. In the dedicated cross-domain run, the standard model reaches an F1 of 0.302, an AUROC of 0.540, and an ECE of 0.819, and recall is 1.0 on every corpus: the model flags almost everything, the precision lands on the label prior, and the AUROC stays near 0.5. Synthetic HaluEval hallucinations differ from natural responses. FaithBench sits between the two extremes at an F1 of 0.8130 and an AUROC of 0.5314, since its summaries are shorter and closer to the HaluEval format, and the full 17{,}790-row RAGTruth corpus lands at an F1 of 0.603, an AUROC of 0.497, and an ECE of 0.560. \subsection{Explanation reliability}

A structured expert audit tested these explanations case by case. The B5 run exported 26 error-enriched cases (9 false positives, 10 false negatives, and 7 inside the 0.35 to 0.65 band), and two review passes were performed with an AI assistant, which the released sheet records in a review-mode field. Both passes judged the explanation against the answer-context evidence rather than the dataset label, they agreed on 23 of 26 cases (88\%), and both marked 15 cases implausible. False positives inherit the length confound, and false negatives occur when a wrong answer reuses context entities, which keeps overlap high. Two audited cases (items 2251 and 2436) carry a hallucinated label even though the context directly supports the answer, so they look like benchmark label noise rather than model errors. The audit sample is deliberately error-heavy, so these proportions describe the audit and not the test set.

The length distribution behind those errors is the more informative signal. Table~\ref{tab:length} bins the full test set by answer length. The hallucinated share is 2.1\% among one-word answers and rises to 99.2\% above 17 words, so answer length is close to a perfect proxy for the label in HaluEval. The model reproduces the pattern. The mean score climbs from 0.024 to 0.993 across the bins. Two-thirds of all false positives fall in the 5-8 word bin at a 10.6\% false-positive rate, and false negatives concentrate in the 1-4 word bins at about an 18\% rate. This mechanism explains the zero-shot transfer result. Natural RAGTruth responses are full sentences, so the model reads their length as evidence of hallucination, which is why recall reaches 1.0 and AUROC stays near 0.5 on that corpus. A logistic regression on the two length features alone reaches F1 = 0.9457 and AUROC = 0.9759, so a large part of the benchmark signal is answer length. \subsection{Discussion and limitations}

Around 85\% of HaluEval is generated with a sampling-then-filtering procedure \cite{bang-etal-2025-hallulens}, so its hallucination patterns are synthetic by construction. The zero-shot transfer result on RAGTruth shows how far in-distribution performance is from natural responses. Length and overlap statistics are highly informative on HaluEval but may not carry over, and the binary label leaves graded severity out of scope. The audit found two cases where the hallucinated label contradicts the context, so some label noise remains. Every feature in the pipeline is English-centric, and the supervised model now trains on HaluEval plus the RAGTruth non-QA tasks, yet domain adaptation remains open work at this scale. Stronger neural baselines, such as a fine-tuned encoder detector, were not evaluated, and the LLM judge is the strongest external comparison in this study. Under domain shift EC-XGB trades recall for precision on unseen corpora. On FaithBench, the flag rate falls from 99.5\% to 7.3\% while the F1 drops because the model becomes conservative. The RAGTruth all-task gains also include task types that the multi-source training saw, so they measure adaptation rather than pure zero-shot transfer, and the held-out QA task improves mainly in calibration. On the source benchmark the raw probabilities are already well calibrated, and the display calibrator cuts the target ECE from 0.726 to 0.126. The calibrated score is also style-sensitive in conversational use, so the interface leads with the evidence verdicts and presents the model score as a secondary signal. Four established directions fit the verification layer next. RAGAs scores claim support and answer relevance without human review \cite{ragas2024}, and RAGChecker diagnoses retrieval and generation at the claim level \cite{ragchecker2024}. VeriScore scores verifiable claims in long-form answers \cite{veriscore2024}, and small verifier models make claim-level checks cheap enough for every answer \cite{minicheck2024}.

A hallucination-risk score predicts risk, not truth, and it must not be treated as a fact-checker. Over-reliance on automated detectors creates false trust, especially when scores are confidently wrong out of domain, which is exactly what the RAGTruth result demonstrates. Detection quality varies across domains and languages, and the current system is English-only. The interface therefore labels scores as risk estimates and shows a training-data warning with every prediction.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Hallucination detection needs to be cheap, fast, and explainable before it is useful in practice. This study presented EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent XGBoost risk predictor. It combines 26 engineered evidence-consistency features with eight claim-level NLI aggregates, log-scaled length features under monotone constraints, multi-source training, calibrated probabilities, and SHAP explanations. On HaluEval QA it reaches an F1 of 0.9855 and an AUROC of 0.9984, on par with the standard tuned XGBoost on a saturated benchmark. It makes fewer false positives at the same operating point. The gain appears under domain shift. The standard model flags 99.9\% of natural RAGTruth answers as risky and EC-XGB flags 61.3\%, with the AUROC rising from 0.475 to 0.582 and the ECE falling from 0.635 to 0.278. The deployed display calibrator cuts the RAGTruth QA ECE from 0.726 to 0.126 and needs a labeled target sample (5{,}034 rows here). The displayed score is a risk estimate, not a fact-checking verdict. One analysis takes 61.8\,ms at the median and costs about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions. That is roughly 100 times less than the GPT 5.6 Luna judge, which reaches a lower F1 on the same 200 samples.

The negative results are reported with the same care as the positive ones. The raw model needs no recalibration on HaluEval, and the entity and hedging features add no measurable value. The held-out RAGTruth QA task stays near an F1 of 0.30 even after multi-source training, while the conservative FaithBench regime trades F1 for calibration. The paper therefore frames the supervised risk model as a benchmark and adaptation estimator, and the deployed system adds claim-level verification with evidence retrieval. Future work targets multicalibration under stronger shift, conformal prediction \cite{conformal2024}, per-domain threshold transfer, shortcut-controlled evaluation on unseen tasks, and neural baselines for comparison.
