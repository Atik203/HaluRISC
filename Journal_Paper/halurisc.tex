\begin{abstract}
  Large language models answer fluently, but some answers lack evidence support. Detectors rely on a large judge, an encoder, or model internals. These options are costly and rarely report calibration under shift. Feature-based detectors are cheaper, but their calibration and length sensitivity are seldom measured together, and HaluEval length is almost a label proxy. This study tests whether a simple feature model can estimate risk and how its calibration and ranking change under shift. EC-XGB combines 26 base features, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator. It is trained on HaluEval QA and RAGTruth rows with grouped splits and three seeds, and evaluated with a fixed-hyperparameter ablation, source and target Platt calibration, conformal prediction, SHAP stability, a length-only baseline, an external ensemble, and judge stress tests. The fixed-hyperparameter EC-XGB reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986 on HaluEval QA. Lexical overlap and length carry the in-domain score: removing them costs 0.0171 and 0.0066 F1. On MedHallu, the model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 pairs (AUROC 0.821), but flags every row and is badly calibrated (ECE 0.493). On HaluEval dialogue and summarization, it fails in different ways: dialogue is flagged almost entirely, and summarization ranks below a length-only model. On RAGTruth QA it reaches AUROC 0.558. Target calibration lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, but the calibrated score flags no target rows at a 0.5 threshold. Split conformal reaches 0.944 coverage at a 90\% target, but positive coverage is only 0.685, and FaithBench coverage falls to 0.320. The GPT-5.6 Luna judge reaches F1 0.565 on natural QA, where EC-XGB reaches 0.310. On a 158-row length-matched set EC-XGB reaches 0.907 and the judge 0.865, but the interval includes zero. EC-XGB is a domain-specific risk score, not a general-purpose detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Black-box detectors score a candidate answer from its text and context. SelfCheckGPT samples several answers and checks whether they agree \cite{selfcheck}. Luna replaces the large judge with a smaller evaluation model \cite{luna}. Semantic entropy clusters sampled answers by meaning \cite{farquhar2024semantic}. A hybrid framework combines frozen encoder features with lightweight classifiers \cite{ieeeTai}. Judges carry high computational costs, and encoder models are hard to interpret. Two recent reviews group detectors into feature-based, encoder-based, and hybrid approaches \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey}. Both name evaluation and reliability as open problems, and a third review reaches the same conclusion about reliability \cite{alansari2026survey}.

HaluAgent showed that small models can select tools, but it needs multiple calls per answer \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. proposed a calibrated multi-score detector with a cost analysis, but it relies on LLM scoring \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. combined retrieval, NLI, and XGBoost, but report only classification metrics \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME attributions to each word, but did not measure their reliability \cite{multimedia}. Deng et al. found that detectors degrade outside their training domain, but focus on multi-turn dialogue \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench shows that modern LLMs still produce summarization hallucinations, but does not measure detector calibration \cite{faithbench}.

The existing literature leaves four issues unaddressed. Calibration is infrequently reported for lightweight detectors, explanation quality is rarely measured, cross-domain behavior is rarely tested, and error patterns are rarely analyzed case by case. The conventional feature-based baseline also has limits. HaluEval is saturated and the score depends on answer length. Its calibration also breaks under shift. This paper addresses these gaps with a feature-based model that scores the output of a black-box LLM. It takes a query $q$, a reference context $c$, and a candidate answer $a$, and outputs $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$. EC-XGB combines 26 base features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator in a tuned XGBoost classifier. Platt calibration maps the score to a probability, SHAP explains feature contributions, and grouped splits with length and overlap controls reduce shortcut solutions. Calibration is fitted on validation rows only, and the target-calibration experiments measure the reliability problem. The claim-level aggregates support per-claim explanations, and the measured transfer gain comes from multi-source training rather than from those aggregates. Transfer limits are established on RAGTruth and FaithBench. The evaluation adds MedHallu \cite{pandit2025medhallu}, a HaluBench subset \cite{ravi2024lynx} with no training overlap, the HaluEval task shifts, conformal prediction, and judge stress tests. The added evidence separates ranking from calibration: the model transfers its ranking to medical pairs but not its probability, and it fails under a task shift.

HaluEval QA provides 10{,}000 question pairs, each with a correct and a hallucinated answer, for training and in-domain testing. RAGTruth provides natural responses for adaptation and recalibration. The target use case is question answering with a reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. The fixed-hyperparameter EC-XGB (m3) reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986 on HaluEval QA. RAGTruth shows the same pattern: the flag rate falls on the tasks seen in training, while held-out QA stays near chance. On MedHallu the same model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 pairs but flags every row. On HaluEval dialogue it flags almost every row, and on HaluEval summarization it ranks below a length-only model. Target calibration on 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, but it does not change the ranking. One answer takes 127.5~ms on GPU (2.00~s on CPU) at about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

This section describes the conventional feature-based detector and the criteria used to evaluate it. A hallucination is an answer that the reference context does not support, or that the context contradicts. Each row is a triple $(q_i,c_i,a_i)$ of question, reference context, and candidate answer, with label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. A detector is usually scored with precision, recall, F1, or the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) on labeled rows. A deployed detector faces two more demands. Calibration requires the predicted risk to match the observed frequency, and expected calibration error (ECE) summarizes the gap across confidence bins \cite{guo}. Explanation requires the warning to name the features that contributed to it, and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) provide that for a tabular model \cite{shap}. This work measures both properties on the black-box model.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 base features in Table~\ref{tab:features}, trained on HaluEval QA. XGBoost models nonlinear interactions and gives exact TreeSHAP values, which the explanation analysis needs. A published feature-based detector uses the same learner with retrieval and NLI features \cite{ijert}, so this model is the natural baseline. Trees are added one at a time, and each tree fits the current gradient and curvature of the loss. A row enters at the root of each tree, follows the split conditions to one leaf per tree, and the leaf weights are summed into a raw margin. A logistic link maps the raw margin to a probability.

Let $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{26}$ be the feature vector of row $i$, $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label, and $n$ the number of training rows. The model sums $K$ trees into a raw margin, $z_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)$. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, z_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $\phi = \{f_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ collects the trees, $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves and leaf-weight vector $w_k \in \mathbb{R}^{T_k}$, $\gamma$ is the penalty per leaf, and $\lambda$ is an L2 weight penalty. The loss $l(y_i,z_i)$ is the logistic loss on the raw margin. The probability is the sigmoid of the raw margin, $\hat{p}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$.

At boosting step $t$, a new tree $f_t$ with $T$ leaves and leaf weights $w_1,\dots,w_T$ is added, and the objective is approximated, up to a constant, by a second-order expansion around the current raw margin,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $g_i = \partial l(y_i,z)/\partial z$ and $h_i = \partial^2 l(y_i,z)/\partial z^2$ are evaluated at $z_i^{(t-1)}$, and $f_t(x_i) = w_{q(x_i)}$ is the leaf weight that row $i$ reaches, with $q(x_i)$ the leaf index. For the logistic loss, $g_i = \hat{p}_i - y_i$ and $h_i = \hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)$.

For a fixed tree structure, the optimal weight of leaf $j$ is
\begin{equation}
  w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda},
\end{equation}
where $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$ and $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$ are the sums over the rows $I_j$ that reach leaf $j$. The gain of a split is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda} \right] - \gamma,
\end{equation}
with $G_L$ and $H_L$ for the left child and $G_R$ and $H_R$ for the right child. A split is kept only when the gain is positive. The tree also stops at the maximum depth or when a leaf has fewer than the minimum child weight.

Depth, learning rate, tree count, and the row and column subsampling rates are tuned on the 14{,}000 training rows. Each seed runs its own 30-configuration search with stratified group five-fold cross-validation, and the grouping keeps both answers to the same question in one fold. The seed-42 values are in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}, and the per-seed values are in the repository. Each seed trains on the 14{,}000 rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows (30 rounds). The 300-tree setting is the tuned upper bound, and seed 42 stops at 209 trees. The headline comparison uses the uncalibrated probability at a fixed 0.5 threshold on the 3{,}000 test rows. Platt and isotonic maps are fitted on the same validation rows for the calibration study. The source-only P0 threshold is selected on those rows at a 5\% false-positive budget. The test rows are never used for tuning, early stopping, calibration, or threshold selection. The final step returns the mean over the three seeds. The search leaves $\gamma$, $\lambda$, and the minimum child weight at their XGBoost defaults.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and evaluation)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, test metrics, and Platt and isotonic maps
    \State extract the 26 base features for every row
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State tune XGBoost on the 14{,}000 training rows with 30 random configurations and stratified group 5-fold cross-validation
    \State train the classifier on the same rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows
    \State evaluate the raw score on the 3{,}000 test rows at the fixed 0.5 threshold
    \State fit Platt and isotonic maps on the validation rows for the calibration study
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Limitations and Research Gap}

The baseline has three method limits. The score depends on answer length, it has no claim-level evidence, and it uses a source-fitted calibrator. It also trains on HaluEval alone. Two evaluation limits sit beside them: HaluEval is saturated, and the source probability does not transfer to a new corpus \cite{ovadia2019}. Section~\ref{sec:proposed} addresses the method limits with log-scaled lengths and monotone constraints, eight claim-level aggregates, RAGTruth non-QA rows, and a source indicator. The calibration protocol addresses the shift limit, and the evaluation reports ranking, calibration, and paired errors rather than F1 alone. The log transform compresses the length scale but does not remove the cue. The gap is a risk score that keeps its ranking and probability when the corpus changes.

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Working Principle}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline. The system takes a question, a reference context, and a candidate answer, and extracts 35 features: 26 base features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator. EC-XGB maps the feature vector to a raw score, and a Platt calibrator fitted on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows maps the score to the displayed probability. The system reports the risk score, per-claim verdicts, and SHAP contributions. The lower band shows the deployment path: a Next.js front end calls a FastAPI service that loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. The rightmost block lists the evaluation corpora. HaluEval measures in-domain performance, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text measure adaptation to task types seen in training, and RAGTruth QA and FaithBench are held out.

The task is to estimate $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$ for a question $q$, reference context $c$ (if available), and a candidate answer $a$ from a black-box LLM. The target is not truthfulness in general but evidence consistency: whether $a$ follows from $c$ or contradicts it. The output is a probability, and a decision threshold turns it into a flag.

\subsection{Features}

The model uses 26 base features in seven groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and eight claim-level aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:claim}). NLI probabilities come from a DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder \cite{deberta} trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}, semantic similarity uses MiniLM embeddings \cite{sbert,minilm}, and entities come from spaCy \cite{spacy}. Length is included because HaluEval labels longer answers as hallucinated more often, so it is a strong but fragile signal. Lexical overlap captures copying and is the strongest SHAP feature, but it cannot detect a fluent paraphrase that contradicts the evidence. A novel entity is a useful warning sign, although a correct answer can introduce a new name. The NLI features are the only group that represents logical support rather than surface overlap. A changed number is a common and easy-to-detect hallucination. Hedging words are weak on their own but separate a cautious correct answer from a confident wrong one. Semantic similarity catches paraphrase and topic drift that lexical overlap misses. The eight claim aggregates repeat the NLI signals at the level of atomic claims, which is the level a reviewer checks. RAGChecker also uses entailment-based claim metrics \cite{ru2024ragchecker}.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB changes the conventional setup in three places. First, the character and word counts are log-scaled, and monotone constraints are placed on the evidence features. The constraints are negative on context overlap, context Jaccard, context entailment, context cosine, claim minimum and mean entailment, and the supported-claim ratio, and positive on context contradiction, novel-entity ratio, novel numbers, claim maximum and mean contradiction, and the contradicted-claim ratio. All other features are unconstrained. Second, the eight claim features in Table~\ref{tab:claim} are added: seven NLI aggregates and a claim count. They use the same NLI scorer and support-first rule as the verification layer. A claim is supported when any retained sentence has an entailment probability of at least 0.5 and at least as high as its contradiction probability. It is contradicted when no sentence supports it and a sentence has a contradiction probability of at least 0.5 and above its entailment probability. Otherwise it is unsupported.

Consider an answer with $K$ claims. Let $C(k)$ be the maximum contradiction probability of claim $k$ over its selected context sentences, $E(k)$ the maximum entailment probability, and $v_k$ its verdict. The aggregate vector is
\begin{equation}
  \psi(x) = [\max_k C(k), \overline{C}, r_{\text{con}}, r_{\text{sup}}, r_{\text{unsup}}, \min_k E(k), \overline{E}, K],
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  r_{\text{con}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{contradicted}], \quad
  r_{\text{sup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{supported}], \quad
  r_{\text{unsup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{unsupported}].
\end{equation}
Here $x=(q,c,a)$ is the input triplet, and $\overline{C}$ and $\overline{E}$ are the means of $C(k)$ and $E(k)$ over the claims. For $K>0$, the three ratios sum to one. The ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that the block adds no aggregate F1 gain. The model input has 35 dimensions: the 26 base features (two of them log-scaled), the eight claim features, and one corpus indicator. The claim count $K$ is kept because an answer with many claims is harder to verify.

Third, the training set adds the RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text rows. A binary corpus indicator is one for those rows and zero for HaluEval, so the classifier can separate the two sources. The classifier never sees RAGTruth QA rows, so its QA ranking is zero-shot. The display calibrator does see labeled QA rows below, so the display score is not zero-shot. The combination is called EC-XGB. Seed 42 is the deployed checkpoint, and the other seeds are a stability check.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecxgb} lists the procedure. Steps 1 and 2 build the feature matrix. Steps 3 to 9 repeat the fit for the three seeds: each repetition builds the 35-feature input, trains on HaluEval plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, fits the display calibrator on the 5{,}034 QA calibration rows, and selects the source and target thresholds. The QA rows stay out of classifier training, which keeps the QA ranking zero-shot.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require HaluEval QA training and validation rows, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text training rows, RAGTruth QA calibration rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}=\{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure seed-42 model and display calibrator, source and target thresholds, per-seed metrics
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim features for every row
    \State log-scale the character and word counts, and attach the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input, with the corpus indicator at zero for HaluEval and one for RAGTruth
    \State train XGBoost on the HaluEval rows plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of classifier training
    \State fit the Platt display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows
    \State select the source threshold on the HaluEval validation split
    \State select the target threshold on the QA calibration rows at a 5\% false-positive budget
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the seed-42 model and calibrator, both thresholds, and the per-seed metrics
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

Source calibration uses the HaluEval validation split. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid $p(x) = \sigma(A f(x) + B)$ to the uncalibrated probability $f(x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are fit by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing step function $g$ so that $p(x) = g(f(x))$ \cite{isotonic}. Both are compared with the uncalibrated probability on the HaluEval test split. The comparison reports expected calibration error over 10 equal-width bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the empirical positive rate and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$, and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. Post-hoc calibration degrades when the target distribution moves \cite{gasparin2024distance}.

The second protocol fits the calibrator on labeled target rows and evaluates it on a disjoint target test split. The deployed EC-XGB display score uses a Platt calibrator fitted on the 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows. Those rows are scored with the corpus indicator set to one, and the API uses the same value for every request. This target calibration needs labeled target rows and is not zero-shot. The source-only protocol (P0 in Table~\ref{tab:protocols}) fits both the calibrator and the threshold on the HaluEval validation split. The deployed calibrator is Platt. An earlier deployment fit compared Platt and isotonic on the test split, and Section~\ref{sec:results} records that choice.

A TreeExplainer computes exact SHAP values \cite{shap} for the raw XGBoost model. Global importance is the mean absolute SHAP value over a stratified test sample, and the explanation endpoint returns the five features with the largest absolute value for one prediction. Post-hoc attributions can be unstable even when the model is fixed \cite{kelodjou2024shap}, so the pilot evaluates them directly \cite{adebayo2018}. A 20-case pilot applies six operators: entity, number, and date replacement, support removal, context insertion, and clause shuffling. It reports the mean absolute score change, the fraction of large changes, the top-1 flip rate, and the Spearman rank correlation between the original and perturbed rankings.

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

The method is deployed in a web application with a Next.js front end and a FastAPI backend. The API loads the EC-XGB model, the Platt calibrator, the SHAP explainer, and the NLI, spaCy, and MiniLM feature models at startup. The deployed checkpoint is the 35-feature m3 model with seed 42. Section~\ref{sec:results} compares it with the conventional B2 checkpoint and with B6 m0, a 26-feature reference from the same tuning protocol that lacks the EC-XGB changes. Claims are split from the answer and checked with sentence-level NLI against the provided context, a local passage index, or a web search API. A claim is supported when any retained sentence has an entailment probability of at least 0.5 and at least as high as its contradiction probability. A contradiction decides only when no sentence supports the claim. A relevance gate drops sentences with no claim vocabulary, and the verdicts are shown per claim.

The contribution is the training and evaluation protocol, not a new learner. EC-XGB combines three standard changes: log-scaled length counts with monotone constraints, claim-level NLI aggregates for explanation, and multi-source training with a corpus indicator. Section~\ref{sec:results} measures what each change contributes. The claim aggregates are kept for per-claim explanation and do not add an aggregate F1 gain. \section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments were run on a Windows laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU, CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128, and 32\,GB of RAM. The code runs on Python 3.12 with scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. Each run can be reproduced because of saved split indices and centralized seeds. A randomized search was performed over 30 hyperparameter configurations with grouped five-fold cross-validation. For the seed-42 conventional model, the configuration has a maximum tree depth of 5, a learning rate of 0.05, 300 trees, a subsampling ratio of 0.7, and a column sampling ratio of 0.7. Each seed is tuned separately for the main comparison, and the component ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} holds one configuration fixed across variants so that the feature effects are not confounded with tuning. Seeds 42, 123, and 456 are repeated where a seed applies; the single-seed tables are labeled.

\subsection{Dataset}

HaluEval QA \cite{haluEval} provides 10{,}000 questions, each with a knowledge passage, a correct answer, and a hallucinated answer, so 20{,}000 labeled rows. The split is 70/15/15 at the item level, giving 14{,}000/3{,}000/3{,}000 rows, so both answers to the same question stay in one split. HaluEval is entirely ChatGPT-generated \cite{haluEval,bang-etal-2025-hallulens}, so the boundary between a factual error and a hallucination is not always clean. RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth} supplies natural responses with human span annotations, and FaithBench \cite{faithbench} supplies modern-LLM summarization hallucinations. RAGTruth keeps its official document-level split: summarization has 4{,}758 training and 900 test rows, data-to-text has 5{,}298 and 900, and QA has 5{,}934 rows in 989 groups, with 900 test rows in 150 groups and 5{,}034 calibration rows. The three 900-row test splits are held out, giving a pooled 2{,}700-row test set. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the corpora and their positive rates.

Two further corpora and a task-shift pair test external validity without fitting or threshold selection. MedHallu pqa\_labeled \cite{pandit2025medhallu} gives 1{,}000 medical QA pairs with human-annotated gold answers and paired LLM-written hallucinations (2{,}000 rows, 50.0\% positive). HaluBench \cite{ravi2024lynx} contributes a stratified 30\% sample of its DROP, FinanceBench, and covidQA rows: 900 rows, 300 per source, balanced at 150 PASS and 150 FAIL. Its HaluEval, RAGTruth, and PubMedQA rows are excluded. The labels are HaluBench PASS/FAIL decisions, and the DROP and FinanceBench rows were converted to QA by the HaluBench authors. A seeded sample of 1{,}000 pairs from each HaluEval task gives 2{,}000 rows per task at 50.0\% positive. This is a task-shift test, not a second independent QA corpus.

Leading and trailing whitespace is trimmed, and lexical matching is case-folded. NER and NLI read the original text, and entity strings are lowercased for matching. A missing context is a special case: lexical overlap is 0 and NLI probabilities are uniform. In m1 and later variants, the two length counts are log1p-transformed; m0 and the conventional model keep raw counts. Claims are split into at most six clause-level segments. When the context has more than four sentences, each claim is matched to its four best sentences by token overlap, and each side of a pair is truncated to 400 characters. HaluEval is split 70/15/15 by question item, RAGTruth keeps its official document-level split, and both index sets are frozen. A RAGTruth answer is positive when it carries at least one annotated hallucination span. FaithBench severity annotations are reduced by a worst-severity rule, and a Questionable annotation counts as positive. HaluEval uses its native label. NLI and SBERT inference ran on the reference GPU in fp16, while lexical and entity features run on the CPU.

The feature-based logistic baselines standardize their inputs on the training split; the TF-IDF models use sparse features, and XGBoost does not scale. The source indicator is 1 for FaithBench and MedHallu and 0 for HaluBench and the task-shift sets. FaithBench and RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text have no question, so question overlap is 0 and question similarity is the cosine between the answer and the encoded empty string. Licenses and the data-availability statement are in the Declarations.

\subsection{Baselines and Experimental Procedure}

Twelve models are tested. The heuristic sets risk to one minus lexical overlap, and a length-only logistic control uses the two log-scaled length counts. Logistic regression and a 300-tree random forest are the classical comparators. The conventional XGBoost model \cite{xgboost} uses the 26 base features. Three further controls isolate the feature design: NLI-only, answer-only TF-IDF, and full-input TF-IDF. A majority classifier, a fine-tuned RoBERTa-base \cite{liu2019roberta}, and a reduced reimplementation of the lightweight ensemble of Sundaragiri et al.\ \cite{ijert} complete the set; the two judges are in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Internal-state detectors are outside the black-box setting \cite{inside2024}. The RoBERTa baseline receives up to 256 tokens and trains for three epochs.

EC-XGB's display calibrator is fitted on the RAGTruth QA split; the in-domain Platt calibrator is fitted on validation. Metrics are reported to four decimals and rates to one. Significance uses McNemar on seed-42 predictions, 2,000 two-level bootstrap resamples, and Wilcoxon across seeds. No question appears in both training and evaluation. Calibration is compared on raw, Platt, and isotonic scores; Table~\ref{tab:protocols} defines P0, P1, and P2, and every protocol uses the same test rows.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Two families of metrics are reported. Threshold-dependent metrics measure the decision at a fixed cut-off, and threshold-free metrics measure how well a model ranks answers. Both are needed because a model can rank well and still make poor decisions when the threshold is fixed. Precision is the share of flagged answers that are truly hallucinated. Recall is the share of hallucinated answers that the detector catches, and F1 is their harmonic mean. The false-positive rate is the share of correct answers that are flagged, which is the operating constraint under distribution shift. The flag rate is the share of answers above the decision threshold, and a rate near 100\% means every answer reaches a human reviewer.
\begin{gather}
  P = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
  R = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
  F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}, \\
  \mathrm{FPR} = \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{TN}}, \qquad
  \mathrm{Flag} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}_i \ge \tau].
\end{gather}

The second group measures ranking, calibration, and paired error patterns. AUROC is the probability that a randomly chosen hallucinated answer receives a higher score than a randomly chosen correct answer, with 0.5 at chance. PR-AUC is the area under the precision-recall curve, and it is more sensitive than AUROC when the positive class is rare. RAGTruth QA has a positive rate of 17.8\%, so PR-AUC is reported beside AUROC. MCC uses all four cells of the confusion matrix and ranges from $-1$ to $1$, with zero at chance. The Wilson interval gives a 95\% range for a proportion and is used for the P2 recall and false-positive rate, where the counts are small.
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{AUROC} = \int_{0}^{1} \mathrm{TPR}(u)\, d\,\mathrm{FPR}(u) = P(s^{+} > s^{-}), \\
  \mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} P(r)\, dr, \qquad
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}\cdot\mathrm{TN} - \mathrm{FP}\cdot\mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}, \\
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\end{gather}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the HaluEval test results for eleven baselines and EC-XGB, averaged over three seeds where a seed applies. The F1 ranking is tight: the random forest is 0.0004 behind the conventional model, and EC-XGB is 0.0009 ahead. That lead is much smaller than the width of the conventional seed-42 bootstrap interval [0.9812, 0.9895], so the ordering is not resolved by the data. The benchmark is saturated, and the differences are small. The ranking columns tell the same story. EC-XGB is first among the feature-based models at AUROC 0.9984 and PR-AUC 0.9987, with the conventional model and random forest within 0.0006; RoBERTa is higher at 0.9990 and 0.9993. Calibration is separate: the conventional model has the lowest ECE, but its Brier score is higher than EC-XGB and RoBERTa, so the two summaries disagree. The weak controls are NLI-only and full-input TF-IDF at 0.6714 and 0.5999 F1; the answer-only TF-IDF model reaches 0.9224, and adding the question and context hurts. The reduced external ensemble reaches F1 0.9517 and AUROC 0.9850; the fixed-hp m3 model is higher by 0.0337 F1 [0.0262, 0.0419] and 0.0135 AUROC [0.0098, 0.0177], with ECE 0.0078 against 0.0141. The gap is consistent with the nonlinear learner and wider feature set, but the baseline is reduced and trained on fewer rows, so the comparison is descriptive.

The XGBoost margin over the random forest is 0.0004 F1, with McNemar $p=0.502$; the difference is not significant. The margin over logistic regression is 0.0187 F1 ($p=1.86\times10^{-10}$) and over the heuristic 0.0495 ($p=2.28\times10^{-30}$). A source-only encoder wins in-domain (F1 0.9912 vs 0.9855) but is near chance on each RAGTruth task (AUROC 0.458 and 0.491 vs 0.582 for EC-XGB); pooling gives 0.337 because it mixes between-task score shifts with within-task ranking. An adapted encoder on the same multi-source rows reaches 0.694 on the seen tasks, above EC-XGB's 0.582, so the seen-task gain is not specific to the feature model. Neither model generalizes to unseen QA (0.490 vs 0.549), and FaithBench is worse for both (0.434 vs 0.594). Adaptation helps any model on seen tasks; the feature model keeps its relative ordering on unseen tasks.

\subsection{Calibration, Shift, and External Validity}

The raw, Platt-scaled, and isotonic versions of the conventional model give ECE 0.0045, 0.0091, and 0.0070 on HaluEval, so the calibrated ECEs are not lower in this sample. Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} shows why. On HaluEval, the points at the two ends sit on the diagonal; the middle of the score range holds few distinct values, so the curve has only a few stable bins. The Platt and isotonic curves overlap the raw curve at both ends. The m3 raw score is also well calibrated in-domain, with ECE 0.0078, so there is little room for calibration to improve on HaluEval.

Platt scaling fits a two-parameter logistic map from the raw score $s$ to a probability,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p} = \sigma(a s + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
  \label{eq:platt}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the calibration slope and $b$ is the intercept. A slope below one means the raw score is overconfident, and a slope near one means the scale is already usable. The Brier skill score compares the calibrated score with a constant predictor at the evaluation prevalence,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{prior}}}.
  \label{eq:bss}
\end{equation}
A positive BSS means the score beats the prior, and a non-positive BSS means it does not.

For the conventional model, the RAGTruth QA histogram in Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} shows almost every row above 0.99, at a mean confidence of 0.998 against an accuracy of 0.178. Source Platt leaves the confidence near 0.99. The m3 score behaves the same way. The fixed-hp m3 raw score is severely overconfident: ECE 0.731 and Brier 0.689, with a Cox slope of 0.18 on the 5{,}034 calibration rows. Source Platt cannot repair a base-rate shift. Target Platt lowers ECE to 0.125 and Brier to 0.161, but the Brier skill score against the test prior is $-$0.10. A constant predictor at the test prevalence has Brier 0.146, lower than every calibrated row, and flags no rows at 0.5. Calibration fixes the scale, not the ranking; AUROC stays at 0.558. The conventional target-Platt row behaves the same way at ECE 0.134 and Brier 0.164. The label budget is the practical constraint, and it is a distribution rather than a point.

The source-only operating point (P0) selects the threshold on the HaluEval validation split at a 5\% false-positive budget. On the HaluEval test split, recall there is 99.0--99.5\% and the false-positive rate stays near 5\%. On RAGTruth QA the same source threshold flags 97.4\% of the tuned checkpoint, so P0 is not a usable target operating point. Table~\ref{tab:target} reports both models on the same 900-row RAGTruth QA test set, whose prevalence is 17.8\%. The calibration split has 31.1\% positives. A prevalence-only predictor set to the test prevalence, 0.1778, shows what a constant score can achieve. On the test rows it reaches ECE 0.000 and Brier 0.146, and it predicts no positives at the 0.5 threshold. At the calibration prevalence of 0.3107 the same predictor gives ECE 0.133 and Brier 0.164, which matches the conventional target-Platt row. The calibrator therefore sets the level to the calibration prevalence, and the residual ECE is dominated by the prevalence gap. At the test prevalence of 0.1778 a constant predictor has Brier 0.146, lower than the target-calibrated rows (0.161--0.164). Figure~\ref{fig:calibshift} shows the raw scores and the target-recalibrated scores for the conventional model. Calibration fixes the scale, not the ranking. 

The conventional target-thresholded mode (P2) selects a threshold at a 5\% false-positive budget (0.313) on the calibration split and reaches a test recall of 5.0\% at a false-positive rate of 5.8\% (precision 15.7\%, F1 0.076, Wilson 95\% intervals: recall [2.6, 9.6], FPR [4.3, 7.7]). The test rate exceeds the 5\% target because the threshold is fixed on calibration. At 17.8\% prevalence, random flagging at a 5.8\% false-positive rate yields 5.8\% recall, so the P2 result is chance-level. 

At the 0.5 threshold, P1 flags no RAGTruth QA rows, and the maximum target-Platt score (0.313) is below the API's lowest risk band (0.35); the deployed card therefore relies on the per-claim verdict adjustment, not on the pre-verdict score alone. All calibration metrics in this section refer to the pre-verdict display score. The card value is adjusted by claim verdicts and is not covered by these ECEs; the displayed percentage is a design aid, not a validated probability.

Table~\ref{tab:conformal} reports the coverage. Split conformal prediction adds a coverage guarantee to the score. The nonconformity score for label $y$ is $s(x,y)=1-\hat{p}_y(x)$, where $\hat{p}_1=\hat{p}$ and $\hat{p}_0=1-\hat{p}$. The calibration quantile and the prediction set are
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q} = \mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\left(\{s(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}\right),
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}\}.
  \label{eq:conformal}
\end{equation}
A class-conditional version replaces the single quantile with one quantile per label,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q}_y = \mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\left(\{s(x_i,y_i) : y_i = y\}\right),
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}_y\}.
  \label{eq:conformal_class}
\end{equation}
The main run fits the Platt map and the quantile on the same 5{,}034 rows, so its calibration coverage is a construction check. A disjoint sensitivity fits Platt on 2{,}514 rows and the quantile on 2{,}520 rows. On that split, the 900 QA test rows reach 0.944 coverage at 90\%, so the marginal guarantee holds. It hides the class split: positive coverage is 0.685, while negative coverage is 1.000. A class-conditional quantile raises positive coverage to 0.887, still slightly below nominal. FaithBench coverage falls to 0.320 and positive coverage to 0.003, because a Platt map fitted on QA sends almost every FaithBench score to the negative side. Conformal prediction therefore certifies the marginal rate only when the calibrator and the quantile are fitted on disjoint rows, and it does not transfer across the task shift. The per-class rate is the one a reviewer needs when positives are 17.8\% of the target.

Table~\ref{tab:external} reports the three external corpora and the task-shift pair. MedHallu is the strongest evidence that the ranking survives a domain change and the clearest evidence that the probability does not. The fixed-hp m3 model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded answer in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 medical pairs [84.5, 88.7], with AUROC 0.821 [0.806, 0.837] on the 2{,}000 rows. The pair accuracy over $M$ pairs is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PA} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\left[\hat{p}(a_m^{+}) > \hat{p}(a_m^{-})\right],
  \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
where $a_m^{+}$ is the hallucinated answer and $a_m^{-}$ the grounded answer. The conventional m0 model reaches 82.2\% and 0.781. The length-only baseline is inverted, at 20.0\% and 0.260, because grounded MedHallu answers are longer than the paired hallucinations (39.7 vs 27.8 words), the opposite of HaluEval (2.2 vs 11.0). The ranking therefore transfers, but the scale does not: m3 flags every row, so its F1 is the all-positive 0.667 and its ECE is 0.493. The source indicator is one for MedHallu and zero for HaluBench; a source-zero sensitivity on MedHallu changes pair accuracy by at most 0.003 and AUROC by at most 0.002, so the indicator does not drive the result. HaluBench is a 30\% stratified sample of the 3{,}000 non-overlapping rows. Its overall AUROC is 0.600, with covidQA 0.833, DROP 0.513, and FinanceBench 0.527; the model transfers to the biomedical subset but is at chance on the other two. The length-only baseline is at 0.514 overall, so 0.600 is not a length artifact.

The task-shift test is the weakest. On dialogue, m3 flags 99.3\% of rows and its F1 equals the all-positive baseline, with AUROC 0.763; the length-only baseline reaches 0.724. On summarization, m3 reaches AUROC 0.717 and flags 37.5\%, while the length-only baseline reaches 0.788 and flags every row. The conventional m0 model flags 99.2\% of dialogue rows and 97.7\% of summarization rows, with AUROC 0.741 and 0.605. On dialogue, both models collapse to the all-positive baseline in F1, though their AUROCs stay above chance; on summarization, both rank below the length-only model. This is a task-shift test, not a second independent QA corpus.

An index stress test asks whether the weak held-out QA result is a retrieval problem. The hybrid BM25 and FAISS index covers the 989 RAGTruth QA source documents. On a 300-row, 50-group sample, recall@1 is 0.94, recall@5 and recall@10 are 1.00, and MRR is 0.959. Replacing the gold context with the top-5 retrieved passages changes AUROC from 0.545 to 0.521, F1 from 0.345 to 0.349, and ECE from 0.706 to 0.704. The AUROC delta is $-$0.023 [$-0.061$, 0.011], which includes zero. Retrieval is therefore not the bottleneck: the model is near chance on RAGTruth QA even with the gold source passage. The qrels are the known source passage, not human web-search judgments, so this measures index recall, not end-to-end search quality. The sample has 50 source groups, so the interval is wide.

In each external panel, both classes pile against 1.0, so the raw score no longer separates hallucinated from correct answers. The 0.5 threshold therefore flags almost every row. This is the visual form of the 99.9\% flag rate in Table~\ref{tab:shift}.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Statistical tests use the paired fixed-hyperparameter predictions and 2{,}000 two-level bootstrap draws, where groups are resampled within each seed and then seeds are resampled. McNemar's test compares the discordant counts $b$ and $c$ on the paired seed-42 rows, where $b$ is the number of rows where the first model is right and the second is wrong, and $c$ is the reverse; the statistic uses a continuity correction. Holm's method controls the family-wise error rate across the four baseline comparisons. The 95\% interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the paired bootstrap deltas. The two-level version resamples groups within each seed before resampling seeds. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares the two models across the three seeds.

Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} gives the McNemar counts. EC-XGB and the conventional model are not significantly different under the deployed checkpoints ($p=0.45$), and the fixed-hyperparameter ablation gives $p=0.789$ on the same test rows. That is the primary comparison, and it is not part of the Holm family, which covers the four baseline comparisons against the conventional model. The random forest matches the conventional model ($p=0.502$). Logistic regression and the heuristic are significantly worse ($p=1.86\times10^{-10}$ and $p=2.28\times10^{-30}$), and both survive Holm correction. The Wilcoxon test across three seeds cannot reach significance (minimum $p=0.25$). Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports the two-level bootstrap. On HaluEval, the m3$-$m0 F1 delta is $+$0.0006 [$-0.0010$, 0.0021], which includes zero, and the AUROC delta is $+$0.0007 [0.0002, 0.0012], which excludes zero by a small margin. The m2$-$m1 F1 delta is $-$0.0004 [$-0.0020$, 0.0012], and the m3$-$m1 delta is $+$0.0001 [$-0.0015$, 0.0018], so the claim features do not add an aggregate gain. Their AUROC deltas are $+$0.0009 and $+$0.0010, both with intervals above zero, so the added features reorder a few rows without changing F1. 

On held-out RAGTruth QA the m3$-$m0 F1 delta is $+$0.0076 [0.0045, 0.0109], but the AUROC delta is $+$0.0017 [$-0.0270$, 0.0291], which includes zero. The F1 gain comes from flagging almost every row, not from better ranking. On the pooled seen tasks the F1 delta is $+$0.0451 [0.0348, 0.0554] and the AUROC delta is $+$0.0962 [0.0644, 0.1295], which is the adaptation gain. The paired fixed-minus-tuned contrasts all include zero, so the rebuild does not change these conclusions.

\subsection{Explanation, Efficiency, and Judges}

The explanation results use the fixed-hyperparameter m3 seed-42 checkpoint. Figure~\ref{fig:shapimp} ranks the top-15 features by mean absolute SHAP over 1{,}000 stratified HaluEval test rows. Context overlap dominates, followed by word count, question-answer cosine, character count, and question-answer Jaccard; the claim-contradiction mean enters at rank eight. The top ten features carry 77.9\% of the total attribution mass, and exact TreeSHAP reproduces the model margin to $9.2\times10^{-6}$. The m3 ranking agrees with the frozen B2 ranking (Spearman $\rho=0.878$, Kendall $\tau=0.730$), so the claim features refine the explanation rather than replace the original drivers. Attribution stability across seeds is high: against seed 42, Spearman is 0.972 for seed 123 and 0.950 for seed 456, top-10 overlap is nine of ten, and the top-5 set is identical. The explanation is stable to the seed, although the perturbation pilot below shows that it is less stable to content edits. The attributions are on the raw margin, not the display score. The audit cases are error-enriched, and their roles change under m3; they are discussed below.

Table~\ref{tab:perturb} shows the m3 pilot. Content edits move the score (entity replacement, support removal, number replacement, date replacement), while structural edits barely move it (irrelevant insert 0.003, clause shuffle 0.0001). The top-1 feature flips in 54.5\% of entity perturbations and in none of the other operators, and Spearman correlation stays between 0.73 and 0.999. The entity flip rate is higher than in the B2 run, so the m3 explanation is less stable when the answer entity changes. The pilot has between two and twenty changed cases per operator, so these values are descriptive, not effect estimates.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Discussion}

On the seed-42 fixed-hyperparameter run, m3 makes 43 errors on the HaluEval test set: 8 false positives and 35 false negatives, an error rate of 1.4\%. The WP5 audit covers all eight false positives and 12 of the 35 false negatives, chosen to cover the codebook rather than sampled at random. The 20-case draft assigns 13 content errors and seven audit artifacts. These counts describe the reviewed cases, not the 3{,}000-row test population. A separate 50-case sheet covers the eight threshold false positives, the 17 highest-scoring negatives, and 25 false negatives; the three authors verified 31 artifact cases (22 grounded over-flags, 7 label-noise, 2 short-answer) and 19 content cases across 12 categories. Table~\ref{tab:length} shows why length matters: the hallucination rate rises from 2.1\% for one-word answers to 90.2\% at five to eight words and 99.2\% at 17+ words. False positives concentrate in the five-to-eight bin, false negatives in the short bins, so length is a near-perfect label indicator on HaluEval.

Four controls test the length explanation. A logistic regression on the two length variables reaches F1 0.9457 and AUROC 0.9759. A reweighting control equalizes six length bins with 247 examples each; on that set EC-XGB reaches F1 0.9867 and AUROC 0.9987, against 0.9475 and 0.9758 for a length-only baseline, a gain of +0.039 F1 and +0.023 AUROC. A length-matching control pairs one positive and one negative at each length, giving 79 pairs (158 examples) over 1{,}000 bootstraps. EC-XGB holds AUROC 0.956 while the length-only baseline falls to 0.521, a point gap of 0.435 (bootstrap mean 0.436 [0.351, 0.522]); the F1 point gap is 0.431 (bootstrap mean 0.440 [0.334, 0.552]). The length cue explains the zero-shot failure but not the in-domain ranking.

Figure~\ref{fig:length} shows the same pattern. The shape explains the zero-shot result: complete sentences are long, so the conventional model reads them as hallucinations. Outside the benchmark, EC-XGB ranks the seen RAGTruth tasks better and the display calibrator gives readable target scores. The ablation leaves lexical and length as the only groups that matter, so the in-domain model is dominantly a length and overlap detector; that is why the conventional model flags 99.9\% of natural long answers and EC-XGB still flags 61.3\%, calibration cannot repair the ranking, and held-out QA stays near 0.55. The external comparison is limited by protocol: no published HaluEval QA result uses the grouped split, a supervised 13B agent reports F1 0.724 on 100 rows \cite{haluagent}, and the benchmark's zero-shot judges report 62.6\% to 69.8\% accuracy \cite{haluEval}. Those settings differ in supervision, evidence access, and test rows, so they are context rather than a head-to-head bar. The benchmark's generation instruction asks for hallucinated answers about five words longer; the observed gap is 8.5 words, and a length-only logistic regression reaches AUROC 0.976 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Any comparison that ignores this construction artifact overstates semantic detection.

Five limits bound these claims. Web-search retrieval quality and end-to-end verdict accuracy are not evaluated; the retrieval stress test uses the known source passage. The judge comparison uses 900 RAGTruth QA rows at natural prevalence and the 158-row length-matched set, which controls length but not domain. The audit is error-enriched. HaluEval is saturated, so its in-domain differences are not resolvable. The deployed display calibrator was selected in an earlier fit that compared Platt and isotonic on the test split; the calibration analysis in Table~\ref{tab:target} refits Platt without test-based selection.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This study tested whether a simple feature model can estimate hallucination risk and how its calibration and ranking change under domain and task shift. EC-XGB is an evidence-consistent XGBoost risk predictor with 26 base features, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator, trained on multi-source rows and paired with calibration and SHAP explanations. On HaluEval QA the fixed-hp model reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986, but lexical overlap and length carry that score: removing them costs 0.0171 and 0.0066 F1, and removing the claim block costs 0.0010. The in-domain score is therefore mostly a length and overlap effect. On RAGTruth the tuned model cuts the pooled flag rate from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, but the fixed-hp model still flags 96.8\% of held-out QA at AUROC 0.558. Target Platt calibration lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, but flags no target rows at 0.5 and loses to a constant predictor on Brier score. Split conformal covers 94.4\% at 90\%, but positive coverage is only 0.685. On MedHallu the model ranks the hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 pairs (AUROC 0.821), but flags every row and is badly calibrated (ECE 0.493). The ranking survives the domain change, but the probability does not. Dialogue collapses to an all-positive flag rate, and summarization ranks below a length-only model. One answer takes 127.5\,ms on GPU and costs about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific risk score, not a general-purpose detector. Future work should test adaptation to unseen QA domains, extend multicalibration over length and claim-count slices, and select thresholds under prevalence shift.
