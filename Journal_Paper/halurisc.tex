\begin{abstract}
  Large language models answer fluently, but some answers are not supported by the evidence. Detectors based on a large judge model, an encoder, or model internals are expensive and rarely report calibration under domain or task shift. Feature-based detectors can be cheaper. Their calibration and sensitivity to answer length are usually not analyzed together. This work investigates whether a simple feature model can predict hallucination risk and how its ranking and calibration are affected by shift. This work introduces EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent XGBoost model with base features, claim-level natural language inference (NLI) aggregate scores, and a source indicator. The model is trained on HaluEval QA and RAGTruth non-QA rows with grouped splits and three seeds, and analyzed with fixed-hyperparameter ablations, source and target calibration, conformal prediction, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) stability analysis, a length-only control model, and judge stress tests. A feature model is cheap and interpretable, and its inputs can be observed directly, making it a feasible choice for routine screening. On HaluEval QA, the fixed-hyperparameter model reaches F1 0.985 and AUROC 0.999, but lexical overlap and length carry that score. On MedHallu, it ranks the hallucinated answer higher than the grounded answer in 86.7\% of 1,000 pairs, but flags every row and is poorly calibrated (ECE 0.493). On RAGTruth QA, the model stays close to chance. Target calibration lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, but flags no target rows at the default threshold. Split conformal prediction reaches 0.944 coverage at 90\%, with only 0.685 positive coverage. The dialogue dataset is almost completely flagged, summarization ranks lower than a length-only model, and a GPT judge beats the feature model on natural QA. Hence, EC-XGB is a domain-specific risk score, not a general-purpose detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Black-box detectors rely on the evaluation of a candidate answer in context \cite{selfcheck,luna,farquhar2024semantic,ieeeTai}. They incur heavy compute costs, while encoder models are hard to interpret. Two recent reviews classify detectors \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey}. Evaluation and reliability of detectors remain open questions. The latter is identified independently in the third survey \cite{alansari2026survey}.

HaluAgent showed that small models could pick tools, though it required several calls per answer \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. designed a calibrated multi-score detector with a cost analysis, but it is based on LLM scoring \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. proposed a retrieval, natural language inference (NLI), and XGBoost solution, but reported only classification metrics \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME explanations to words but did not measure their reliability \cite{multimedia}. In Deng et al.'s study of multi-turn dialogue, detector performance deteriorated outside the training domain \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench shows that current LLMs still produce summarization hallucinations, but it does not measure detector calibration \cite{faithbench}.

Thus, four open problems remain in existing research. First, the calibration of lightweight detectors is rarely evaluated. Second, explanation quality is evaluated even less often. Third, few models have been evaluated on cross-domain behavior. Finally, error-pattern analysis on a per-example basis remains rare. The existing conventional feature-based benchmark also has several issues: it depends on answer length, does not give claim-level explanations, trains exclusively on HaluEval with a source-fitted calibrator, and lacks transferable probabilities \cite{ovadia2019}. HaluEval is saturated as well. There is still a gap in the form of a risk score that maintains rank and probability when the corpus changes. This paper fills these gaps with a feature-based model that scores a black-box LLM output. Given a query $q$, a reference context $c$, and a candidate answer $a$, it outputs $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$. The EC-XGB model combines 26 features divided into seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator inside a tuned XGBoost classifier. The score is mapped to a probability with Platt calibration, and explanations are produced with Shapley additive explanations (SHAP). Grouped splits with length and overlap controls reduce shortcut learning. Source calibration is fitted on validation data only. Target-calibration experiments check the reliability of predictions. The log transform compresses the length scale but keeps the cue. The claim aggregates support per-claim explanations, but the seen-task gain on RAGTruth comes from the multi-source rows and source indicator, not the claim aggregates. Transfer limits are established on RAGTruth and FaithBench. The evaluation adds MedHallu \cite{pandit2025medhallu}, a HaluBench subset \cite{ravi2024lynx} with no training overlap, the HaluEval task shifts, conformal prediction, and judge stress tests. It reports F1, ranking, calibration, and paired errors, separating ranking from calibration: the model transfers ranking to medical pairs but not its probability, and it fails under the task shift.

HaluEval QA provides 10,000 question pairs, each with a correct and a hallucinated answer, for training and in-domain testing. RAGTruth provides natural responses for adaptation and recalibration. The target application is question answering with a reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. The fixed-hyperparameter EC-XGB stack (m3) reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986 on HaluEval QA. RAGTruth shows the same pattern: the flag rate falls on tasks seen in training, while held-out QA stays near chance. On MedHallu, the same model ranks the hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1,000 pairs but flags every row. On HaluEval dialogue it flags almost every row, and on HaluEval summarization it ranks below a length-only model. Target calibration on 5,034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125 without changing the ranking. One answer takes 127.5~ms on GPU (2.00~s on CPU), and the approximate cost is about \$0.001 per 1,000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

This section covers the traditional feature-based detector along with its evaluation metrics. The hallucinations are the answers that cannot be supported by the reference context or contradict it. Every data row is in the form of a tuple $(q_i,c_i,a_i)$ comprising the question, reference context, and candidate answer with the label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. The traditional way of evaluating the detector is to use any one of the four metrics: precision, recall, F1, or AUROC of the labeled tuples. There are two additional requirements that must be satisfied by the deployed detector. Calibration demands that the risk score should match the observed frequency of the positive outcome, which is summarized by ECE \cite{guo}. Explanation means that the detector must point out the features that contributed to the warning, which is provided by SHAP for tabular models \cite{shap}.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 base features in Table~\ref{tab:features}, trained on HaluEval QA. XGBoost models nonlinear interactions and gives exact TreeSHAP values, which the explanation analysis needs. A published feature-based detector uses the same learner with retrieval and NLI features \cite{ijert}, so this model is the natural baseline. Trees are added one at a time, and each tree fits the current gradient and curvature of the loss. A row enters at the root of each tree, follows the split conditions to one leaf per tree, and the leaf weights are summed into a raw margin. A logistic link maps the raw margin to a probability.

Let $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{26}$ be the feature vector of row $i$, $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label, and $n$ the number of training rows. The model sums $K$ trees into a raw margin, $z_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)$. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, z_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $\phi = \{f_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ collects the trees, $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves and leaf-weight vector $w_k \in \mathbb{R}^{T_k}$, $\gamma$ is the penalty per leaf, and $\lambda$ is an L2 weight penalty. The loss $l(y_i,z_i)$ is the logistic loss on the raw margin. The probability is the sigmoid of the raw margin, $\hat{p}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$.

At boosting step $t$, a new tree $f_t$ with $T$ leaves and leaf weights $w_1,\dots,w_T$ is added, and the objective is approximated, up to a constant, by a second-order expansion around the current raw margin,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $g_i = \partial l(y_i,z)/\partial z$ and $h_i = \partial^2 l(y_i,z)/\partial z^2$ are evaluated at $z_i^{(t-1)}$, and $f_t(x_i) = w_{q(x_i)}$ is the leaf weight that row $i$ reaches, with $q(x_i)$ the leaf index. For the logistic loss, $g_i = \hat{p}_i - y_i$ and $h_i = \hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)$.

For a fixed tree structure, the optimal weight of leaf $j$ is
\begin{equation}
  w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda},
\end{equation}
where $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$ and $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$ are the sums over the rows $I_j$ that reach leaf $j$. The gain of a split is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda} \right] - \gamma,
\end{equation}
with $G_L$ and $H_L$ for the left child and $G_R$ and $H_R$ for the right child. A split is kept only when the gain is positive. The tree also stops at the maximum depth or when a leaf has fewer than the minimum child weight.

Depth, learning rate, tree count, and the row and column subsampling rates are tuned on the 14{,}000 training rows. Each seed runs its own 30-configuration search with stratified group five-fold cross-validation, and the grouping keeps both answers to the same question in one fold. The seed-42 values are in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}, and the per-seed values are in the repository. Each seed trains on the 14{,}000 rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows (30 rounds). The 300-tree setting is the tuned upper bound, and seed 42 stops at 209 trees. The headline comparison uses the uncalibrated probability at a fixed 0.5 threshold on the 3{,}000 test rows. Platt and isotonic maps are fitted on the same validation rows for the calibration study. The source-only P0 threshold is selected on those rows at a 5\% false-positive budget. The test rows are never used for tuning, early stopping, calibration, or threshold selection. The final step returns the mean over the three seeds. The search leaves $\gamma$, $\lambda$, and the minimum child weight at their XGBoost defaults. Algorithm~\ref{alg:baseline} summarizes the tuning, training, and calibration steps.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and evaluation)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, test metrics, and Platt and isotonic maps
    \State extract the 26 base features for every row
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State tune XGBoost on the 14{,}000 training rows with 30 random configurations and stratified group 5-fold cross-validation
    \State train the classifier on the same rows with early stopping on the 3{,}000 validation rows
    \State evaluate the raw score on the 3{,}000 test rows at the fixed 0.5 threshold
    \State fit Platt and isotonic maps on the validation rows for the calibration study
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Working Principle}

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{revision/architecture-flow-large.png}
  \caption{End-to-end pipeline, deployment path, and evaluation corpora of EC-XGB.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}

The aim is to predict $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$, where $q$ is the input question, $c$ is the optional context, and $a$ is the answer generated by the LLM. Truthfulness is not the concern here, but rather the assessment of evidence consistency, that is, whether $a$ is entailed by $c$ or contradicts $c$. The output of the system is a probability value, which is translated to a label through the decision threshold. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline used to calculate the probability. The pipeline takes the inputs and extracts 35 features: 26 base features split into seven groups, eight aggregate NLI scores at the claim level, and a source indicator. EC-XGB takes the features and generates a raw score, which is calibrated by a Platt calibrator trained on RAGTruth QA calibration rows to obtain the displayed probability. The model outputs a risk score, verdicts at the claim level, and SHAP values. The bottom part of the figure shows the deployment pipeline, where the Next.js front end calls the FastAPI service, which loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. The right block lists the evaluation datasets. HaluEval evaluates in-domain performance, while RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text measure performance on task types seen in training. RAGTruth QA and FaithBench are held out.

\subsection{Features}

The model uses 26 base features in seven groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and eight claim-level aggregates (Table~\ref{tab:claim}). NLI probabilities come from the DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder \cite{deberta} trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}. Semantic similarity is measured with MiniLM embeddings \cite{sbert,minilm}, and entities are found through spaCy named entity recognition (NER) \cite{spacy}. Length is used since HaluEval labels longer answers as hallucinated more often, making length a powerful but fragile feature. Lexical overlap indicates copying and is the strongest SHAP feature; nevertheless, it does not detect a fluent paraphrase that contradicts the evidence. A novel entity is a useful warning sign, although a new name can be part of a correct answer. The NLI features are the only group that provides logical support and not just overlap. A changed number is a common and easy-to-detect case of hallucination. Although hedging words are weak on their own, they make it possible to tell apart a cautious correct answer from a confident wrong one. Semantic similarity finds paraphrase and topic changes missed by lexical overlap. The eight claim-level aggregates repeat NLI features at the level of atomic claims, which is the level reviewers usually operate at. RAGChecker uses entailment-based claim metrics \cite{ru2024ragchecker}.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB modifies the conventional setup in terms of features and training samples. The character and word counts are log-transformed, and monotone constraints are placed on the evidence features. The constraints are negative for context overlap, context Jaccard, context entailment, context cosine, claim minimum and mean entailment, and the supported-claim ratio, and positive for context contradiction, novel-entity ratio, novel numbers, claim maximum and mean contradiction, and the contradicted-claim ratio. All other features remain unconstrained. Eight claim features from Table~\ref{tab:claim} are included: seven NLI aggregates and the claim count. They use the same NLI scorer and the support-first rule as the verification layer. A claim is supported if there is a retained sentence with an entailment probability of at least 0.5 that is at least as large as its contradiction probability. It is contradicted if there is no supporting sentence and there is a sentence with a contradiction probability of at least 0.5 that is larger than its entailment probability. Otherwise, the claim is considered unsupported.

Consider an answer with $K$ claims. Let $C(k)$ be the maximum contradiction probability of claim $k$ over its selected context sentences, $E(k)$ the maximum entailment probability, and $v_k$ its verdict. The aggregate vector is
\begin{equation}
  \psi(x) = [\max_k C(k), \overline{C}, r_{\text{con}}, r_{\text{sup}}, r_{\text{unsup}}, \min_k E(k), \overline{E}, K],
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  r_{\text{con}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{contradicted}], \quad
  r_{\text{sup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{supported}], \quad
  r_{\text{unsup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{unsupported}].
\end{equation}
Here $x=(q,c,a)$ is the input triplet, and $\overline{C}$ and $\overline{E}$ are the means of $C(k)$ and $E(k)$ over the claims. For $K>0$, the three ratios sum to one. The model input has 35 dimensions: the 26 base features (two of them log-scaled), the eight claim features, and one source indicator.

The training sample is extended to include the RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text rows. The ablation starts with m0, the 26-feature conventional reference, and moves on to m1, which uses log-transformed length counts under monotone constraints, to m2, which adds the eight claim features (34 features total), and to m3, which uses the RAGTruth non-QA rows with the source indicator (35 features total). A binary source indicator equal to one is used for those rows and equal to zero for HaluEval, which allows the classifier to separate the two sources. The classifier does not see RAGTruth QA rows and thus performs zero-shot QA ranking. In contrast, the display calibrator sees labeled QA rows and produces a non-zero-shot display score. The 35-feature setup is called EC-XGB, with seed 42 as the deployed checkpoint and other seeds as stability checks. The ablation described in Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that the claim block makes no aggregate F1 contribution, and the claim count $K$ is kept because answers with more claims are harder to verify. Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecxgb} describes the procedure: steps 1 and 2 generate the feature matrix, and steps 3 to 9 perform the model fitting for the three seeds. Each iteration generates the 35-feature input, trains on HaluEval plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, fits the display calibrator on the 5{,}034 QA calibration rows, and sets the source and target thresholds.



\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require HaluEval QA training and validation rows, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text training rows, RAGTruth QA calibration rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}=\{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure seed-42 model and display calibrator, source and target thresholds, per-seed metrics
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim features for every row
    \State log-scale the character and word counts, and attach the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input, with the source indicator at zero for HaluEval and one for RAGTruth
    \State train XGBoost on the HaluEval rows plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of classifier training
    \State fit the Platt display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows
    \State select the source threshold on the HaluEval validation split
    \State select the target threshold on the QA calibration rows at a 5\% false-positive budget
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the seed-42 model and calibrator, both thresholds, and the per-seed metrics
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

The source calibration uses the HaluEval validation split. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid mapping $p(x) = \sigma(A f(x) + B)$ to the uncalibrated probability $f(x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are learned by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression learns a non-decreasing step function $g$ such that $p(x) = g(f(x))$ \cite{isotonic}. Both methods are tested against the uncalibrated probability on the HaluEval test split. The comparison provides the expected calibration error measured over 10 equal-width bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the empirical positive rate and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$, and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. Post-hoc calibration degrades when the target distribution moves \cite{gasparin2024distance}.

In the second protocol, the calibrator is trained on labeled target rows and tested on a disjoint target test split. The deployed EC-XGB display score is calibrated using a Platt calibrator fitted on the 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows. These rows have the source indicator set to one, and the API uses the same value for all requests. This target calibration requires labeled target rows and is not zero-shot. The source-only protocol (P0 in Table~\ref{tab:protocols}) fits the calibrator and selects the threshold on the HaluEval validation split. The deployed calibrator is Platt. Platt versus isotonic was compared on the test split in an earlier deployment; see Section~\ref{sec:results}.

TreeExplainer computes the exact SHAP values \cite{shap} of the raw XGBoost model. The global importance of a feature is defined as the mean absolute SHAP value over a stratified test sample. The explanation endpoint returns five features with the largest absolute SHAP values for a given prediction. Post-hoc attribution scores can be unstable even when the underlying model is stable \cite{kelodjou2024shap}, so the pilot evaluates them directly \cite{adebayo2018}. A 20-case pilot evaluates the effect of six operators: entity, number, and date replacements, support removal, context insertion, and clause shuffling. It measures the mean absolute score change, the fraction of large changes, the top-1 flip rate, and the Spearman rank correlation between the original and perturbed rankings.

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

The method is used in a web application with a Next.js frontend and a FastAPI backend. The browser calls the Next.js backend-for-frontend (BFF) which streams chat tokens and forwards tool calls to the model. The analyze route calls FastAPI via the \texttt{/api/ml/*} rewrite. At startup, FastAPI loads the EC-XGB model, the Platt calibrator, the SHAP explainer, and the NLI, spaCy, and MiniLM feature models. The deployed checkpoint corresponds to the 35-feature m3 model with seed 42. Section~\ref{sec:results} tests this model against B2, the conventional 26-feature checkpoint trained on HaluEval QA, and against B6 m0, the fixed-hyperparameter 26-feature reference from the same tuning protocol. Claims are separated from the answer and verified with sentence-level NLI against the context, a local passage index, or a web search API. A claim is supported if at least one retained sentence provides an entailment probability of at least 0.5 and not lower than its contradiction probability. A contradiction decides only if no sentence supports the claim and a sentence has a contradiction probability of at least 0.5 that is higher than its entailment probability. A relevance gate filters out sentences that do not contain claim vocabulary, and verdicts are returned for each claim. The card shows these verdicts as follows: a contradicted claim is considered high-risk, an unsupported claim is medium-risk, and a fully supported answer is low-risk. The percentage is the display score. The chat card adjusts this score based on the claim mixture, and the analyze card reports the unadjusted Platt score. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the scoring procedure for a single answer and Figure~\ref{fig:system} shows where each component runs.

In the example of the hallucinated number, the context says 5 days per decade whereas the answer says 10. The raw score is 0.999 and the target-calibrated score is 35.4\%, which the card rounds to 35\% and puts in the medium band (0.35 to 0.60). The claim view marks the number claim unsupported, hence the chat card reports medium risk. Although the two labels agree in this case, they come from different rules. The claim verdict audit is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The requirements file fixes all dependencies; split indices are saved with the artifacts; and the audit scripts check that rebuilt and reported values match the frozen artifacts. Audits run after each build and fail if there is any difference. The contribution is in the training and evaluation protocol, not in the development of a new learner. EC-XGB uses three standard changes: log-scaled length counts with monotone constraints, claim-level NLI aggregations for explanation, and multi-source training with a source indicator. Section~\ref{sec:results} measures what each change contributes. The claim aggregations are kept for explaining each claim and do not contribute to any additional F1 gain. The seen-task gain comes from the m3 stack that adds the RAGTruth rows and the source indicator, and the two are not disentangled. No call to the LLM judge is required for the risk score. The claim judge provides an additional verification step, but the reported EC-XGB results do not depend on it.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments were done on a Windows laptop equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6~GB GPU, CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128, and 32~GB of RAM. The code is executed using Python 3.12 with scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. Each experimental run can be reproduced owing to stored split indices and centrally set seeds. A random search was done across 30 hyperparameter configurations using grouped five-fold cross-validation. For the seed-42 conventional model, the hyperparameter configuration has a maximum tree depth of 5, a learning rate of 0.05, 300 trees, a subsampling ratio of 0.7, and a column sampling ratio of 0.7. Each seed was independently tuned for the main experiment, and the ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} holds one configuration fixed in all variants so that feature effects are isolated from tuning. Seeds 42, 123, and 456 are repeated where a seed applies, and the single-seed tables are labeled.

\subsection{Dataset}

HaluEval QA \cite{haluEval} consists of 10{,}000 question-answer pairs, with a knowledge passage, a correct answer, and a hallucinated answer provided. Data is divided with a 70/15/15 split by item so that both answers associated with the same question are kept together. HaluEval is entirely generated by ChatGPT \cite{haluEval,bang-etal-2025-hallulens}, making the difference between factual inaccuracies and hallucinations somewhat blurry. RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth} provides natural answers with human span annotations, and FaithBench \cite{faithbench} provides hallucinations for contemporary LLM summaries. RAGTruth maintains its official document-level partition: summarization and data-to-text provide training rows and a held-out test set each, and QA provides 5{,}034 calibration rows and a held-out test set. RAGTruth QA is a subset of the full corpus, so its rows are not additive. Three held-out test sets make up the pooled RAGTruth test set. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the corpora, their sizes, and positive rates. MedHallu pqa\_labeled \cite{pandit2025medhallu} adds medical QA pairs with human-annotated gold answers and paired LLM hallucinations. HaluBench \cite{ravi2024lynx} provides a stratified 30\% sample of its DROP, FinanceBench, and covidQA rows, balanced between PASS and FAIL; its HaluEval, RAGTruth, and PubMedQA rows are excluded. Seeded samples of HaluEval tasks are used as task-shift sets. External corpora are never used for fitting or threshold selection.

Leading and trailing whitespace is trimmed, and lexical matching is case-insensitive. NER and NLI are performed on the original text, with entity strings converted to lowercase for matching. Missing context is a special case where lexical overlap is zero and NLI probabilities are uniformly distributed. In m1 and later variants, both length counts are log1p-transformed; in m0 and the conventional model, raw counts are used. Claims are split into no more than six clause-level segments, with conjunctions split into clauses. If the context has more than four sentences, each claim is matched to its four best sentences based on token overlap, and both sides of a pair are truncated to 400 characters. Both index sets are frozen. A RAGTruth answer is considered positive if it includes at least one annotated hallucination span. Severity annotations in FaithBench are condensed by a worst-severity principle, and a Questionable annotation is counted as positive. HaluEval uses its own label scheme. NLI and MiniLM inference ran on the reference GPU in fp16, and lexical and entity features were calculated on the CPU. The feature-based logistic baselines standardize their inputs on the training split; TF-IDF models use sparse features, and XGBoost does not scale. The source indicator takes a value of 1 for FaithBench and MedHallu, and 0 for HaluBench and the task-shift sets. FaithBench, RAGTruth summarization, and data-to-text have no question; therefore, question overlap is 0, and question similarity is defined as the cosine similarity between the answer and the empty string encoding. Licenses and the data availability statement are in the Declarations.

\subsection{Baselines and Experimental Procedure}

Twelve models are used in the evaluation. The heuristic assigns risk as one minus lexical overlap, and the length-only logistic control uses two log-scaled length counts. The classical models include logistic regression and a 300-tree random forest. The conventional XGBoost model \cite{xgboost} uses all 26 base features. Three more controls investigate the feature design: NLI-only, answer-only TF-IDF, and full-input TF-IDF. The set is completed by the majority classifier, fine-tuned RoBERTa-base \cite{liu2019roberta}, and the light reimplementation of the ensemble of Sundaragiri et al.\ \cite{ijert}; the two judges are presented in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Internal-state detectors are beyond the scope of the black-box approach \cite{inside2024}. The RoBERTa baseline processes up to 256 tokens and trains for three epochs. The majority baseline predicts the negative class, and, therefore, probability metrics are not applicable. The external ensemble uses five features (ROUGE-L, SBERT cosine similarity, two NLI probabilities, answer length) in the logistic regression model trained on 14{,}000 training samples; the selected threshold of 0.55 gives F1 = 0.9514, so the fixed 0.5 threshold does not affect the comparison. EC-XGB's display calibrator is fit on the RAGTruth QA split, and the in-domain Platt calibrator is fit on the validation set. Metrics are rounded to four decimals and rates to one. Significance uses McNemar's test on the seed-42 predictions, 2,000 two-level bootstrap resamples, and Wilcoxon tests across seeds. No question appears in both training and evaluation. The calibration quality is estimated on raw, Platt, and isotonic scores; see Table~\ref{tab:protocols} for definitions of P0, P1, and P2; the test rows are the same for all protocols.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Two types of metrics are introduced here. The first set includes the threshold-dependent metrics for evaluating the decision made at the fixed cut-off point. The second set contains the threshold-free metrics for evaluating how well the model ranks responses. Both are needed since the model can have high ranking performance and poor decision performance if the cut-off point is fixed. Precision is the proportion of flagged responses that were hallucinated. Recall is the proportion of hallucinated responses detected by the system, and F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. False-positive rate (FPR) is the proportion of correct responses that get flagged and serves as an operating constraint under distributional shift. True positive (TP) and true negative (TN) indicate the correctly classified instances, and false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) are a correct answer flagged as hallucinated and a hallucinated answer that is not flagged, respectively. The flag rate is the proportion of responses exceeding the decision threshold; if it approaches 100\%, then every response reaches a human reviewer.
\begin{gather}
  \text{Precision }(P) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
  \text{Recall }(R) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
  F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}, \\
  \text{False-positive rate }(\mathrm{FPR}) = \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{TN}}, \qquad
  \text{Flag rate} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}_i \ge \tau].
\end{gather}

The second set includes ranking, calibration, and paired error metrics. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) is equal to the probability that a random hallucinated answer has a higher score than a random correct answer, with 0.5 meaning chance level. Area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) is more informative when the positive class is rare, and RAGTruth QA has a positive rate of 17.8\%. Thus, PR-AUC is also reported. The Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) uses all four cells of the confusion matrix and has values ranging from $-1$ to $1$, with zero being the value of random performance. The Wilson interval gives a 95\% confidence range for a proportion and is used for the P2 recall and FPR when the number of observations is small.
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{AUROC} = \int_{0}^{1} \mathrm{TPR}(u)\, d\,\mathrm{FPR}(u) = P(s^{+} > s^{-}), \\
  \mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} P(r)\, dr, \qquad
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}\cdot\mathrm{TN} - \mathrm{FP}\cdot\mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}, \\
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\end{gather}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:models} shows the HaluEval evaluation results for eleven baselines and EC-XGB averaged over three seeds where relevant. EC-XGB outperforms the conventional model in F1 by 0.0009, an improvement smaller than the width of the conventional seed-42 bootstrap confidence interval [0.9812, 0.9895], such that the benchmark cannot provide a definite order. The ranking columns show a comparable trend: EC-XGB takes first place among the feature-based models in AUROC and PR-AUC, with the conventional model and random forest close behind, and RoBERTa slightly above. Calibration paints a different picture, as the conventional model takes the lowest expected calibration error (ECE) and EC-XGB and RoBERTa take the lowest Brier scores. The weak controls are NLI-only and full-input TF-IDF, whereas answer-only TF-IDF is much stronger; adding question and context lowers performance. The reduced external ensemble is behind EC-XGB in F1 by 0.0337 [0.0262, 0.0419] and 0.0135 AUROC [0.0098, 0.0177], but it is trained on fewer rows and with a narrower feature set, so this gap is descriptive. The margin over logistic regression is 0.0187 F1 ($p = 1.86\times10^{-10}$), and over the heuristic is 0.0495 ($p = 2.28\times10^{-30}$). A source-only encoder wins in-domain on F1 (0.9912 vs 0.9855) but is near chance on each RAGTruth task (AUROC 0.458 on summarization and 0.491 on data-to-text, compared to 0.736 and 0.770 for EC-XGB on those tasks). Pooling gives 0.337 for the encoder and 0.582 for EC-XGB, as the encoder's pooled score is a blend of between-task score shifts and within-task ranking. An adapted encoder on the same multi-source rows reaches 0.694 on the pooled RAGTruth test set, beating EC-XGB's 0.582; hence, the seen-task gain is not specific to the feature model. Neither model generalizes to unseen QA (0.490 vs 0.549), and FaithBench is worse for both (0.434 vs 0.594). Adaptation helps any model on seen tasks; the feature model preserves its relative ordering on unseen tasks.

\subsection{Calibration, Shift, and External Validity}

The raw, Platt-scaled, and isotonic versions of the conventional model have ECE values of 0.0045, 0.0091, and 0.0070 on HaluEval, showing that the calibrated versions do not decrease ECE in this sample. Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} clarifies this result. On HaluEval, the points at the two ends lie on the diagonal; the middle range of scores contains few distinct values, leading to only a small number of stable bins. FaithBench is aggregated because its scores concentrate near 1.0. The Platt and isotonic curves overlap the raw curve at both ends. The m3 raw score is already well calibrated in-domain, with ECE 0.0078, providing little room for improvement in calibration on HaluEval. Platt scaling applies a two-parameter logistic transformation mapping the uncalibrated probability $s$ (the raw score in the figures) to a probability,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p} = \sigma(a s + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
  \label{eq:platt}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the calibration slope and $b$ is the intercept. A slope below one means the raw score is overconfident, and a slope near one means the scale is already usable. The Brier skill score (BSS) compares the calibrated score with a constant predictor at the evaluation prevalence,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{prior}}}.
  \label{eq:bss}
\end{equation}
A positive BSS means the score beats the prior, and a non-positive BSS means it does not.

Table~\ref{tab:conformal} reports the coverage. Split conformal prediction adds a coverage guarantee to the score. The nonconformity score for label $y$ is $s(x,y)=1-\hat{p}_y(x)$, where $\hat{p}_1=\hat{p}$ and $\hat{p}_0=1-\hat{p}$. The calibration quantile and the prediction set are
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q} = s_{(k)}, \quad k = \left\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}\},
  \label{eq:conformal}
\end{equation}
where $s_{(1)} \le \cdots \le s_{(n)}$ are the ordered calibration scores; this finite-sample order statistic yields coverage of at least $1-\alpha$ for the prediction set. A class-conditional version replaces the single quantile with one quantile per label,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q}_y = s_{y,(k_y)}, \quad k_y = \left\lceil (n_y+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}_y\},
  \label{eq:conformal_class}
\end{equation}
where $s_{y,(1)} \le \cdots \le s_{y,(n_y)}$ are the ordered scores in class $y$ and $n_y$ is the number of calibration rows with label $y$.
The main run fits the Platt map and the quantile on the same 5{,}034 rows, so its coverage is a construction check; it gives 0.940 and 0.967 coverage on QA and 0.900 and 0.950 on the calibration rows. A disjoint sensitivity fits Platt on 2{,}514 rows and the quantile on 2{,}520 rows. On that split, the 900 QA test rows reach 0.944 coverage at 90\%, so the marginal guarantee holds. The marginal guarantee hides the class split: positive coverage is 0.685, while negative coverage is 1.000. A class-conditional quantile on the full calibration rows raises positive coverage to 0.887, still slightly below nominal. FaithBench coverage falls to 0.320 and positive coverage to 0.003, because a Platt map fitted on QA sends almost every FaithBench score to the negative side. Conformal prediction therefore certifies the marginal rate only when the calibrator and the quantile are fitted on disjoint rows, and it does not transfer across the task shift. Multicalibration by answer length and claim count shows where the average ECE hides error. On the 900 QA test rows, the target-Platt score has overall ECE 0.125. The worst length bin is 60 words or fewer (n=154), with ECE 0.275; the worst claim-count bin is a single claim (n=36), with ECE 0.346. The Brier score does not follow the ECE ranking, so the average is not a safe summary for short or one-claim answers. FaithBench is not improved by the QA calibrator: its target-Platt ECE is 0.629 against 0.453 for the raw score.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Statistical tests use the paired fixed-hyperparameter predictions and 2{,}000 two-level bootstrap draws, where groups are resampled within each seed and then seeds are resampled. McNemar's test compares the discordant counts $b$ and $c$ on the paired seed-42 rows, where $b$ is the number of rows where the first model is right and the second is wrong, and $c$ is the reverse; the statistic uses a continuity correction. Holm's method controls the family-wise error rate across the four baseline comparisons. The 95\% interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the paired bootstrap deltas. The two-level version resamples groups within each seed before resampling seeds. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares the two models across the three seeds. McNemar's results are presented in Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar}. Neither EC-XGB nor the conventional method exhibits a significant difference under the deployed checkpoints ($p = 0.45$), whereas the fixed-hp ablation shows $p = 0.789$ on the same test rows. The fixed-hp m0-vs-m3 comparison is the primary test, and it is not part of the Holm family. The family consists of four baseline comparisons against the conventional method. The test uses the single configuration defined in Table~\ref{tab:ec}. The random forest shows no significant difference from the conventional method ($p = 0.502$). Logistic regression and the heuristic significantly underperform ($p = 1.86\times10^{-10}$ and $p = 2.28\times10^{-30}$), with both surviving the Holm correction. The Wilcoxon test with three seeds does not produce a significant difference (minimum $p$-value = 0.25). Table~\ref{tab:stats} contains the results of the two-level bootstrap evaluation. On the HaluEval dataset, the F1 delta for m3 minus m0 is $+$0.0006, with CI [$-$0.0010, 0.0021], which encompasses zero, whereas the AUROC delta is $+$0.0007, with CI [0.0002, 0.0012], which excludes zero by a small margin. The F1 deltas for m2 minus m1 and m3 minus m1 are $-$0.0004 (CI [$-$0.0020, 0.0012]) and $+$0.0001 (CI [$-$0.0015, 0.0018]). Thus, the claim features provide no overall improvement in terms of F1. The AUROC deltas are $+$0.0009 and $+$0.0010, with both intervals above zero.

In the held-out RAGTruth QA experiment, the m3$-$m0 F1 difference is $+$0.0076 [0.0045, 0.0109], while the AUROC difference is $+$0.0017 [$-$0.0270, 0.0291], which includes zero. The F1 gain is achieved mainly by flagging almost every row, not by improving the ranking. In the pooled seen tasks experiment, the F1 difference is $+$0.0451 [0.0348, 0.0554], and the AUROC difference is $+$0.0962 [0.0644, 0.1295]. All the paired fixed-minus-tuned differences include zero, meaning that the rebuild does not affect these results. In HaluEval, the groups are item pairs (1{,}500); in RAGTruth QA, there are 150 source groups; and in the pooled split, there are 450 groups. The QA AUROC difference includes zero, suggesting that the held-out transfer result is unresolved, and the pooled row measures adaptation to seen tasks.

\subsection{Explanation, Efficiency, and Judges}

The explanation uses the fixed-hyperparameter m3 seed-42 checkpoint. Figure~\ref{fig:shapimp} lists the top-15 features ranked by mean absolute SHAP value over 1{,}000 stratified HaluEval test rows. Context overlap is the strongest driver, followed by word count, question-answer cosine, character count, and question-answer Jaccard similarity. The claim-contradiction mean appears at rank eight. The top ten features cover 77.9\% of the attributions, and exact TreeSHAP matches the model margin up to $9.2\times10^{-6}$. The attributions are calculated for the raw margin, not the display score. The m3 rankings are highly correlated with the frozen B2 rankings (Spearman's $\rho = 0.878$, Kendall's $\tau = 0.730$); therefore, the claim features refine the explanation, rather than replace the original drivers. Cross-seed Spearman correlation is 0.972 and 0.950; nine of the top ten features are shared, and the top five features are identical. The explanation is resilient to the choice of the seed but not to content editing. Table~\ref{tab:perturb} presents a 20-case perturbation pilot: content edits move the score, structural edits barely shift it, and the top-1 feature is flipped in 54.5\% of entity perturbations and none of the other operators. The rate of entity flipping is higher than in B2, so the explanation is less stable when the answer entity changes; the per-operator sample size is small. The structured AI-assisted audit of 26 cases enriched for errors and re-scored under m3 evaluated each explanation based on consistency with answer-context evidence, rather than the dataset label. Thirteen explanations were considered implausible, three uncertain, and ten plausible, so the explanations are used as diagnostic aids, not faithful attributions. False positives carry the length confound, and false negatives frequently rely on entities repeated in the context. As the sample is enriched for errors, the percentages characterize the audit, not the test set. The labels are author-checked pre-annotations: LLM tools assisted the annotation, and the authors reviewed and verified the final labels. Agreement with the pre-annotations was 0.98 for explanation, 0.94 for taxonomy, and 0.88 for claims. Cohen's $\kappa$ was 0.965, 0.923, and 0.788; the authors changed 2\%, 6\%, and 12\% of rows, and the blind Fleiss $\kappa$ was 0.940, 0.868, and 0.761.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Discussion}

On the seed-42 fixed-hyperparameter run, m3 makes 43 errors on the HaluEval test set (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}), an error rate of 1.4\%. The WP5 audit covers seven of the eight false positives and 12 of the 35 false negatives, chosen to cover the codebook. A separate 50-case sheet, reviewed by one author, adds 31 artifact cases and 19 content cases across 12 categories. These describe the reviewed cases, not the 3{,}000-row test population. Table~\ref{tab:length} shows that length is a near-perfect label indicator on HaluEval, with false positives concentrated in the five-to-eight bin and false negatives in the short bins, although the 17+ bin has only two negative rows. The controls change the length distribution in two ways. A reweighting control equalizes six length bins with 247 examples each, and a length-matching control pairs one positive and one negative at each length. Reweighting gives EC-XGB a gain of 0.039 F1 and 0.023 AUROC over the length-only regression, which is already competitive on its own (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Length matching gives 79 pairs (158 examples) over 1{,}000 bootstraps. EC-XGB holds AUROC 0.956 while the length-only baseline falls to 0.521. The AUROC point gap is 0.435 (bootstrap mean 0.436 [0.351, 0.522]), and the F1 point gap is 0.431 (bootstrap mean 0.440 [0.334, 0.552]).

Figure~\ref{fig:length} shows the same pattern. Complete sentences are long, so the conventional model reads them as hallucinations, and the in-domain model is mostly a length and overlap detector. Published HaluEval QA results use different splits, supervision, and evidence access. A supervised 13B agent reports F1 0.724 on 100 rows, so these published numbers are context rather than a head-to-head bar. The benchmark also asks for hallucinated answers about five words longer, and the observed gap is 8.5 words, so any comparison that ignores this construction artifact overstates semantic detection. The claims in this paper are bounded by the tests that were not run. Retrieval quality and end-to-end verdict accuracy are not evaluated, the judge comparison controls length but not domain, and the audit is error-enriched. The deployed display calibrator was selected in an earlier fit that compared Platt and isotonic on the test split, and Table~\ref{tab:target} refits Platt without that selection.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This study tested EC-XGB, a 35-feature evidence-consistent model, as a hallucination risk score and asked how its calibration and ranking change under domain and task shift. Its ranking survives a change of corpus, so the score can order answers for review, and the per-claim verdicts give a reviewer a reason for each flag. Its probability does not survive that change: the display score needs labeled target rows, and the target prevalence then sets its level. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific screening score, not a general-purpose detector. The next test is adaptation to an unseen QA domain, with thresholds selected under prevalence shift. A seen-task source ablation would then separate the RAGTruth rows from the source indicator.
