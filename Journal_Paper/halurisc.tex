\begin{abstract}
  Large language models answer fluently, and some answers are not supported by the evidence. Detectors rely on a large judge, an encoder, or model internals; these options are costly and rarely report calibration under shift. Feature-based detectors are cheaper, but their calibration, length sensitivity, and shift behaviour are seldom measured together, and HaluEval length is almost a label proxy. This study tests whether a simple feature model can estimate risk and how its calibration and ranking change under shift. The model, evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB), combines 26 base features, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator. It is trained on HaluEval QA and RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text rows with grouped splits and three seeds, and evaluated with a fixed-hyperparameter ablation, source and target Platt calibration, split and class-conditional conformal prediction, SHAP stability, a length-only baseline, an external ensemble, and judge stress tests. The fixed-hp EC-XGB reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986 on HaluEval QA. Lexical overlap and length carry the in-domain score; removing them costs 0.0171 and 0.0066 F1. On MedHallu, the model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 pairs (AUROC 0.821), but flags every row and is badly calibrated (ECE 0.493). On a task shift to dialogue and summarization it collapses, with summarization below a length-only model. On RAGTruth QA it reaches AUROC 0.558; target calibration lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125 but flags no target rows at 0.5, conformal coverage hides 0.69 positive coverage, and FaithBench coverage falls to 0.32. The GPT judge reaches F1 0.565 on natural QA, but on a length-matched set EC-XGB reaches 0.907 and the judge 0.865. EC-XGB is a domain-specific risk score, not a general-purpose detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A number of researchers have examined how to apply machine learning techniques to assess the likelihood of a response being a hallucination. One such technique is SelfCheckGPT, which uses several generated answers and assesses their similarity to determine if there has been a hallucination \cite{selfcheck}. Another technique is Luna, where a smaller evaluation model is used instead of a larger judge model \cite{luna}. A third technique is semantic entropy, which identifies meaning-based groupings of similar responses \cite{farquhar2024semantic}. A hybrid framework combines frozen encoder features with lightweight classifiers and reports strong benchmark scores \cite{ieeeTai}. Judges carry high computational costs and encoder models are hard to interpret. Two recent reviews have grouped approaches to detecting hallucinations into three categories including feature-based detectors, encoder-based detectors, and combinations of the above \cite{ji2023survey,huang2025survey}. Both reviews identify evaluation and reliability as two major areas that require additional research, and a third review determines that reliability is an area of concern as well \cite{alansari2026survey}.

Six recent studies address the same topic of assessing hallucinations. HaluAgent demonstrated that small language models can select appropriate tools and detect multiple forms of hallucinations, but it required multiple tool calls for each answer \cite{haluagent}. Valentin et al. proposed a calibrated multi-score detector with a detailed cost analysis, but the detector relies heavily on large language model (LLM) scoring for its primary source of information \cite{costeff}. Sundaragiri et al. proposed a system that uses retrieval, natural language inference (NLI) verification, and an XGBoost classifier, and they report only classification metrics \cite{ijert}. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME attributions to each word, but did not test whether those values remained consistent across words \cite{multimedia}. Deng et al. found that detectors degrade once they leave the domain in which they were trained, and their method focuses on multi-turn dialogue signals \cite{spikescore}. FaithBench shows that modern LLMs still produce hard summarization hallucinations, and it does not assess calibration risks \cite{faithbench}.

The existing literature leaves four issues unaddressed. Calibration is infrequently reported for lightweight detectors, explanation quality is rarely measured, cross-domain behavior is rarely tested, and error patterns are rarely analyzed case by case. The conventional feature-based baseline is also saturated on HaluEval, depends on answer length, and loses calibration under shift. This paper addresses all four issues by developing a black-box model that takes a query $q$, a reference context $c$, and a candidate answer $a$, and outputs $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$. The model, evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB), consists of 26 evidence-consistency features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, and a source indicator, classified by a tuned XGBoost model. Platt and isotonic calibration yield the probability, SHAP explanations reveal feature contributions, artifact-control baselines and group-aware splits reduce shortcut solutions, and validation-only calibration quantifies the reliability problem. The claim-level aggregates support per-claim explanations, and the measured transfer gain comes from multi-source training rather than from those aggregates. Transfer limits are established on RAGTruth and FaithBench. The evaluation adds an independent medical corpus (MedHallu), a non-overlapping HaluBench subset, a task-shift test on HaluEval dialogue and summarization, split and class-conditional conformal prediction, a fixed-hyperparameter feature-group ablation, cross-seed SHAP stability, and length-matched and prompt-sensitivity judge stress tests. The added evidence separates ranking from calibration: the model transfers its ranking to medical pairs but not its probability, and it collapses under a task shift.

HaluEval QA provides 10{,}000 paired correct and hallucinated answers for training and in-domain testing \cite{haluEval}. RAGTruth provides natural responses for adaptation and recalibration \cite{ragtruth}. The scope is question answering with a reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. The fixed-hp m3 model reaches an F1 of 0.9854 and an AUROC of 0.9986 on HaluEval QA. On natural RAGTruth answers, the flag rate falls from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, and the AUROC rises from 0.475 to 0.582. On an independent medical corpus, the same fixed-hp model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 pairs but flags every row; on a task shift to dialogue and summarization it collapses, with summarization ranking below a length-only model. Target calibration on 5{,}034 labeled rows lowers the RAGTruth QA ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, but it does not change the ranking. On HaluEval, recall stays above 99\% at a five percent false-positive rate, and one analysis takes 127.5~ms at about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions.

\section{Conventional Method}\label{sec:conventional}

\subsection{Preliminaries}

Fluent hallucinations are claims where the amount of support from the evidence is not enough. The detection can be assessed in terms of precision, recall, F1, or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in an evaluation setting on labeled data. Typical datasets for testing detectors are HaluEval \cite{haluEval} and TruthfulQA \cite{truthqa}, and factual accuracy of long-form text generation can be verified by FActScore \cite{factscore}. In a deployment context, a detector faces tougher evaluation, because it has to contribute to a decision process and to be explainable. Calibration addresses the first problem -- the predicted risk has to represent the true risk, and calibration is measured with the use of expected calibration error (ECE) via reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. The second one -- having the detector's warning, the person needs an explanation of it. For tabular models such information comes from SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) attributions \cite{shap}. Both kinds of evaluation are conducted in the current work on the black box approach to the model.

\subsection{Baseline Method}

The conventional method is a tuned XGBoost classifier \cite{xgboost} over the 26 hand-crafted features. XGBoost handles nonlinear interactions, trains quickly on tabular data, and supports exact SHAP values. Boosted trees remain competitive with tuned neural networks on tabular data. The model adds trees one at a time, and each tree fits the error left by the previous ensemble. The final score passes through a logistic link, so the output is a probability.

Let $x_i$ be the feature vector of row $i$ and $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its label. The model sums $K$ trees, $z_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)$. The training objective combines a differentiable loss with a regularizer over the trees,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big( \gamma T_k + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert w_k \rVert^2 \big),
\end{equation}
where $\phi = \{f_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ collects the trees, $f_k$ is the $k$-th tree with $T_k$ leaves of weight $w_k$, $\gamma$ is a leaf-count penalty, and $\lambda$ is an L2 weight penalty. The loss $l$ is the standard logistic loss and the probability comes from the sigmoid of the summed leaf score, $\hat{y}_i = \sigma(z_i) = 1/(1 + e^{-z_i})$.

At boosting step $t$, a new tree with $T$ leaves is added, and the objective is approximated by a second-order expansion around the current prediction,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $g_i$ and $h_i$ are the first and second derivatives of the loss at the current prediction. For a fixed tree structure, the optimal weight of leaf $j$ is
\begin{equation}
  w_j^{*} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda},
\end{equation}
where $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$ and $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$ are the sums over the rows $I_j$ that reach leaf $j$. The gain of a split is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda} \right] - \gamma,
\end{equation}
with $G_L$ and $H_L$ for the left child and $G_R$ and $H_R$ for the right child. A split is kept only when the gain is positive. This is the criterion used to grow each tree.

Tree depth, learning rate, tree count, and the two subsample rates are tuned on the training split; the selected values are in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:baseline} lists the eight-step procedure. The search draws 30 configurations and scores each with grouped five-fold cross-validation. The grouping keeps both answers to the same question in one fold. Each seed trains on 14,000 rows, fits Platt scaling on 3,000 validation rows, selects the threshold on validation, and tests once on 3,000 rows. The test rows are never used for tuning or calibration. Step 8 returns the mean over the three seeds, which is why every seeded aggregate table reports a mean.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Baseline feature-based XGBoost (training and evaluation)}
  \label{alg:baseline}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled rows $(q_i, c_i, a_i, y_i)$ and seeds $\mathcal{S} = \{42,123,456\}$
    \Ensure trained model, calibrator, and decision threshold
    \State extract the 26 evidence-consistency features for every row
    \State tune XGBoost with a randomized search and grouped 5-fold cross-validation
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State train the classifier on the training split
    \State fit Platt calibration and select the decision threshold on the validation split
    \State evaluate on the fixed test split
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the mean metrics over the seeds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This baseline has three limits. The benchmark is saturated, so extra features do not improve F1. The score is sensitive to answer length, and the probability scale breaks under shift \cite{ovadia2019}. The baseline also lacks claim-level evidence, trains on one corpus, and reports a source-calibrated probability that is not calibrated under target shift. These limits motivate the three changes in Section 3.

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Working Principle}

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{revision/architecture-flow-large.png}
  \caption{End-to-end pipeline of EC-XGB.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} reads from left to right. It takes a question, reference context, and a candidate answer from a black-box LLM, and produces a risk probability and per-feature explanations. The first stage collects the three inputs: the question, the reference context, and the candidate answer. A missing context is allowed and is treated as a separate condition. The second stage extracts 35 features. The seven base groups cover length and style, lexical overlap, entity novelty, NLI, numeric overlap, hedging, and semantic similarity. Eight claim-level NLI aggregates are added to the 26 base features, and a binary source indicator marks whether the answer came from a natural response corpus. The third stage passes the 35 features to the EC-XGB classifier. The classifier returns a raw score, which is a probability over the hallucinated class. The fourth stage applies the Platt calibrator fitted on the RAGTruth QA calibration split. The calibrated score is the number shown to the user. The fifth stage produces the outputs: the risk score, the per-claim verdicts, and the SHAP feature contributions. The lower band shows the deployment path. A Next.js front end calls a FastAPI service, and the service loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. The rightmost block lists the evaluation corpora. HaluEval measures in-domain performance, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text measure adaptation to task types seen in training, and RAGTruth QA and FaithBench are held out. The training band records the monotone constraints, the grouped five-fold cross-validation, and the three seeds used for every reported mean.

The task consists of estimating the probability $P(\text{hallucinated}\mid q,c,a)$ of a hallucination, conditioned on a question $q$, reference context $c$ (if available) and a candidate answer $a$ coming from a black-box LLM. It is not aimed at verifying truthfulness of the candidate answer, but at finding those cases in which the candidate answer does not match the provided evidence, i.e. it does not follow from or contradicts it. The output is a probability, not a binary label. A decision threshold turns the probability into a flag.

\subsection{Features}

The model uses 26 base features in seven groups (Table~\ref{tab:features}) and eight aggregate features computed at the claim level (Table~\ref{tab:claim}). For NLI, six signal probabilities come from a DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder \cite{deberta} trained on SNLI \cite{snli} and MultiNLI \cite{mnli}. They indicate whether one statement follows logically from another. Semantic similarity uses MiniLM sentence embeddings \cite{sbert,minilm}, and entities come from spaCy named entity recognition \cite{spacy}.
Length and style features count characters, words, sentences, and average word length. They are included because the benchmark labels longer answers as hallucinated more often, so length is a strong but fragile signal. Lexical overlap measures how much of the answer appears in the context and in the question. It captures direct copying and is the strongest feature in the SHAP ranking, but it cannot detect a fluent paraphrase that contradicts the evidence. Entity features count named entities and the share that do not appear in the context. A novel entity is a useful warning sign, although a correct answer can also introduce a new name. The NLI features give the probability that the context entails, contradicts, or is neutral to the answer, in both directions. They are the only group that represents logical support rather than surface overlap. Numeric features compare the numbers in the answer with the numbers in the context. A changed number is a common hallucination and is easy to detect. Hedging features count uncertainty words such as ``may'' or ``likely''. They are weak on their own, but they help separate a cautious correct answer from a confident wrong one. Semantic features use cosine similarity between the context and the answer and between the question and the answer. They catch paraphrase and topic drift that lexical overlap misses. The eight claim-level aggregates in Table~\ref{tab:claim} repeat the NLI and overlap signals at the level of atomic claims, which is the level a reviewer actually checks. Entailment-based claim metrics are also used by RAGChecker \cite{ru2024ragchecker}.

\subsection{Evidence-Consistent XGBoost}

EC-XGB alters three key aspects of the conventional XGBoost learner. These changes allow each component of the learning process to be assessed individually. First, raw-length counts are replaced with log-scaled values, and monotonicity constraints ensure that risk cannot decrease as contradiction or novelty rises and cannot increase as entailment, overlap, or semantic similarity rises. Raw-count length is replaced with its log-scaled value, $\ell' = \log(1+\ell)$. Second, the eight claim-level aggregates in Table~\ref{tab:claim} are added to the evidence signals that the deployed verification layer reports, so the classifier sees the same signals. Consider an answer composed of $K$ claims, with $C(k)$ the contradiction and $E(k)$ the entailment of claim $k$, and let $v_k$ be the verdict of claim $k$. The aggregate vector is
\begin{gather}
  \psi(x) = [\max_k C(k), \overline{C}, r_{\text{con}}, r_{\text{sup}}, r_{\text{unsup}}, \min_k E(k), \overline{E}, K], \\
  r_{\text{con}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{contradicted}], \quad
  r_{\text{sup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{supported}], \\
  r_{\text{unsup}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[v_k = \text{unsupported}].
\end{gather}
Here $\overline{C}$ and $\overline{E}$ are the means of $C(k)$ and $E(k)$ over the claims. Let $\phi'(x)$ be the complete model input and $\tilde{\phi}(x)$ the remaining 24 base features excluding the two log-scaled length columns. There are 35 input features in total (24 base + 2 log-scaled length + 8 claim + 1 source). The claim count $K$ is kept as a feature because an answer with many claims is harder to verify.
The third modification is the addition of a source indicator $s \in \{0,1\}$ as a training feature for RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text tasks. Since all RAGTruth QA samples are filtered out during the training phase, the QA task itself is still zero-shot. The deployed display score is fitted on the labeled target samples and is therefore not zero-shot. This combination of modifications is termed EC-XGB. The deployed checkpoint uses seed 42.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecxgb} lists the training and deployment procedure. Steps 1 and 2 build the feature matrix. The length columns are log-scaled, and the monotone constraints are attached to the features they govern. Steps 3 to 9 repeat the fit for the three seeds. Each repetition builds the 35-feature input, trains on HaluEval plus the RAGTruth non-QA rows, fits the display calibrator on the 5{,}034 RAGTruth QA calibration rows, selects the source threshold on the HaluEval validation split, and selects the target threshold on the calibration rows at a 5\% false-positive budget. The QA rows are excluded from training in every repetition, which keeps the QA test zero-shot. The output is the model, the display calibrator, and the two thresholds.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Evidence-consistent XGBoost (EC-XGB) training and deployment}
  \label{alg:ecxgb}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require labeled HaluEval QA rows, RAGTruth non-QA rows, seeds $\mathcal{S}$
    \Ensure deployed model, display calibrator, source threshold, target threshold
    \State extract the 26 base features and the eight claim-level aggregates
    \State log-scale the length features and set the monotone constraints
    \For{each seed $s \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \State build the 35-feature input with the source indicator
    \State train XGBoost on HaluEval plus RAGTruth non-QA rows, and keep every RAGTruth QA row out of training
    \State fit the display calibrator on the RAGTruth QA calibration split (P1)
    \State select the source-only threshold on the HaluEval validation split (P0)
    \State select the target threshold on the same calibration split at five percent FPR (P2)
    \EndFor
    \State \Return the deployed model, calibrator, and thresholds
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The contribution is the multi-source training protocol and the evaluation, not a new learner. Each piece is standard on its own, and together they hold the in-domain score, cut the false-alarm rate on the task types seen in training, and produce feature-level attributions whose reliability is measured. The deployed system adds claim-level verification with evidence retrieval and a display calibrator fitted on the target domain. Relative to the closest feature-based detector \cite{ijert}, the claim-level features support per-claim explanations. The measured transfer gain comes from multi-source training and the calibration protocol. Relative to the LLM-scored detector \cite{costeff} and the tool-based detector \cite{haluagent}, the difference is that inference needs no LLM judge calls; the feature pipeline still runs three small pretrained models. Their scores come from other corpora and other protocols, so the comparison is about the protocol and not about the numbers. None of the three reports the calibration error or the shifted flag rate that this study measures.

\subsection{Calibration and Explainability}

Source calibrators are fit on the validation split only. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid $p(x) = \sigma(A f(x) + B)$ to the raw score $f(x)$, where $A$ and $B$ are fit by maximum likelihood \cite{platt}. Isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing step function $g$ so that $p(x) = g(f(x))$ \cite{isotonic}. Both are compared against the raw model on the test split. The comparison uses expected calibration error over $M=10$ bins,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m)$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ are the accuracy and mean confidence in bin $B_m$, together with the Brier score $\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$ and reliability diagrams \cite{guo}. Post-hoc calibration is not robust when the target distribution moves \cite{gasparin2024distance}. A second step targets a new domain. The calibrator can be re-fit on a small labeled sample of the target corpus, and the effect is measured on a disjoint held-out part of the target corpus. The deployed EC-XGB display score follows the same rule: its calibrator is fitted on the 5{,}034-row RAGTruth QA calibration split, and the source indicator is set to one for natural responses. This target calibration needs labeled target rows and is not zero-shot. The source-only protocol uses the HaluEval validation split for both the calibrator and the threshold.

The global SHAP \cite{shap} explanations from a TreeExplainer applied to the raw XGBoost model were used to create Mean Absolute Importance summaries. Local explanations highlighting the contributions of features involved in a particular prediction are given. Post-hoc attribution methods can be unstable even when the model is fixed \cite{kelodjou2024shap}. Since the feature-attribution techniques might be highly unreliable, the reliability of the explanations has been evaluated directly \cite{adebayo2018}. The test responses have been perturbed in six different ways (by replacing entity names, numbers, and dates, eliminating support context, inserting additional context, and shuffling clauses). This study quantifies the reliability by calculating four different measures (the mean absolute score change, the fraction of large score changes, the top-1 flip rate, and the Spearman rank correlation between the original and perturbed scores).

\subsection{System and Reproducibility}

These methods are used in a two-tiered web application. The system is a Next.js frontend and a FastAPI backend that loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. Three checkpoints appear in Section~\ref{sec:results}. B2 is the conventional 26-feature model trained on HaluEval only. It supplies the conventional row of the model-comparison table, the conventional rows of the target-calibration table, the length analysis, and the audit. B6 m0 is a separate 26-feature tuning run under the EC-XGB protocol, reported as m0 in the component ablation and as the conventional rows of the cross-domain table. EC-XGB m3 is the 35-feature multi-source model that supplies the remaining EC-XGB rows, the feature-group ablation, and the deployed system. Claims are split from the answer and checked with sentence-level NLI against the provided context, an indexed collection, or live web search. A support-first rule treats any supporting sentence as evidence, and a contradiction counts only when no support exists. A relevance gate removes retrieved sentences with no claim vocabulary, and the verdicts are shown per claim.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments were run on a Windows-based computer (laptop), with an NVIDIA RTX 3060 6\,GB GPU, CUDA 12.8, torch 2.11.0+cu128, and 32\,GB of RAM. The programming language used to run the code is Python. The Python version used in this experiment is 3.12. The code utilizes the following packages: scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, spaCy 3.8.14, and sentence-transformers 5.6.1. Each run can be reproduced because of saved split indices and centralized seeds. A randomized search was performed over 30 hyperparameter configurations with grouped five-fold cross-validation. For the seed-42 conventional model, the configuration has a maximum tree depth of 5, a learning rate of 0.05, 300 trees, a subsampling ratio of 0.7, and a column sampling ratio of 0.7. Each seed is tuned separately for the main comparison, and the component ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} holds one configuration fixed across variants so that the feature effects are not confounded with tuning. Seeds 42, 123, and 456 are repeated where a seed applies; the single-seed tables are labeled.

\subsection{Dataset}

The QA subset of HaluEval \cite{haluEval} serves as the training data. This dataset has 10{,}000 questions. Each question has a knowledge passage that it is based upon, a correct answer, and a hallucinated version of that answer. Therefore, there are 20{,}000 rows of labeled data. The 20{,}000 rows are split 70/15/15 at the group level into 14{,}000 training, 3{,}000 validation, and 3{,}000 test rows. Since both versions of the answer to each question come from the same knowledge passage, the model can easily learn the relationship between the context and the answers, so the split is at the group level. About 85\% of HaluEval was generated by ChatGPT through sampling and filtering \cite{haluEval,bang-etal-2025-hallulens}. The boundary between a factual error and a hallucination is therefore not always clean.
Transfer was evaluated using two datasets. First, RAGTruth \cite{ragtruth}: a collection of responses produced through natural processes that have been annotated with word-level spans by humans. Second, FaithBench \cite{faithbench}: a hallucination-based summarization benchmark based on outputs from modern large language models. RAGTruth ships official splits. Summarization has 4{,}758 training and 900 test rows, and data-to-text has 5{,}298 training and 900 test rows. The QA task contributed 5{,}934 rows in 989 groups, of which 900 rows in 150 groups form its test set and 5{,}034 rows form the calibration block. The groups do not overlap. The three 900-row test splits are held out from training, so the pooled RAGTruth test set has 2{,}700 rows. The positive rate is 50.0\% on HaluEval, 34.9\% on the pooled RAGTruth test split, 17.8\% on the RAGTruth QA test rows, 31.1\% on the RAGTruth QA calibration rows, and 68.1\% on FaithBench. The summary of the corpora is provided in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.

Three further corpora test external validity without training, calibration, or threshold selection. MedHallu pqa\_labeled \cite{pandit2025medhallu} provides 1{,}000 medical QA pairs with human-annotated gold answers and paired LLM-written hallucinations, each with gold knowledge, giving 2{,}000 rows at 50.0\% positive. HaluBench \cite{ravi2024lynx} supplies 900 rows from DROP, FinanceBench, and covidQA, 300 rows per source at 50.0\% positive; its HaluEval, RAGTruth, and PubMedQA rows are excluded to avoid double counting and overlap with MedHallu. Labels are HaluBench PASS/FAIL decisions, not the original source labels, and the DROP and FinanceBench rows were converted to question-answer form by the HaluBench authors. The HaluEval dialogue and summarization subsets provide 1{,}000 pairs each, 2{,}000 rows per task at 50.0\% positive, as a task-shift test; they share the HaluEval generator but change the task, so they are not a second independent QA corpus.

\subsection{Baselines and Experimental Procedure}

Twelve models are tested. The heuristic sets risk to one minus lexical overlap. Logistic regression and a 300-tree random forest are the classical comparators; the conventional XGBoost model \cite{xgboost} uses the 26 base features. Three controls isolate the feature design: NLI-only, answer-only TF-IDF, and full-input TF-IDF. A majority classifier, a fine-tuned RoBERTa-base \cite{liu2019roberta}, and a reduced reimplementation of the lightweight ensemble of Sundaragiri et al.\ \cite{ijert} complete the set; the two judges are in Section~\ref{sec:results}.



Internal-state detectors are outside the black-box setting \cite{inside2024}. The RoBERTa baseline receives up to 256 tokens and trains for three epochs. EC-XGB's display calibrator is fitted on the RAGTruth QA split; the in-domain Platt calibrator is fitted on validation. Metrics are reported to four decimals, NLL to two, and rates to one. Significance uses McNemar on seed-42 predictions, 2,000 two-level bootstrap resamples, and Wilcoxon across seeds. No question appears in both training and evaluation. Calibration is compared on raw, Platt, and isotonic scores; Table~\ref{tab:protocols} defines P0, P1, and P2, and every protocol uses the same test rows.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Two families of metrics are reported. Threshold-dependent metrics measure the decision at a fixed cut-off. Threshold-free metrics measure how well a model ranks answers. Both are needed because a model can rank well and still make poor decisions when the threshold is fixed. The confusion matrix supplies the four counts used below, TP, FP, FN, and TN. Precision is the share of flagged answers that are truly hallucinated, and a high value means a reviewer can trust an alert. Recall is the share of hallucinated answers that the detector catches, and a high value means few bad answers pass. F1 is the harmonic mean of the two, and it punishes a model that is strong on one metric and weak on the other. The false-positive rate is the share of correct answers that are flagged. It is the operating constraint under distribution shift, where most natural answers are correct. The flag rate is the share of answers above the decision threshold, and it measures the review workload. A flag rate near 100\% means the detector sends every answer to a human.
\begin{gather}
  P = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
  R = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
  F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}, \\
  \mathrm{FPR} = \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{TN}}, \qquad
  \mathrm{Flag} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}_i \ge \tau].
\end{gather}

The second group measures ranking, calibration and paired error patterns. AUROC is the probability that a randomly chosen hallucinated answer receives a higher score than a randomly chosen correct answer. It measures ranking and does not depend on a threshold, and a value of 0.5 is chance. PR-AUC is the area under the precision-recall curve, and it is more sensitive than AUROC when the positive class is rare. RAGTruth QA has a positive rate of 17.8\%, so PR-AUC is reported beside AUROC. MCC uses all four cells of the confusion matrix and ranges from $-1$ to $1$, with zero at chance. Accuracy is misleading on an imbalanced test set, so MCC is the safer single-number summary. Negative log-likelihood rewards confident correct probabilities and penalizes confident wrong ones, and lower is better. The Wilson interval gives a 95\% range for a proportion and is used for the P2 recall and false-positive rate, where the counts are small.
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{AUROC} = \int_{0}^{1} \mathrm{TPR}(u)\, d\,\mathrm{FPR}(u) = P(s^{+} > s^{-}), \\
  \mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} P(r)\, dr, \qquad
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}\cdot\mathrm{TN} - \mathrm{FP}\cdot\mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}, \\
  \mathrm{NLL} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)\right], \\
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\end{gather}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the HaluEval test results for eleven baselines and EC-XGB, averaged over the three seeds where a seed applies. The F1 ranking is tight. Logistic regression is 0.019 behind the conventional model, the random forest is 0.0004 behind, and EC-XGB is 0.0009 ahead. The 0.0009 lead is much smaller than the width of the seed-42 bootstrap interval for the conventional F1, [0.9812, 0.9895], so the ordering is not resolved by the data. The precision and recall columns show why. The random forest gains recall but loses precision, and EC-XGB improves both slightly. These differences are small because the benchmark is saturated. The conventional row in Table~\ref{tab:models} is the B2 checkpoint. The m0 row in the component ablation uses the fixed configuration described in Section~\ref{sec:results}, so the two differ by 0.0003 in F1 and 0.0005 in ECE.

The ranking columns tell the same story. AUROC and PR-AUC place EC-XGB first among the feature-based models, at 0.9984 and 0.9987, with the conventional model and the random forest within 0.0006. RoBERTa is higher at 0.9990 and 0.9993. Logistic regression is lower, at 0.9932 AUROC. MCC is 0.9697 for the conventional model and 0.9715 for EC-XGB. The calibration columns are separate from discrimination. The conventional model has the lowest ECE at 0.0045, but its Brier score is higher than EC-XGB and RoBERTa. The two summaries therefore disagree on which model is better. ECE depends on the bin edges, while the Brier score uses every row. The neural baseline is the strongest model in-domain, at 0.9912 F1 and 0.9990 AUROC. The weak controls are the NLI-only and full-input TF-IDF models, at 0.6714 and 0.5999 F1; the answer-only TF-IDF model reaches 0.9224, and the majority baseline scores zero. The NLI probabilities carry signal but not enough, and adding the question and context to a bag-of-words model hurts. The reduced external ensemble reaches F1 0.9517 and AUROC 0.9850. The fixed-hp m3 model is higher by 0.0337 F1 [0.0262, 0.0419] and 0.0135 AUROC [0.0098, 0.0177] on paired bootstrap draws, and its ECE is 0.0078 against 0.0141. The gap is consistent with the nonlinear learner and the wider feature set, but the baseline is a reduced reimplementation on fewer training rows, so the comparison is descriptive.

The XGBoost margin over the random forest is small, at 0.0004 in F1, and the paired McNemar test on the same seed-42 rows gives $p=0.502$. The difference is not significant. The margin over logistic regression is larger, at 0.0187 in F1, and the same test gives $p=1.86\times10^{-10}$. The margin over the heuristic is 0.0495 in F1, with $p=2.28\times10^{-30}$. The random forest is therefore the only classical baseline that matches XGBoost on this benchmark. A neural baseline trained solely on the source material outperforms EC-XGB in-domain (F1 0.9912 vs 0.9855), but it is near chance on each RAGTruth task (AUROC 0.458 for summarization and 0.491 for data-to-text), against 0.582 for EC-XGB. The pooled seed-mean AUROC is 0.337; pooling mixes between-task score shifts with within-task ranking. Training an adapted encoder on exactly the same multi-source rows raises it to 0.694 on those tasks, above EC-XGB's 0.582, so the gain on seen tasks is not specific to the feature model. This adaptation does not enable transfer learning, since neither the adapted encoder nor EC-XGB generalizes to the unseen QA task (adapted encoder = 0.490 vs EC-XGB = 0.549), and FaithBench is worse for both (adapted encoder = 0.434 vs original EC-XGB = 0.594). Adaptation improves any model's performance on seen tasks, whereas the feature model retains its relative ordering on unseen tasks.

\subsection{Calibration, Shift, and External Validity}

The raw, Platt-scaled, and isotonic versions of the conventional model give ECE 0.0045, 0.0091, and 0.0070 on HaluEval, so the calibrated ECEs are not lower in this sample. Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} shows why. On HaluEval, the points at the two ends sit on the diagonal; the middle of the score range holds few distinct values, so the curve has only a few stable bins. The Platt and isotonic curves overlap the raw curve at both ends. The m3 raw score is also well calibrated in-domain, with ECE 0.0078, so there is little room for calibration to improve on HaluEval.

Platt scaling fits a two-parameter logistic map from the raw score $s$ to a probability,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p} = \sigma(a s + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
  \label{eq:platt}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the calibration slope and $b$ is the intercept. A slope below one means the raw score is overconfident, and a slope near one means the scale is already usable. The Brier skill score compares the calibrated score with a constant predictor at the evaluation prevalence,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{prior}}}.
  \label{eq:bss}
\end{equation}
A positive BSS means the score beats the prior, and a non-positive BSS means it does not.

For the conventional model, the RAGTruth QA histogram in Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} shows almost every row above 0.99, at a mean confidence of 0.998 against an accuracy of 0.178. Source Platt leaves the confidence near 0.99. The m3 score behaves the same way. The fixed-hp m3 raw score is severely overconfident: ECE 0.731 and Brier 0.689, with a Cox slope of 0.18 on the 5{,}034 calibration rows. Source Platt cannot repair a base-rate shift. Target Platt lowers ECE to 0.125 and Brier to 0.161, but the Brier skill score against the test prior is $-$0.10. A constant predictor at the test prevalence has Brier 0.146, lower than every calibrated row, and flags no rows at 0.5. Calibration fixes the scale, not the ranking; AUROC stays at 0.558. The conventional target-Platt row behaves the same way at ECE 0.134 and Brier 0.164. The label budget is the practical constraint, and it is a distribution rather than a point.

Table~\ref{tab:conformal} reports the coverage. Split conformal prediction adds a coverage guarantee to the score. The nonconformity score for label $y$ is $s(x,y)=1-\hat{p}_y(x)$, where $\hat{p}_1=\hat{p}$ and $\hat{p}_0=1-\hat{p}$. The calibration quantile and the prediction set are
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q} = \mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\left(\{s(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}\right),
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}\}.
  \label{eq:conformal}
\end{equation}
A class-conditional version replaces the single quantile with one quantile per label,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q}_y = \mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\left(\{s(x_i,y_i) : y_i = y\}\right),
  \qquad
  C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat{q}_y\}.
  \label{eq:conformal_class}
\end{equation}
The main run fits the Platt map and the quantile on the same 5{,}034 rows, so its calibration coverage is a construction check. A disjoint sensitivity fits Platt on 2{,}514 rows and the quantile on 2{,}520 rows. On that split, the 900 QA test rows reach 0.944 coverage at 90\%, so the marginal guarantee holds. It hides the class split: positive coverage is 0.685, while negative coverage is 1.000. A class-conditional quantile raises positive coverage to 0.887, still slightly below nominal. FaithBench coverage falls to 0.320 and positive coverage to 0.003, because a Platt map fitted on QA sends almost every FaithBench score to the negative side. Conformal prediction therefore certifies the marginal rate only when the calibrator and the quantile are fitted on disjoint rows, and it does not transfer across the task shift. The per-class rate is the one a reviewer needs when positives are 17.8\% of the target.

Table~\ref{tab:external} reports the three external corpora and the task-shift pair. MedHallu is the strongest evidence that the ranking survives a domain change and the clearest evidence that the probability does not. The fixed-hp m3 model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded answer in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 medical pairs [84.5, 88.7], with AUROC 0.821 [0.806, 0.837] on the 2{,}000 rows. The pair accuracy over $M$ pairs is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PA} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\left[\hat{p}(a_m^{+}) > \hat{p}(a_m^{-})\right],
  \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
where $a_m^{+}$ is the hallucinated answer and $a_m^{-}$ the grounded answer. The conventional m0 model reaches 82.2\% and 0.781. The length-only baseline is inverted, at 20.0\% and 0.260, because grounded MedHallu answers are longer than the paired hallucinations (39.7 vs 27.8 words), the opposite of HaluEval (2.2 vs 11.0). The ranking therefore transfers, but the scale does not: m3 flags every row, so its F1 is the all-positive 0.667 and its ECE is 0.493. The source indicator is one for MedHallu and zero for HaluBench; a source-zero sensitivity on MedHallu changes pair accuracy by at most 0.003 and AUROC by at most 0.002, so the indicator does not drive the result. HaluBench is a 30\% stratified sample of the 3{,}000 non-overlapping rows. Its overall AUROC is 0.600, with covidQA 0.833, DROP 0.513, and FinanceBench 0.527; the model transfers to the biomedical subset but is at chance on the other two. The length-only baseline is at 0.514 overall, so 0.600 is not a length artifact.

The task-shift test is the weakest. On dialogue, m3 flags 99.3\% of rows and its F1 equals the all-positive baseline, with AUROC 0.763; the length-only baseline reaches 0.724. On summarization, m3 reaches AUROC 0.717 and flags 37.5\%, while the length-only baseline reaches 0.788 and flags every row. The conventional m0 model flags 99.2\% of dialogue rows and 97.7\% of summarization rows, with AUROC 0.741 and 0.605. Both models fail to transfer; the dialogue failure is the all-positive baseline, and the summarization failure is below the length-only ranking. This is a task-shift test, not a second independent QA corpus.

An index stress test asks whether the weak held-out QA result is a retrieval problem. The hybrid BM25 and FAISS index covers the 989 RAGTruth QA source documents. On a 300-row, 50-group sample, recall@1 is 0.94, recall@5 and recall@10 are 1.00, and MRR is 0.959. Replacing the gold context with the top-5 retrieved passages changes AUROC from 0.545 to 0.521, F1 from 0.345 to 0.349, and ECE from 0.706 to 0.704. The AUROC delta is $-$0.023 [$-0.061$, 0.011], which includes zero. Retrieval is therefore not the bottleneck: the model is near chance on RAGTruth QA even with the gold source passage. The qrels are the known source passage, not human web-search judgments, so this measures index recall, not end-to-end search quality. The sample has 50 source groups, so the interval is wide.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Statistical tests use the paired fixed-hyperparameter predictions and 2{,}000 two-level bootstrap draws, where groups are resampled within each seed and then seeds are resampled. McNemar's test compares the discordant counts $b$ and $c$ on the paired seed-42 rows, where $b$ is the number of rows where the first model is right and the second is wrong, and $c$ is the reverse. The test statistic is
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2 = \frac{(|b-c|-1)^2}{b+c},
\end{equation}
with a continuity correction. Holm's method orders the $m$ p-values and adjusts the $i$-th value as
\begin{equation}
  p_{(i)}^{\text{Holm}} = \min_{j \ge i} \left\{ \min\left(1, (m-j+1)p_{(j)}\right) \right\},
\end{equation}
which controls the family-wise error rate across the four baseline comparisons. The paired bootstrap delta is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_b = \theta_{\text{EC}}(\mathcal{D}_b) - \theta_{\text{conv}}(\mathcal{D}_b),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_b$ is a resample of groups and $\theta$ is the metric. The 95\% interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the $\Delta_b$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = \left[\Delta_{(\alpha/2)}, \Delta_{(1-\alpha/2)}\right].
\end{equation}
The two-level version resamples groups within each seed and then resamples seeds,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta^{*} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left[\theta_{\text{EC}}^{*}(\mathcal{D}_{s}^{*}) - \theta_{\text{conv}}^{*}(\mathcal{D}_{s}^{*})\right],
  \label{eq:twolevel}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_{s}^{*}$ is a group resample for seed $s$ and $S=3$. The Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic is
\begin{equation}
  W = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \operatorname{rank}(|d_s|) \mathbf{1}[d_s > 0],
\end{equation}
where $d_s$ is the difference between the two models on seed $s$ and $S=3$.

Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} gives the McNemar counts. EC-XGB and the conventional model are not significantly different under the deployed checkpoints ($p=0.45$), and the fixed-hyperparameter ablation gives $p=0.789$ on the same test rows. That is the primary comparison, and it is not part of the Holm family, which covers the four baseline comparisons against the conventional model. At $p=0.45$ it would not survive correction. The random forest matches the conventional model ($p=0.502$). Logistic regression and the heuristic are significantly worse ($p=1.86\times10^{-10}$ and $p=2.28\times10^{-30}$), and both survive Holm correction. The Wilcoxon test across three seeds cannot reach significance (minimum $p=0.25$). Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports the two-level bootstrap. On HaluEval, the m3$-$m0 F1 delta is $+$0.0006 [$-0.0010$, 0.0021], which includes zero, and the AUROC delta is $+$0.0007 [0.0002, 0.0012], which excludes zero by a small margin. The m2$-$m1 F1 delta is $-$0.0004 [$-0.0020$, 0.0012], and the m3$-$m1 delta is $+$0.0001 [$-0.0015$, 0.0018], so the claim features do not add an aggregate gain. Their AUROC deltas are $+$0.0009 and $+$0.0010, both with intervals above zero, so the added features reorder a few rows without changing F1. On held-out RAGTruth QA the m3$-$m0 F1 delta is $+$0.0076 [0.0045, 0.0109], but the AUROC delta is $+$0.0017 [$-0.0270$, 0.0291], which includes zero. The F1 gain comes from flagging almost every row, not from better ranking. On the pooled seen tasks the F1 delta is $+$0.0451 [0.0348, 0.0554] and the AUROC delta is $+$0.0962 [0.0644, 0.1295], which is the adaptation gain. The paired fixed-minus-tuned contrasts all include zero, so the rebuild does not change these conclusions.

\subsection{Explanation, Efficiency, and Judges}

The explanation results use the fixed-hyperparameter m3 seed-42 checkpoint. Figure~\ref{fig:shapimp} ranks the top-15 features by mean absolute SHAP over 1{,}000 stratified HaluEval test rows. Context overlap dominates, followed by word count, question-answer cosine, character count, and question-answer Jaccard; the claim-contradiction mean enters at rank eight. The top ten features carry 77.9\% of the total attribution mass, and exact TreeSHAP reproduces the model margin to $9.2\times10^{-6}$. The m3 ranking agrees with the frozen B2 ranking (Spearman $\rho=0.878$, Kendall $\tau=0.730$), so the claim features refine the explanation rather than replace the original drivers. Attribution stability across seeds is high: against seed 42, Spearman is 0.972 for seed 123 and 0.950 for seed 456, top-10 overlap is nine of ten, and the top-5 set is identical. The explanation is stable to the seed, although the perturbation pilot below shows that it is less stable to content edits. The attributions are on the raw margin, not the display score. The audit cases are error-enriched, and their roles change under m3; they are discussed below.

Table~\ref{tab:perturb} shows the m3 pilot. Content edits move the score (entity replacement, support removal, number replacement, date replacement), while structural edits barely move it (irrelevant insert 0.003, clause shuffle 0.0001). The top-1 feature flips in 54.5\% of entity perturbations and in none of the other operators, and Spearman correlation stays between 0.73 and 0.999. The entity flip rate is higher than in the B2 run, so the m3 explanation is less stable when the answer entity changes. The pilot has between two and twenty changed cases per operator, so these values are descriptive, not effect estimates.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Discussion}

On the seed-42 fixed-hyperparameter run, m3 makes 43 errors on the HaluEval test set: 8 false positives and 35 false negatives, an error rate of 1.4\%. The WP5 audit covers all eight false positives and 12 of the 35 false negatives, chosen to cover the codebook rather than sampled at random. The 20-case draft assigns 13 content errors and seven audit artifacts. The artifact group is dominated by grounded answers that the detector over-flags (four cases) and one-word answers with zero lexical overlap (two cases); one case is benchmark label noise. The content group is dominated by entity errors (conflation, distortion, misattribution, and fabricated names), followed by generic or descriptive answers, coreference, numeric or temporal errors, relation inversion, and wrong granularity. These counts describe the reviewed cases, not the 3,000-row test population, and the labels are an author-verification draft rather than independent human annotation. A separate 50-case sheet covers the eight threshold false positives, the 17 highest-scoring negatives, and 25 false negatives. The three authors verified 31 artifact cases (22 grounded over-flags, 7 label-noise cases, 2 short-answer over-flags) and 19 content cases across 12 categories. Table~\ref{tab:length} shows why length matters for the conventional model: the hallucination rate rises from 2.1\% for one-word answers to 90.2\% at five to eight words and 99.2\% at 17+ words. False positives concentrate in the five to eight bin, and false negatives in the short bins. Answer length is therefore a near-perfect label indicator on HaluEval.

Four controls test the length explanation. A logistic regression on the two length variables reaches F1 0.9457 and AUROC 0.9759. A reweighting control equalizes six length bins with 247 examples each; on that set EC-XGB reaches F1 0.9867 and AUROC 0.9987, against 0.9475 and 0.9758 for a length-only baseline, a gain of +0.039 F1 and +0.023 AUROC. A length-matching control pairs one positive and one negative at each length, giving 79 pairs (158 examples) over 1{,}000 bootstraps. EC-XGB holds AUROC 0.956 while the length-only baseline falls to 0.521, a gap of 0.436 [0.351, 0.522]; the F1 gap is 0.440 [0.334, 0.552]. The length cue explains the zero-shot failure but not the in-domain ranking.

Figure~\ref{fig:length} shows the same pattern: the rate jumps from 12.7\% at three to four words to 90.2\% at five to eight words, and false negatives are most frequent by rate in the short bins; by count, 18 of 36 are in the five to eight bin. The shape explains the zero-shot result: complete sentences are long, so the conventional model reads them as hallucinations. The informative results are outside the benchmark. EC-XGB ranks the RAGTruth task types seen in training better than the conventional model, and the display calibrator gives readable target scores. The gain comes from multi-source training, not a different learning system. HaluEval labels length: 2.1\% of one-word answers are hallucinations, and 99.2\% at 17+ words. The ablation leaves lexical and length as the only groups that matter, so the in-domain model is dominantly a length and overlap detector. That is why natural long answers are flagged at 99.9\%, calibration cannot repair the ranking, and held-out QA stays near 0.55. The contribution is the protocol that exposes this chain, not a general detector. A fine-tuned encoder still wins in-domain, so the choice is a cost-transparency-accuracy trade-off. The external comparison is limited by protocol. No published HaluEval QA result uses the grouped split of this study. A supervised 13B agent reports F1 0.724 on 100 HaluEval QA rows \cite{haluagent}, and the benchmark's zero-shot judges report 62.6\% to 69.8\% accuracy \cite{haluEval}. Those settings differ in supervision, evidence access and test rows, so they are context rather than a head-to-head bar. The benchmark's generation instruction asks for hallucinated answers about five words longer than correct ones; the observed gap is 8.5 words. In this test split the mean answer length is 2.28 words for faithful answers and 10.79 for hallucinated answers, and a length-only logistic regression reaches AUROC 0.976 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Any comparison that ignores this construction artifact overstates semantic detection. Five limits bound these claims. Web-search retrieval quality and end-to-end verdict accuracy are not evaluated; the retrieval stress test uses the known source passage. The judge comparison uses 900 RAGTruth QA rows at natural prevalence and the 158-row length-matched set, which controls length but not domain. The audit is error-enriched. The HaluEval benchmark is saturated, so its in-domain differences are not resolvable. The deployed display calibrator was selected in an earlier fit that compared Platt and isotonic on the test split; the calibration analysis in Table~\ref{tab:target} refits Platt without any test-based selection.




\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A useful hallucination detector must be fast, inexpensive, and explainable. This research has created EC-XGB, an evidence-consistent XGBoost risk predictor with 26 base features, eight claim-level NLI aggregates, a source indicator, multi-source training, calibration, and SHAP explanations. On HaluEval QA the fixed-hp model reaches F1 0.9854 and AUROC 0.9986, and the tuned checkpoint is 0.9855 and 0.9984. Lexical overlap and length carry the in-domain score: removing them costs 0.0171 and 0.0066 F1, while the claim block costs only 0.0010. Cross-seed SHAP rankings are stable, so the explanation is reproducible. On RAGTruth, the pooled flag rate falls from 99.9\% to 61.3\%, but held-out QA remains at 96.8\% and AUROC 0.558. Target calibration lowers ECE from 0.731 to 0.125, yet the calibrated score flags no target rows at 0.5 and a constant predictor has a lower Brier score. Split conformal covers 94.4\% at a 90\% target, but positive coverage is only 0.685; a class-conditional quantile raises it to 0.887, and FaithBench coverage falls to 0.320. On MedHallu the model ranks the paired hallucinated answer above the grounded one in 86.7\% of 1{,}000 pairs (AUROC 0.821) but flags every row and is badly calibrated (ECE 0.493), so the ranking survives the domain change while the probability does not. Dialogue and summarization both fail to transfer, and summarization ranks below a length-only model. One analysis takes 127.5\,ms on GPU and 2.00\,s on CPU and costs about \$0.001 per 1{,}000 predictions. GPT reaches F1 0.565 on natural QA, but EC-XGB reaches 0.907 on the length-matched set against 0.865. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific risk assessment and adaptation check, not a general-purpose detector. Future work should cover domain adaptation, stronger multicalibration, and threshold transfer.
