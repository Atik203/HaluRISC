31   1. Introduction
32       A number of researchers have examined how to apply machine learning techniques to assess the likelihood of
33   a response being a hallucination. One such technique is SelfCheckGPT, which uses several generated answers and
34   assesses their similarity to determine if there has been a hallucination [1]. Another technique is Luna, where a
35   smaller evaluation model is used instead of a larger judge model [2]. A third technique is semantic entropy, which
36   identifies meaning-based groupings of similar responses [3]. A hybrid framework combines frozen encoder features
37   with lightweight classifiers and reports strong benchmark scores [4]. Judges carry high computational costs and
38   encoder models are hard to interpret. Two recent reviews have grouped approaches to detecting hallucinations into
39   three categories including feature-based detectors, encoder-based detectors, and combinations of the above [5, 6].
40   Both reviews identify evaluation and reliability as two major areas that require additional research, and a third review
41   determines that reliability is an area of concern as well [7]. A fourth survey frames the problem as factuality evaluation
42   and reaches the same conclusion [8].
43       Six recent studies address the same topic of assessing hallucinations. HaluAgent demonstrated that small language
44   models can select appropriate tools and detect multiple forms of hallucinations, but it required multiple tool calls for
45   each answer [9]. Valentin et al. proposed a calibrated multi-score detector with a detailed cost analysis, but the detector
46   relies heavily on large language model (LLM) scoring for its primary source of information [10]. Sundaragiri et al.
47   proposed a system that uses retrieval, natural language inference (NLI) verification, and an XGBoost classifier, and
48   they report only classification metrics [11]. Haq et al. assigned SHAP-LIME attributions to each word, but did not
49   test whether those values remained consistent across words [12]. Deng et al. found that detectors degrade once they

50   leave the domain in which they were trained, and their method focuses on multi-turn dialogue signals [13]. FaithBench
51   shows that modern LLMs still produce hard summarization hallucinations, and it does not assess calibration risks [14].
52       The existing literature leaves four issues unaddressed. Calibration is infrequently reported for lightweight detectors,
53   explanation quality is rarely measured, cross-domain behavior is rarely tested, and error patterns are rarely analyzed
54   case by case. The conventional feature-based baseline is also saturated on HaluEval, depends on answer length, and
55   loses calibration under shift. This paper addresses all four issues by developing a black-box model that takes a query 𝑞,
56   a reference context 𝑐, and a candidate answer 𝑎, and outputs 𝑃 (hallucinated ∣ 𝑞, 𝑐, 𝑎). The model, evidence-consistent
57   XGBoost (EC-XGB), consists of 26 evidence-consistency features in seven groups, eight claim-level NLI aggregates,
58   and a source indicator, classified by a tuned XGBoost model. Platt and isotonic calibration yield the probability,
59   SHAP explanations reveal feature contributions, artifact-control baselines and group-aware splits reduce shortcut
60   solutions, and validation-only calibration quantifies the reliability problem. The claim-level aggregates support per-
61   claim explanations, and the measured transfer gain comes from multi-source training rather than from those aggregates.
62   Transfer limits are established on RAGTruth and FaithBench.
63       HaluEval QA provides 10,000 paired correct and hallucinated answers for training and in-domain testing [15].
64   RAGTruth provides natural responses for adaptation and recalibration [16]. The scope is question answering with a
65   reference context, and FaithBench serves only as an out-of-domain summarization stress test. EC-XGB reaches an F1
66   of 0.9855 on HaluEval QA. On natural RAGTruth answers, the flag rate falls from 99.9% to 61.3%, and the AUROC
67   rises from 0.475 to 0.582. Target calibration on 5,034 labeled rows lowers the RAGTruth QA ECE from 0.726 to 0.126,
68   but it does not change the ranking. On HaluEval, recall stays above 99% at a five percent false-positive rate, and one
69   analysis takes 61.8 ms at about $0.001 per 1,000 predictions.


70   2. Conventional Method
71   2.1. Preliminaries
72        Fluent hallucinations are claims where the amount of support from the evidence is not enough. The detection can
73   be assessed in terms of precision, recall, F1, or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in an
74   evaluation setting on labeled data. Typical datasets for testing detectors are HaluEval [15] and TruthfulQA [17], and
75   factual accuracy of long-form text generation can be verified by FActScore [18]. In a deployment context, a detector
76   faces tougher evaluation, because it has to contribute to a decision process and to be explainable. Calibration addresses
77   the first problem – the predicted risk has to represent the true risk, and calibration is measured with the use of expected
78   calibration error (ECE) via reliability diagrams [19]. The second one – having the detector’s warning, the person
79   needs an explanation of it. For tabular models such information comes from SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
80   attributions [20]. Both kinds of evaluation are conducted in the current work on the black box approach to the model.

81   2.2. Baseline Method
82       The conventional method is a tuned XGBoost classifier [21] over the 26 hand-crafted features. XGBoost handles
83   nonlinear interactions, trains quickly on tabular data, and supports exact SHAP values. Boosted trees remain
84   competitive with tuned neural networks on tabular data [22]. The model adds trees one at a time, and each tree fits the
85   error left by the previous ensemble. The final score passes through a logistic link, so the output is a probability.

104       This baseline has three limits. The benchmark is saturated, so extra features do not improve F1. The score is sensitive
105   to answer length, and the probability scale breaks under shift [23]. The baseline also lacks claim-level evidence, trains

108   3. Proposed Method
109   3.1. Working Principle
110        Figure 1 reads from left to right. It takes a question, reference context, and a candidate answer from a black-box
111   LLM, and produces a risk probability and per-feature explanations. The first stage collects the three inputs: the question,
112   the reference context, and the candidate answer. A missing context is allowed and is treated as a separate condition. The
113   second stage extracts 35 features. The seven base groups cover length and style, lexical overlap, entity novelty, NLI,
114   numeric overlap, hedging, and semantic similarity. Eight claim-level NLI aggregates are added to the 26 base features,
115   and a binary source indicator marks whether the answer came from a natural response corpus. The third stage passes
116   the 35 features to the EC-XGB classifier. The classifier returns a raw score, which is a probability over the hallucinated
117   class. The fourth stage applies the Platt calibrator fitted on the RAGTruth QA calibration split. The calibrated score is
118   the number shown to the user. The fifth stage produces the outputs: the risk score, the per-claim verdicts, and the SHAP
119   feature contributions. The lower band shows the deployment path. A Next.js front end calls a FastAPI service, and the
120   service loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. The rightmost block lists the evaluation
121   corpora. HaluEval measures in-domain performance, RAGTruth summarization and data-to-text measure adaptation
122   to task types seen in training, and RAGTruth QA and FaithBench are held out. The training band records the monotone
123   constraints, the grouped five-fold cross-validation, and the three seeds used for every reported mean.

124        The task consists of estimating the probability 𝑃 (hallucinated ∣ 𝑞, 𝑐, 𝑎) of a hallucination, conditioned on a question
125   𝑞, reference context 𝑐 (if available) and a candidate answer 𝑎 coming from a black-box LLM. It is not aimed at verifying
126   truthfulness of the candidate answer, but at finding those cases in which the candidate answer does not match the
127   provided evidence, i.e. it does not follow from or contradicts it. The output is a probability, not a binary label. A
128   decision threshold turns the probability into a flag.

129   3.2. Features
130       The model uses 26 base features in seven groups (Table 1) and eight aggregate features computed at the claim level
131   (Table 2). For NLI, six signal probabilities come from a DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder [24] trained on SNLI [25] and
132   MultiNLI [26]. They indicate whether one statement follows logically from another. Semantic similarity uses MiniLM
133   sentence embeddings [27, 28], and entities come from spaCy named entity recognition [29]. Length and style features
134   count characters, words, sentences, and average word length. They are included because the benchmark labels longer
135   answers as hallucinated more often, so length is a strong but fragile signal. Lexical overlap measures how much of the
136   answer appears in the context and in the question. It captures direct copying and is the strongest feature in the SHAP
137   ranking, but it cannot detect a fluent paraphrase that contradicts the evidence. Entity features count named entities and
138   the share that do not appear in the context. A novel entity is a useful warning sign, although a correct answer can
139   also introduce a new name. The NLI features give the probability that the context entails, contradicts, or is neutral
140   to the answer, in both directions. They are the only group that represents logical support rather than surface overlap.
141   Numeric features compare the numbers in the answer with the numbers in the context. A changed number is a common
142   hallucination and is easy to detect. Hedging features count uncertainty words such as “may” or “likely”. They are weak
143   on their own, but they help separate a cautious correct answer from a confident wrong one. Semantic features use cosine
144   similarity between the context and the answer and between the question and the answer. They catch paraphrase and
145   topic drift that lexical overlap misses. The eight claim-level aggregates in Table 2 repeat the NLI and overlap signals
146   at the level of atomic claims, which is the level a reviewer actually checks. Entailment-based claim metrics are also
147   used by RAGChecker [30].

171   on its own, and together they hold the in-domain score, cut the false-alarm rate on the task types seen in training, and
172   produce feature-level attributions whose reliability is measured. The deployed system adds claim-level verification with
173   evidence retrieval and a display calibrator fitted on the target domain. Relative to the closest feature-based detector [11],

199   3.5. System and Reproducibility
200       These methods are used in a two-tiered web application. The system is a Next.js frontend and a FastAPI backend
201   that loads the model, calibrator, explainer, and feature models at startup. Three checkpoints appear in Section 5. B2 is
202   the conventional 26-feature model trained on HaluEval only. It supplies the conventional row of the model-comparison
203   table, the feature-group ablation, the conventional rows of the target-calibration table, and the SHAP analysis and audit.
204   B6 m0 is a separate 26-feature tuning run under the EC-XGB protocol, reported as m0 in the component ablation and
205   as the conventional rows of the cross-domain table. EC-XGB m3 is the 35-feature multi-source model that supplies the
206   remaining EC-XGB rows and the deployed system. Claims are split from the answer and checked with sentence-level
207   NLI against the provided context, an indexed collection, or live web search. A support-first rule treats any supporting
208   sentence as evidence, and a contradiction counts only when no support exists. A relevance gate removes retrieved
209   sentences with no claim vocabulary, and the verdicts are shown per claim.

235   4.2. Dataset
236       The QA subset of HaluEval [15] serves as the training data. This dataset has 10,000 questions. Each question has a

239   and 3,000 test rows. Since both versions of the answer to each question come from the same knowledge passage, the
240   model can easily learn the relationship between the context and the answers, so the split is at the group level. About
241   85% of HaluEval was generated by ChatGPT through sampling and filtering [15, 36]. The boundary between a factual
242   error and a hallucination is therefore not always clean. Transfer was evaluated using two datasets. First, RAGTruth

257   4.3. Baselines and Experimental Procedure
258        Ten baselines and EC-XGB are tested. The heuristic sets risk to one minus lexical overlap. Logistic regression and
259   a 300-tree random forest are the classical comparators; the conventional XGBoost model [21] uses the 26 base features.
260   Three controls isolate the feature design: NLI-only, answer-only TF-IDF, and full-input TF-IDF. A majority classifier
261   and a fine-tuned RoBERTa-base [37] complete the set; the two judges are in Section 5.
262        Internal-state detectors are outside the black-box setting [38]. The RoBERTa baseline receives up to 256 tokens and
263   trains for three epochs. EC-XGB’s display calibrator is fitted on the RAGTruth QA split; the in-domain Platt calibrator
264   is fitted on validation. Metrics are reported to four decimals, NLL to two, and rates to one. Significance uses McNemar
265   on seed-42 predictions, 1,000 group bootstrap resamples, and Wilcoxon across seeds. No question appears in both
266   training and evaluation. Calibration is compared on raw, Platt, and isotonic scores; Table 4 defines P0, P1, and P2, and
267   every protocol uses the same test rows.

276   4.4. Evaluation Metrics
277       Two families of metrics are reported. Threshold-dependent metrics measure the decision at a fixed cut-off.
278   Threshold-free metrics measure how well a model ranks answers. Both are needed because a model can rank well
279   and still make poor decisions when the threshold is fixed. The confusion matrix supplies the four counts used below,
280   TP, FP, FN, and TN. Precision is the share of flagged answers that are truly hallucinated, and a high value means a
281   reviewer can trust an alert. Recall is the share of hallucinated answers that the detector catches, and a high value means
282   few bad answers pass. F1 is the harmonic mean of the two, and it punishes a model that is strong on one metric and weak

      Rahaman et al.: Preprint                                                                                       Page 9 of 24
                                                        Evidence-Consistent XGBoost

283   on the other. The false-positive rate is the share of correct answers that are flagged. It is the operating constraint under
284   distribution shift, where most natural answers are correct. The flag rate is the share of answers above the decision
285   threshold, and it measures the review workload. A flag rate near 100% means the detector sends every answer to a
286   human.

512   6. Conclusion
513       A useful hallucination detector must be fast, inexpensive, and explainable. This research has created EC-XGB,
514   an evidence-consistent XGBoost risk predictor. EC-XGB uses 26 evidence-consistency features, 8 claim-level NLI
515   aggregates, and a source indicator, with log-scaled lengths under monotone constraints, multi-source training,
516   calibrated probabilities, and SHAP explanations. On HaluEval QA it reaches an F1 of 0.9855 and an AUROC of
517   0.9984, which matches conventional XGBoost on a saturated benchmark. On the RAGTruth tasks used in training, the
518   pooled flag rate falls from 99.9% to 61.3%, while held-out QA remains at 97.4% and AUROC 0.549. Target calibration
519   lowers ECE from 0.726 to 0.126, but a prevalence-only predictor at the calibration prevalence matches its Brier score,
520   and recall at a 5% false-positive budget is 5.0%. Under matched multi-source training, the adapted encoder reaches
521   0.694 on the seen RAGTruth tasks, above EC-XGB’s 0.582, while the source-only encoder wins in-domain (0.9912
522   F1) but is near chance on each seen task (0.455 and 0.477). On unseen QA both are near chance (AUROC 0.490 vs
523   0.549), and EC-XGB’s F1 stays near 0.30, so EC-XGB is less degraded than the adapted encoder but not a usable
524   QA detector. One analysis takes 61.8 ms and costs about $0.001 per 1,000 predictions. The GPT 5.6 Luna judge costs
525   about 106 times more per 1,000 predictions and runs about 23 times slower. EC-XGB is therefore a domain-specific
526   risk assessment tool and adaptation check, not a general-purpose detector. Future work will cover multicalibration
527   under stronger shifts, conformal prediction, threshold transfer, and controlled tests for shortcuts.
